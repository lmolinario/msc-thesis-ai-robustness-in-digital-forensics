\section{Summary of the Research}
\label{sec:conclusions-summary}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This thesis investigated the operational robustness of \gls{ai}-based systems
used for automated image classification and triage in forensic contexts. The
study was motivated by the observation that nominal performance on clean and
well-structured data does not fully characterize the behavior of systems that
may be applied to heterogeneous, degraded, anomalous, or deliberately
manipulated digital evidence. In a \gls{df} workflow, errors affect not only
classification performance but also the prioritization of material, the
allocation of analyst attention, and the reliability of the subsequent human
review process.

The research therefore adopted an operational perspective. Adversarial attacks,
anti-forensic transformations, and \gls{ood} inputs were used as distinct
experimental conditions for examining how automated systems behave beyond the
clean baseline. The objective was not to construct a conventional
\gls{advml} benchmark or to identify a universally superior system. Rather, the
study assessed whether high nominal performance remains stable when input
conditions change and whether the resulting errors create operational risks for
forensic triage.

The experimental design combined two complementary evaluation levels. The
first level comprised three transparent proxy models: EfficientNet-B0,
ResNet18, and \gls{clip}. In this thesis, proxy models denote fully inspectable
experimental reference models used to analyze predictions, confidence-related
indicators, robustness degradation, and error direction under controlled
conditions. Selected attribution patterns were additionally examined for
EfficientNet-B0 through the qualitative \gls{xai} analysis.

The second level comprised
\gls{magnetaxiomtool}/\gls{magnetai},
\gls{griffeye}/\gls{t3kcore},
\gls{excirefoto2025}, and
\gls{cellebriteinseyets}. These systems were evaluated as black-box or
near-black-box software systems using only observable outputs and documented
normalization rules. The proxy models were not intended to reproduce their
proprietary internal models; they served as fully inspectable experimental
references.

A central methodological feature was the use of the same frozen experimental
corpus at both evaluation levels. The corpus comprised a 1\,000-image binary
subset, balanced between \texttt{weapon} and \texttt{non\_weapon}, together
with a separate 500-image \gls{ood} branch. Adversarial and anti-forensic
variants were generated exclusively from the binary subset. The resulting
forensic evaluation bundle contained 11\,500 files: 1\,000 clean binary images,
500 clean \gls{ood} images, 5\,000 adversarial variants, and 5\,000
anti-forensic variants. The selected black-box configurations produced one
normalized decision for every bundle item, while the proxy-model pipeline
preserved the fold-aware evaluation design described in
Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

The complete procedure was formalized through the \gls{fairlab} protocol. The
protocol does not constitute a new classifier or a new proprietary software
platform. It is a task-specific, reproducible, and auditable evaluation
framework that connects dataset construction, human validation, deterministic
partitioning, proxy-model evaluation, perturbation generation, blind-bundle
construction, black-box output normalization, quantitative metrics,
explainability analysis, and traceability controls. Its central purpose is to
make operational robustness experimentally observable and reconstructible.

The principal conclusion is that robustness cannot be inferred from clean
accuracy alone. It must be evaluated as a contextual property of a specific
system version, configuration, task definition, input population, and forensic
workflow. Automated outputs may provide useful support for triage, but they
must remain traceable, reviewable, and subordinate to expert assessment
\cite{costantini2019digital,sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

\section{Main Contributions}
\label{sec:conclusions-contributions}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The contribution of this thesis is articulated across four closely connected
dimensions.

First, the thesis formalizes \gls{fairlab} as a methodological protocol for
evaluating the operational robustness of \gls{ai}-based image-analysis systems
in \gls{df}. The protocol extends evaluation beyond clean classification
accuracy and explicitly incorporates a separate \gls{ood} branch, adversarial
attacks, anti-forensic transformations, black-box software assessment,
forensically relevant error analysis, explainability, and traceability. The
methodological contribution lies in the integration of these components into a
single coherent evaluation process rather than in the introduction of a new
classification architecture.

Second, the work establishes a common frozen experimental basis for parallel
proxy-model and black-box evaluation. The final dataset contains 500
\texttt{weapon} images, 500 \texttt{non\_weapon} images, and 500 images
reserved for the separate \gls{ood} branch. The same underlying samples and
derived artifacts were used across the two evaluation levels.

The model-dependent adversarial artifacts generated against the fold-aware
EfficientNet-B0 checkpoints were subsequently evaluated on the other proxy
architectures and submitted, through the blind bundle, to all selected
black-box configurations. The proxy-model pipeline therefore served both as an
inspectable evaluation layer and as the controlled generation layer for the
model-dependent adversarial artifacts used in the parallel black-box
assessment.

The proxy models processed manifest-linked experimental artifacts, whereas the
selected software systems received byte-identical image copies under anonymized
bundle identifiers. This design makes the comparison dependent on system
behavior and output mapping, rather than on independently assembled datasets.

Third, the thesis provides a traceable experimental artifact. Stable
identifiers, manifests, fold assignments, model checkpoints, generated-image
records, bundle identifiers, cryptographic digests, normalized predictions,
metric tables, and reporting assets preserve the relationship between source
images and final results. The distinction among traceability, integrity, and
controlled reproducibility is maintained throughout the implementation. This is
particularly important when complete source datasets or proprietary software
exports cannot be publicly redistributed.

Fourth, the study provides an error-oriented operational analysis of
heterogeneous automated image-analysis systems. The comparison does not assume
architectural equivalence among the evaluated components. Instead, it examines
their observable behavior through false negatives, false-positive workload,
\gls{ood} weapon assignments, configuration sensitivity, transfer behavior,
and the continued need for human review. This perspective makes it possible to
compare different operational risk profiles without constructing an absolute
ranking among systems.

A complementary qualitative \gls{xai} analysis was conducted using
\gls{ig} on five representative EfficientNet-B0 cases. These visualizations
support the diagnosis of selected correct and incorrect decisions. They are not
treated as independent evidence of robustness and are not extended to the
selected black-box software systems. These visualizations
support the diagnosis of selected correct and incorrect decisions, but they are
not treated as independent evidence of robustness and are not extended to the
selected black-box software systems.

\section{Main Experimental Findings}
\label{sec:conclusions-findings}

The results confirm that nominal performance and operational robustness are
related but non-equivalent properties. High clean accuracy provides an important
baseline, but it does not predict how a system will behave under distributional
mismatch, image-level transformations, adversarial manipulation, or a different
operational configuration.

The clean results already reveal different risk profiles. EfficientNet-B0
minimizes the total number of clean errors among the proxy models, whereas
\gls{clip} achieves the lowest clean \gls{fnr} at the cost of a substantially
higher \gls{fpr}. This confirms that systems with similar or high accuracy may
allocate errors differently between missed detections and additional review
workload.

The separate \gls{ood} analysis identifies a distinct operational risk. The
\gls{ood} samples are neither a third supervised class nor ordinary negative
instances. They include anomalous, borderline, degraded, synthetic, and
semantically adjacent content. The observed weapon-assignment rates show that both proxy models and selected
black-box configurations may map such inputs to one of the two closed-set
operational classes. These results do not establish whether each \gls{ood} image is
investigatively relevant; they describe
how systems behave when confronted with content outside the nominal binary
population.

The anti-forensic results indicate that plausible image-level transformations
can affect automated analysis without producing the extreme collapse observed
under the strongest direct adversarial conditions. Their operational importance
therefore depends less on aggregate degradation alone and more on the direction
of the induced errors. A limited accuracy drop may still be relevant when it
corresponds to a \texttt{weapon}\(\rightarrow\)\texttt{non\_weapon}
transition.

The adversarial analysis demonstrates the clearest separation between nominal
accuracy and source-model robustness. The attacks generated against
fold-aware EfficientNet-B0 checkpoints used different access assumptions:
\gls{fgsm}, \gls{sigmazero}, and \gls{superdeepfool} used white-box access,
whereas \gls{onepixel} used score-based access. Their evaluation on ResNet18,
\gls{clip}, and the selected software systems measures transfer behavior rather
than direct robustness against attacks optimized independently for those
systems. The model-agnostic \gls{colorshift} condition follows a different
experimental rationale.

The black-box results reinforce the importance of configuration and observable
output semantics. The behavior of \gls{excirefoto2025} changes substantially
between \texttt{D20}, \texttt{D50}, and \texttt{D80}. A more permissive
setting reduces missed detections but increases false-positive workload and
\gls{ood} weapon assignments. Similarly, the low \gls{fpr} observed for
\gls{griffeye}/\gls{t3kcore} must be interpreted together with the
conservative bookmark mapping used to define a positive result. These outcomes
support an analysis based on trade-offs rather than an absolute ranking.

Overall, the findings show that validation on clean data is not equivalent to
validation under operational conditions. Robustness assessment must jointly
consider baseline performance, \gls{ood} behavior, adversarial and
anti-forensic degradation, configuration sensitivity, output mapping,
traceability, and the continued role of human review.

\section{Operational and Forensic Implications}
\label{sec:conclusions-operational-implications}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The experimental evidence supports the use of automated image-analysis systems
as triage aids rather than as autonomous systems for evidentiary
decision-making. Their principal operational value lies in helping analysts prioritize and organize
large collections of multimedia content. This value depends, however, on the
ability to reconstruct how a result was produced and to verify it against the
source artifact and the broader investigative context.

False negatives and false positives create different operational consequences.
A false negative on the \texttt{weapon} class may prevent potentially relevant
material from receiving appropriate review priority. A false positive may
increase the number of images requiring manual examination and dilute the
analyst's attention. Neither error type can be interpreted solely through its
contribution to aggregate accuracy. The relevant balance depends on the purpose
of the workflow and on the relative cost assigned to missed detections and
additional review workload.

The same principle applies to \gls{ood} inputs. An operational
\texttt{weapon} assignment on an \gls{ood} image does not constitute a standard
binary false positive, because the sample is outside the supervised task. It may
nevertheless trigger a flag, affect prioritization, or increase review workload.
Where confidence-related scores are available, they must be interpreted within
the corresponding model and must not be treated as stand-alone evidence or as
directly comparable calibrated probabilities across architectures.

Configuration is part of the forensic and operational context. The
\gls{excirefoto2025} sensitivity analysis shows that changing the
\texttt{Distance limit} changes the balance among recall, false positives, and
\gls{ood} absorption. The same principle applies to the bookmark mapping used
for \gls{griffeye}/\gls{t3kcore} and to the enabled functions and export logic
of the other selected systems. A reported result is therefore inseparable from
the software version, configuration, operational mapping, and normalization
procedure under which it was obtained.

Traceability is consequently a substantive requirement rather than an
administrative addition. The \gls{fairlab} implementation preserves the links
among source images, generated variants, bundle identifiers, software outputs,
normalized decisions, metrics, and reporting assets. This structure makes it
possible to distinguish system behavior from data-processing errors, mapping
choices, and residual limitations. In a forensic context, an automated output
that cannot be reconstructed or independently reviewed has limited analytical
value.

These considerations support a \textit{human-in-the-loop} model of use. The
analyst remains responsible for contextualizing automatic results, examining
unflagged and ambiguously flagged content where appropriate, documenting the
system configuration, and integrating automated triage with the remaining
forensic evidence. This approach is consistent with principles of human
oversight, reliability, and accountability in trustworthy \gls{ai}
\cite{EUAI2020,nist2023airmf}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:conclusions-limitations}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The findings apply within the scope of the frozen protocol and the defined case
study. They must not be interpreted as a universal validation of the evaluated
models or software systems. The principal limitations are summarized below.

\begin{itemize}
    \item The dataset contains 1\,500 manually selected images and does not
    represent the complete diversity of firearm-related, non-firearm, and
    out-of-distribution content encountered in operational investigations.
    Selection was performed by a single reviewer, and no inter-rater agreement
    was measured.

    \item The source distribution is not uniform. In particular, the
    \texttt{non\_weapon} branch is predominantly derived from
    \texttt{05\_deepweb}. Source-specific regularities may therefore influence
    some results despite the source-aware fold construction.

    \item The adversarial and anti-forensic suites are finite and use one frozen
    parameterization per condition. Different budgets, severities, encodings,
    implementations, or combined transformations may produce different
    robustness profiles.

    \item The four model-dependent attacks were generated against fold-aware
    EfficientNet-B0 checkpoints. The results for ResNet18, \gls{clip}, and the
    selected software systems measure transfer behavior of those artifacts and
    do not constitute direct attacks optimized independently against every
    evaluated system.

    \item The proxy models are transparent experimental references, not
    reconstructions of proprietary internal architectures. Their behavior
    cannot be assumed to explain the internal operation of the selected
    black-box software systems.

    \item Black-box results depend on the evaluated versions, enabled
    capabilities, export formats, and normalization rules. The
    \gls{griffeye}/\gls{t3kcore} bookmark mapping and the
    \gls{excirefoto2025} distance setting directly affect the observed balance
    between missed detections and false-positive workload.

    \item Proxy-model \gls{ood} rates aggregate five predictions per image,
    whereas black-box rates use one normalized decision per image. The reported
    proportions describe comparable operational tendencies but are not based on
    identical numbers of prediction events.

    \item The retained \gls{xai} analysis is limited to five representative
    EfficientNet-B0 cases. The historical figures were generated using 32
    integration steps and are interpreted qualitatively; no strict convergence
    claim is made, and the findings cannot be extended to the proprietary
    systems.

    \item The blind bundle contains a small residual metadata information
    channel. The leave-out sensitivity analysis indicates negligible aggregate
    impact, but the frozen protocol does not include a matched
    metadata-neutralized bundle through which visual and metadata effects could
    be isolated directly.

    \item The study evaluates operational triage behavior and does not determine
    the legal admissibility or evidentiary weight of automated outputs. Such an
    assessment would require consideration of the applicable legal framework,
    chain-of-custody requirements, independent validation, and case-specific
    evidentiary context.
\end{itemize}

These limitations delimit the claims supported by the experiment. The thesis
provides a protocol-specific assessment of operational robustness, not a
software-system certification, a universal ranking, or an evidentiary
validation of automated decisions.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusions-future-work}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Future research directions derive directly from the observed results and the
limitations of the frozen protocol.

A first extension should apply the protocol to additional software versions,
configurations, and operational categories. Black-box and near-black-box
systems may change across releases, and the same software family may expose
different models, thresholds, output fields, or semantic categories over time.
Repeating the protocol across versions would help distinguish stable behavioral
patterns from results that depend on a specific configuration or export format.
Alternative documented normalization rules could also be evaluated where a
software output supports more than one defensible operational mapping.

The experimental corpus should also be expanded. Future studies could include
larger multi-source collections, additional firearm-related subclasses,
different forensic visual domains, and images obtained from device exports,
messaging platforms, social networks, and acquisition pipelines. A
multi-annotator review process and formal agreement measures would make the
semantic validation stage more transparent and would quantify the uncertainty
associated with borderline samples.

Further work is required on \gls{ood} management. The present results show that
closed-set systems may assign anomalous or semantically adjacent inputs to an
operational class. Future evaluations should therefore examine calibrated
uncertainty estimates, abstention or rejection mechanisms, explicit \gls{ood}
detectors, and unknown-output categories where supported by the evaluated
software. These mechanisms should be assessed not only through technical
metrics but also through their effect on analyst workload and missed-detection
risk.

The adversarial evaluation could be extended by generating matched attacks
independently against each transparent proxy architecture under comparable
budgets. A multi-source design would make it possible to separate direct
source-model vulnerability from cross-model transferability more systematically.
Additional conditions could include adversarial patches, physical or hybrid
manipulations, composite transformations, broader parameter sweeps, and
defensive strategies such as adversarial training or robust preprocessing.
These extensions should remain tied to operationally meaningful forensic
scenarios rather than becoming an isolated algorithmic benchmark.

The anti-forensic branch could similarly be expanded through multiple severity
levels, transformation chains, platform-specific recompression, repeated
encoding, cropping, screenshot acquisition, partial occlusion, and combined
visual and metadata manipulations. The objective would be to determine whether
small individual effects accumulate when transformations occur sequentially in
realistic evidence-handling or content-sharing processes.

A specific extension should address residual embedded metadata. Matched
metadata-preserving and metadata-neutralized versions of the same blind bundle
would make it possible to isolate the contribution of visual content from
metadata-dependent behavior. This comparison would strengthen blind-input validation.
It would also provide a direct sensitivity control for systems that index embedded metadata,
filenames, or other non-pixel information.

The explainability component could be broadened through additional methods,
larger case sets, stability analysis, and explicit attribution-convergence
checks. Such work should evaluate whether explanations remain consistent across
clean and perturbed variants and whether they provide reproducible diagnostic
value to analysts. Explainability should nevertheless remain supplementary to
quantitative robustness testing and should not be treated as evidence of
classification correctness.

Finally, the protocol would benefit from independent replication and broader
artifact availability. Where licensing and data-protection constraints prevent
full redistribution, repositories can still preserve scripts, manifests,
configuration records, cryptographic digests, sanitized normalized outputs,
aggregate metrics, and validation reports. Shared evaluation protocols built on
these principles could support more systematic comparison across laboratories,
software versions, and forensic tasks while maintaining the distinction between
experimental reproducibility and unrestricted access to sensitive or
proprietary material.

\section{Closing Remarks}
\label{sec:conclusions-closing}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────


This thesis has shown that the operational robustness of \gls{ai}-based image
classification in \gls{df} cannot be reduced to clean accuracy. Systems that
perform well under nominal conditions may nevertheless exhibit missed
detections, additional review workload, \gls{ood} absorption, configuration
sensitivity, or severe degradation under specific manipulations.


The central contribution is the parallel evaluation of transparent proxy models
and selected black-box software systems on the same frozen experimental corpus.
This design combines experimental control with operational relevance while
preserving the distinction between inspectable model behavior and normalized
black-box outputs. The \gls{fairlab} protocol and the corresponding forensic
evaluation bundle make the complete process traceable from the source image to
the reported metric.

Within the defined scope, no evaluated system can be considered unconditionally
robust.

This conclusion does not preclude the use of \gls{ai}-assisted software
systems in forensic workflows. It defines the conditions required for their
responsible use: task-specific validation, documented versions and
configurations, explicit management of anomalous inputs, traceable artifacts,
transparent mapping rules, and expert review of the resulting outputs.

Operational robustness must therefore be demonstrated under the conditions in
which a system is expected to operate. It cannot be presumed from nominal
performance, proprietary status, or the apparent confidence of an automated
output. In forensic image triage, automation remains most defensible when it is
used as a controlled and auditable support mechanism within a
\textit{human-in-the-loop} workflow.

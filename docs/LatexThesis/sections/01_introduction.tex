\chapter{Introduction}\label{chap:introduction}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The integration of \gls{ai}-based systems into \gls{df}
workflows addresses the need to examine growing volumes of digital data and to
promptly identify content that may be relevant to an investigation. When such
support concerns the automated classification of images, however, the
reliability of the analysis may be compromised by degraded or intentionally
manipulated inputs capable of reducing the likelihood that investigatively relevant content
will be correctly brought to the analyst's attention.
Against this background, the present thesis evaluates the operational
robustness of proxy models and AI-based software systems employed for the automated
classification or semantic retrieval of images depicting firearms and other
firearm-oriented visual content, comparing their behavior on unmanipulated
data, out-of-distribution content, and images subjected to adversarial
perturbations or anti-forensic transformations. The objective is to contribute
to the evaluation of automation as a controlled support for forensic analysis,
while maintaining human oversight, procedural traceability, and the cautious
interpretation of automated outputs as central principles
\cite{costantini2019digital,sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section[Digitalization, Threats, and the Central Role of Digital Forensics]{Digitalization, Threats, and the Central Role of \glsentrylong{df}}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The progressive digitalization of contemporary society has profoundly
transformed how information is produced, stored, transmitted, and analyzed. Cloud infrastructures, distributed services, interconnected mobile
devices, social platforms, and heterogeneous information systems now generate an
increasing volume of digital data, which plays an increasingly relevant role in
everyday life as well as in professional, institutional, and judicial
activities. This evolution has also significantly expanded the attack surface
of information systems, increasing opportunities for exploitation by hostile
actors and multiplying the possible points at which digital evidence may be
compromised \cite{garfinkel2010digital,casey2011digital}.

Alongside the opportunities offered by technological innovation, increasingly
sophisticated threats have emerged, characterized by high technical
heterogeneity and a growing capacity for concealment. These include data
exfiltration and manipulation, the alteration of multimedia files, the
selective deletion of traces, the use of anonymization techniques, and, more
generally, all those practices aimed at hindering the reliable reconstruction
of events. In such a scenario, \gls{df} assumes a central role as the
discipline responsible for the identification, acquisition, preservation,
analysis, and presentation of digital evidence according to criteria of
methodological rigor, integrity, and verifiability
\cite{palmer2001road,reith2002examining,nist2006guide,iso27037}.

According to the definition commonly referenced in the literature, \gls{df}
consists of the application of scientific methods and technical procedures
intended to make digital data usable as evidence in an investigative or
judicial context \cite{palmer2001road}. This approach requires that every phase
of the process be conducted in a traceable, repeatable, and defensible manner,
so that the outcome of the analysis is not only technically correct but also
sustainable from an evidentiary standpoint. In operational terms, the forensic
process is commonly described as a sequence of phases including
identification, acquisition, preservation, analysis, and presentation of
evidence, all sharing the need to ensure integrity, transparency, and
verifiability \cite{reith2002examining,nist2006guide}. This need becomes
particularly relevant in cases in which evidence is distributed across multiple
devices, stored in virtualized environments, present in volatile form, or
exposed to intentional manipulation \cite{nist2006guide,iso27037}.

The growing incidence of cybercrime and, more broadly, the now pervasive role
of digital technologies in the commission and documentation of crimes have
consolidated the function of \gls{df} asa crucial link between technical expertise and fact-finding.
Within this framework, the quality of
analytical procedures and the reliability of the tools employed directly affect
the ability to reconstruct events correctly, identify those responsible, and
preserve the evidentiary value of digital evidence in judicial proceedings
\cite{costantini2019digital,clough2015principles,europol2019iocta}.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section[Automation of Forensic Analysis and the Role of AI]{Automation of Forensic Analysis and the Role of \glsentryshort{ai}}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The quantitative and qualitative expansion of the data subject to investigation
has progressively made an exclusively manual approach to forensic analysis less
sustainable. Traditional methodologies, while maintaining a fundamental role in
terms of technical control and evidence validation, are often difficult to
scale when investigators must examine large volumes of images, videos, logs,
conversations, system files, and heterogeneous artifacts originating from
different sources. Under these conditions, automation becomes an operational
necessity even before it becomes a matter of efficiency
\cite{garfinkel2010digital,costantini2019digital}.

In this context, the integration of \gls{ai} techniques into \gls{df} tools has
introduced new possibilities for supporting investigative analysis. In
particular, \textbf{\gls{ml}} and \textbf{\gls{dl}} methods make it possible to
automate tasks such as the semantic classification of images and videos, the
recognition of recurring patterns, the grouping of similar evidence, log
analysis, and the identification of content potentially relevant to an
investigation. The triage function becomes even more significant in cases in
which analysts must repeatedly examine sensitive content: studies conducted on
professionals engaged in the analysis of child sexual abuse material highlight
the possible psychological impact of prolonged exposure to such content and the
operational interest in filtering and safer presentation technologies aimed at
reducing the operator's unnecessary exposure \cite{sanchez2019practitioner}.
Although this does not coincide with the \texttt{weapon}/\texttt{non\_weapon}
classification task evaluated in the present thesis, such evidence shows that
the reliability of automation may affect not only workflow efficiency, but also
the operational conditions under which analysts review digital content
\cite{costantini2019digital,sanna2024preliminary}.

The application of \gls{ai} to \gls{df}, however, is not limited to merely
accelerating existing processes. It introduces a qualitative change in the way
investigative information is filtered, ordered, and prioritized. \gls{ai}-based
systems integrated into software used for forensic analysis can suggest
semantic categories, support the automatic clustering of multimedia content,
highlight anomalies, and propose correlations that direct the operator's
attention toward specific elements of the evidence. This trend can also be
observed in the main professional tools used in the field, in which
\gls{ai}-based modules are progressively being integrated to support
classification, sorting, and preliminary evidence selection
\cite{sanna2025cybercrime,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

However, the advantage offered by automation cannot be evaluated exclusively in
terms of efficiency. In forensic contexts, every reduction in human workload
achieved through automatic models must be balanced against requirements of
reliability, transparency, and control. A system that automatically classifies
content as relevant or irrelevant does not merely speed up the analyst's work,
but also contributes to determining which evidence will receive priority
attention and which, by contrast, may risk being overlooked. It follows that
the introduction of \gls{ai} into forensic workflows requires critical
reflection not only on model performance, but also on the soundness of their
decisions in complex operational scenarios and on the possibility of making
their limitations more understandable through \textbf{\gls{xai}} approaches
\cite{Adadi2018,Doshi-Velez2017,sanna2025cybercrime}.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section[Robustness of AI-based Systems and Adversarial and Anti-Forensic Threats]{Robustness of \glsentryshort{ai}-based Systems and Adversarial and Anti-Forensic Threats}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The use of \gls{ai}-based systems in high-criticality domains has highlighted a
fundamental issue: high performance under standard conditions does not
necessarily imply reliability in the presence of altered, degraded, or hostile
inputs. In the case of \gls{df}, this aspect is particularly significant,
because automated classification systems may be exposed to intentionally
manipulated content, transformations introduced along the image acquisition and processing chain, or
inputs that differ substantially from the distribution of the data used during
development or training.

The problem of intentional alteration of digital evidence, however, did not
originate with the introduction of \gls{ai}. Anti-forensic techniques more
broadly include strategies aimed at hindering the identification, acquisition,
examination, or interpretation of potentially relevant data, for example
through selective deletion of traces, metadata alteration, encryption, file
concealment, or information hiding. Among these techniques, steganography makes
it possible to conceal information within apparently ordinary content, such as
digital images, thereby making it more difficult to detect both for the human
analyst and through automatic tools not specifically designed to identify it
\cite{yaacoub2021digital,sanna2025cybercrime}. In the present work,
steganography is referenced as a representative example of the anti-forensic
domain, whereas the experimental evaluation focuses on image manipulations
directly relevant to the robustness of automated visual classification.

At the same time, the literature on \gls{advml} has shown that \gls{ml} and
\gls{dl} models may be vulnerable to perturbations specifically designed to
induce classification errors, even when such alterations are limited or only
slightly perceptible to a human observer. These manipulated inputs, commonly
referred to as \emph{adversarial examples}, call into question the assumption
that a classifier achieving high performance on clean data is necessarily
reliable in real-world scenarios. The definition of the \emph{threat model},
the distinction among different levels of attacker knowledge, and the need for
security-oriented evaluations therefore constitute central elements in the
analysis of automatic systems exposed to hostile inputs
\cite{goodfellow2015explaining,carlini2017towards,biggio2018wild,nowroozi2021survey}.

In the forensic domain, adversarial attacks and anti-forensic techniques must
be kept conceptually distinct. The former primarily aim to exploit the
fragilities of a model's decision function; the latter may operate on digital
evidence even in the absence of any \gls{ai} system, with the more general goal
of concealing, degrading, or making an artifact less informative. The two
perspectives may nevertheless intersect when an intentional manipulation
reduces the ability of an AI-based system to flag investigatively relevant
content. In such a scenario, an attack against automated classification also
takes on an anti-forensic purpose, because it may affect the triage,
prioritization, and subsequent human review of evidence
\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

The notion of robustness adopted in the present thesis must therefore be
understood as the ability of the system to maintain sufficiently reliable
behavior not only in the presence of unmanipulated data, but also with respect
to intentional perturbations, realistic image transformations, and content that
does not fully conform to the expected conditions of analysis. From this
perspective, a non-robust system may reduce the probability that relevant
images are correctly brought to the analyst's attention, or may increase the
analytical noise generated by erroneous classifications. Robustness therefore
does not constitute a marginal problem of model optimization, but rather an
essential component of the operational reliability of automation within a
forensic workflow.

\section{Research Problem and Relevance in Realistic Forensic Scenarios}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Despite the extensive literature on adversarial attacks and the robustness of
\gls{ml} models, the evaluation of these critical issues in realistic forensic
scenarios remains limited. Many studies focus on standardized benchmarks,
datasets constructed for general-purpose \textit{computer vision}, or
experimental conditions far removed from actual investigative workflows. By
contrast, studies more closely aligned with forensic practice show that
automated image analysis must contend with large collections extracted from
digital evidence, heterogeneous operational classes, and content relevant to
specific investigative needs. In this regard, Silva et al.\ propose an
AI-based system for classifying images contained in criminal evidence,
including categories such as firearms, ammunition, documents, screenshots, and
nudity; more recently, Sanna et al.\ have shown that automatic functionalities
integrated into software used for forensic analysis may show vulnerabilities
when confronted with manipulated content or inputs that do not conform to
ordinary conditions of use
\cite{silva2021microservices,sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

In the domain of \gls{df}, the problem is not merely to establish whether a
classifier can be induced to fail, but to understand to what extent such
fragility affects the system's ability to support reliable decisions during
evidence analysis. This requires evaluation sets aligned with the operational
scenario, composed of heterogeneous content, images subject to plausible
transformations, borderline cases, and situations in which the distinction
between relevant, irrelevant, or ambiguous content is not always clear-cut.
This need is consistent with DFRWS research on the evaluation of automatic
systems for visual content: although referring to \textit{perceptual hashing}
rather than the semantic classification of firearms, the PHASER framework
highlights the importance of studying tools on datasets constructed according
to the specific deployment scenario and its related anomalous cases
\cite{mckeown2024phaser}. In the present thesis, this principle is applied to
a task centered on the identification of realistic individual firearms and
firearm-oriented visual content, selected because it is representative of a
visual category of potential investigative interest, already considered in the
literature, and compatible with controlled experimental validation
\cite{silva2021microservices,Hao2018DeepFirearm}. The evaluation distinguishes
the operational classes \texttt{weapon} and \texttt{non\_weapon} from an
\texttt{ood} class, used to represent borderline, anomalous, or semantically
external content with respect to the main task domain; the precise composition
of the three classes is described later within the experimental contribution of
the thesis. Adversarial perturbations and anti-forensic transformations are
instead applied to the binary subset consistent with the main task.

The evaluation design also distinguishes between two complementary levels of
observation. For proxy models, the experimental setup makes it possible to
apply adversarial perturbations under controlled conditions, consistently with
the level of model access required by the selected techniques; for commercial
software, the evaluation is instead conducted in a \textit{black-box} setting,
observing the outputs available to the analyst without assuming knowledge of
the internal logic of the AI-based components. Anti-forensic transformations
are considered separately as \textit{model-agnostic} manipulations, applicable
regardless of access to the classifier.

Within this framework, system vulnerability is not only theoretically
relevant, but also produces immediate operational consequences. Classification
errors induced by manipulated inputs may alter the priority assigned to
evidence, generate analytical noise, or reduce the system's ability to flag
potentially relevant content. Robustness, therefore, concerns not only the
stability of the model with respect to controlled perturbations, but also the
overall quality of the support it is able to provide within the forensic
workflow \cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

The research problem addressed in this thesis is located precisely at this
intersection between algorithmic robustness and operational reliability. The
goal is not merely to observe a generic performance degradation, but to
systematically analyze how different automated classification models and
different AI-based software systems behave when deployed in a realistic
forensic scenario and subjected to adversarial perturbations and anti-forensic
transformations. This perspective makes it possible to move beyond a purely
abstract evaluation of model security, directing the analysis toward the
concrete consequences that such vulnerabilities may have on triage, evidence
selection, and, more broadly, the entire investigative workflow.

The relevance of this research is therefore twofold. On the one hand, it is
scientific, since it contributes to the definition of an experimental framework
more closely aligned with the needs of the forensic domain and to the
understanding of the limitations of \gls{ai}-based models in the presence of
hostile inputs. On the other hand, it is operational, since it provides useful
elements for critically evaluating the use of automatic systems in
investigative contexts, promoting an approach that does not privilege nominal
accuracy alone, but also robustness, traceability, and verifiability of model
behavior. In this sense, the thesis is situated within a line of research that
seeks to contribute to the definition of more domain-specific benchmarks and
more broadly shared comparative protocols for the evaluation of \gls{ai} in
\gls{df}
\cite{silva2021microservices,mckeown2024phaser,sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

\section{Research Objectives}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
In light of the critical issues highlighted in the previous sections, this
thesis aims to systematically evaluate the operational robustness of proxy
models and AI-based software systems employed, within the context of \gls{df}, for the
automated classification or semantic retrieval of images of potential
investigative interest. The general objective is to determine to what extent
these systems maintain reliable behavior, with respect to the reference
conditions represented by unmanipulated inputs, when exposed to degraded,
altered, or intentionally perturbed images. In an investigative scenario, such
alterations may be intentionally applied by subjects seeking to reduce the
likelihood that relevant content will be identified through automated detection
or triage procedures. In addition, recent studies show that general-purpose
generative \gls{ai} tools may facilitate the production of code intended to
conceal information in digital images through steganographic techniques;
although this does not coincide with the transformations experimentally
evaluated in the present thesis, such evidence broadens the range of possible
anti-forensic behaviors facilitated by automated tools
\cite{sanna2025cybercrime}. Particular attention is devoted both to
adversarial perturbations and anti-forensic transformations, and to system
behavior in the presence of \gls{ood} content, that is, images that are
external, ambiguous, or not representative of the operational domain of the
main \texttt{weapon}/\texttt{non\_weapon} task, and that may therefore produce
operationally relevant errors
\cite{hendrycks2017baseline,hendrycks2019benchmarking}.

From this perspective, the work is structured around four main objectives. The
first is the definition of a rigorous, documented, and reproducible
experimental protocol for analyzing the robustness of proxy models and AI-based
software employed for the automated classification or semantic retrieval of
images in a realistic forensic context. This protocol includes the
construction of a final manually validated dataset, the definition of a binary
subset intended for the generation and evaluation of adversarial perturbations
and anti-forensic transformations, the controlled generation of manipulated
inputs, and the preparation of an evaluation bundle that can also be used with
selected commercial software for operational evaluation in a forensic context.

The second objective is to compare heterogeneous models, differing in
architecture and representational paradigm, in order to observe possible
differences in behavior with respect to clean images, \gls{ood} content,
anti-forensic transformations, and adversarial perturbations. In particular,
the proxy models make it possible to carry out a controlled quantitative
analysis of performance degradation, whereas the AI-based software systems evaluated in
a black-box setting makes it possible to examine the operational relevance of
the problem with respect to the outputs actually available to the analyst.

The third objective concerns measuring the impact of manipulations on the
ability of systems to correctly recognize images belonging to forensic classes
of interest. The analysis is not limited to variation in overall accuracy, but
also considers the error patterns introduced, behavior with respect to
out-of-distribution content, the confidence associated with erroneous
decisions, and, from a diagnostic perspective, the qualitative interpretation
of predictions through \gls{xai} techniques.

The fourth objective is to relate the experimental evidence to an assessment of
the operational reliability of automation within \gls{df} workflows. From this
perspective, \gls{ai}-based systems are not regarded as substitutes for the
analyst's judgment, but rather as supports for the triage and examination of
digital content, whose reliability must be verified under realistic and
potentially hostile conditions. The analysis therefore aims to highlight
limitations, operational risks, and methodological requirements necessary for
the use of such systems to remain compatible with a \textit{human-in-the-loop}
approach and with the verifiability requirements inherent in forensic activity
\cite{costantini2019digital,faqir2023digital,sanna2024preliminary}.

The adopted approach therefore does not aim merely to measure the accuracy of
classifiers in a controlled environment, but to understand how the interaction
among models, software systems, data, and perturbations may concretely affect
the support provided to the analyst. In this sense, the objective of the
research is not exclusively performance-oriented, but also methodological and
applied: to define an evaluation protocol that makes it possible to assess the
robustness of \gls{ai}-based systems in relation to their possible use in
realistic investigative scenarios, while keeping human supervision central to
the interpretation and validation of the results.

\section{Contribution of the Thesis}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The main contribution of this thesis lies in the definition and application of
an experimental methodology for evaluating the operational robustness of proxy
models and AI-based software systems employed, within a \gls{df} context, for the
automated classification or semantic retrieval of images. Unlike studies
primarily focused on standard \textit{computer vision} benchmarks or on
robustness evaluations conducted in generic domains, the present work adopts a
perspective oriented toward the needs of forensic analysis: it does not
consider only nominal classification capability, but also examines the
resilience of the system in the presence of hostile, degraded, ambiguous, or
out-of-distribution inputs
\cite{nowroozi2020phd,hendrycks2019benchmarking,sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

The experimental protocol adopted in this thesis is named \textbf{\gls{fairlab}}.
It organizes, in a documented and reproducible manner, the components required
for the evaluation: the final frozen dataset, proxy models, adversarial
perturbations, anti-forensic transformations, commercial software selected for
operational evaluation, quantitative metrics, \gls{xai} analysis, and
traceability controls. \gls{fairlab} is not proposed as a new standalone
software tool or as a general theoretical architecture, but as a methodological
protocol used to make the experimental evaluation carried out in this thesis
transparent, verifiable, and reproducible.

A first contribution consists in the integration, within the same experimental
design, of two complementary levels of evaluation. The first concerns the
controlled evaluation of proxy models, through which datasets, perturbations,
metrics, and \gls{xai} analyses can be examined under reproducible and
comparable conditions. The second concerns the black-box operational evaluation
of the selected commercial software, conducted through a common set of images
organized by experimental condition and used to observe the behavior of the
outputs available to the analyst. This integration makes it possible to connect
the quantitative analysis of models with the verification of the behavior of
software that can actually be used in digital content analysis activities,
without assuming access to the internal logic of the relevant AI-based
components \cite{costantini2019digital,sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

A second contribution concerns the construction of a final frozen dataset, as
well as the definition of a selection protocol consistent with the
investigative problem addressed. The final dataset is organized into three
semantically distinct classes, denoted as \texttt{weapon}, \texttt{non\_weapon},
and \texttt{ood}, for a total of 1500 balanced images. The \texttt{weapon}
class includes only realistic individual firearms, or clearly recognizable
firearm-oriented visual content; the \texttt{non\_weapon} class includes
realistic negatives consistent with the task; the \texttt{ood} class collects
borderline or out-of-distribution cases with respect to the main task, such as
knives, swords, replicas, toy or airsoft weapons, synthetic renders, images
from video games, vehicles or non-individual armaments, war scenes,
explosions, degraded images, or content anomalous with respect to the domain of
interest. Starting from the final dataset, a binary subset of 1000 images is
also defined, composed only of the \texttt{weapon} and \texttt{non\_weapon}
classes, and intended for the generation and evaluation of adversarial
perturbations and anti-forensic transformations. Sample selection follows a
\textit{human-in-the-loop} paradigm, in which the manual component is not
concealed, but documented as an integral part of the methodological protocol,
thus making explicit the decision criteria, assumptions, and possible
limitations involved in the construction of the experimental corpus
\cite{sambasivan2021data}.

A third contribution concerns the definition of an evaluation plan based on
heterogeneous data, classification criteria consistent with the investigative
problem addressed, and a set of manipulations selected to represent two
distinct yet operationally related perspectives: adversarial perturbations
designed to alter classifier responses, and anti-forensic transformations that
are plausible from the standpoint of the life cycle and concealment of digital
images. In this way, the thesis does not merely verify whether a system can be
induced to fail, but also analyzes the nature of the error and its possible
implications for triage and the preliminary selection of relevant content
\cite{barni2018cf,nowroozi2024verifying,yaacoub2021digital}.

A fourth contribution consists in the comparison among models differing in
architecture and representational paradigm, together with the black-box
evaluation of the commercial software included in the protocol: Magnet
AXIOM/Magnet.AI, Excire Foto 2025, Cellebrite Inseyets/Physical Analyzer, and
Magnet Griffeye/T3K CORE. This approach makes it possible to avoid conclusions
that depend exclusively on a single classifier and to observe whether the
fragilities identified under controlled conditions also assume relevance with
respect to the outputs available in software used in the context of forensic
analysis. Within this framework, the \gls{xai} analysis applied to the proxy
models serves a complementary diagnostic function: it does not certify the
correctness of classification, but supports the qualitative interpretation of
representative cases of error or instability.

Overall, the thesis proposes an interpretation of robustness not merely as a
technical property of the model, but as a functional requirement for the
controlled use of automation in \gls{df}. The empirical analysis is therefore
intended to assess the extent to which out-of-distribution data, adversarial
perturbations, and anti-forensic transformations affect the stability of
automatic outputs and the quality of the support provided to the analyst, while
keeping human supervision central to the interpretation and verification of the
results.

\section{Technical-Forensic Relevance and Profiles of Evidentiary Reliability}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
In forensic contexts, the technical correctness of a tool cannot be assessed
independently of the context in which it is used and of the consequences that
its results may have on evidence analysis. The introduction of \gls{ai}-based
systems into investigative workflows therefore raises a question that is both
technical and forensic in nature: to what extent can an automated output be
considered sufficiently reliable to guide the analyst's work without
compromising the completeness of the examination or introducing systematic
errors that are difficult to detect
\cite{faqir2023digital,sanna2025cybercrime}.

The issue becomes particularly significant when automatic systems operate in a
substantially opaque manner, that is, when the analyst has access to the
classification result but not to a full understanding of the decision-making
process that produced it. In such cases, model fragility with respect to
manipulated or unexpected inputs does not merely represent an algorithmic
limitation, but may also affect the quality of the entire investigative
process. A false negative may lead to the failure to select relevant content; a
false positive may generate noise, divert resources, and increase the burden of
manual verification. Within this framework, \gls{xai} approaches can provide
diagnostic support to the analyst by making it possible to examine, with the
necessary caution, which components of the input contributed most to the
prediction and whether the model's behavior appears coherent or anomalous in
the presence of manipulated images. Such approaches do not automatically make
the classification reliable, nor do they replace experimental validation or
human oversight, but they do contribute to documenting and critically
interpreting the role played by automation in forensic analysis
\cite{Adadi2018,Doshi-Velez2017}.

From this perspective, operational robustness is directly connected to the need
for traceability, verifiability, and critical oversight that characterizes
\gls{df}. An automatic system can certainly provide effective support for
evidence triage and prioritization, but its use requires that results be
interpreted in light of the model's limitations, the experimental conditions
under which it was evaluated, and the possible margins of error. The present
thesis is situated precisely within this problematic space: it does not assume
automation to be intrinsically reliable, but instead examines its empirical
resilience in order to understand under which conditions it may offer useful
support and under which, by contrast, it requires particular caution.

From an evidentiary standpoint, this approach does not imply that the model
should be regarded as an autonomous source of technical truth, but rather that
any use of it must be accompanied by adequate methodological awareness. In
other words, the relevance of the present study also lies in providing useful
elements to distinguish between the merely instrumental use of \gls{ai} and
uncritical reliance on algorithmic decisions that are not sufficiently robust.
This is particularly important in a context in which digital evidence requires
rigorous procedures and in which the adoption of automatic tools must be
defensible not only in terms of efficiency, but also in terms of technical
controllability and their reliability in forensic contexts
\cite{cpp1988,aiact2024,swgde_guidelines}.

\section{Structure of the Thesis}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The thesis is organized into six chapters, following a progression that moves
from the theoretical context and the state of the art to the experimental
methodology, the analysis of the results, and the discussion of their
operational implications.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction}} introduces the research problem, the
objectives of the work, and the contribution of the thesis with respect to the
operational robustness of proxy models and AI-based software systems employed in \gls{df} for the
automated classification or semantic retrieval of images.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:background}} presents the theoretical foundations
related to digital evidence, the use of \gls{ai} in forensic analysis,
automated image classification, \gls{advml}, anti-forensic techniques, and
\gls{xai}.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:state-of-the-art}} critically analyzes the relevant
literature, identifying the gap concerning the integrated evaluation of the
robustness of models and AI-based software systems in realistic forensic scenarios.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}} describes the \gls{fairlab}
experimental protocol, including dataset construction and validation, proxy
models, the considered perturbations, the black-box evaluation of the selected
software, the metrics, and the reproducibility controls.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:results}} presents the experimental results,
discussing system behavior on unmanipulated images, out-of-distribution
content, anti-forensic transformations, adversarial perturbations, and
qualitative cases examined through \gls{xai}.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusions}} synthesizes the main findings,
discusses their implications for the controlled use of automation in forensic
contexts, and identifies the limitations of the study as well as possible
directions for future research.

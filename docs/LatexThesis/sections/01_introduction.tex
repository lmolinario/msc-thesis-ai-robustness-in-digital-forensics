\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The integration of \gls{ai}-based systems into \gls{df} workflows addresses
the need to examine growing volumes of digital data and identify content that
may be relevant to an investigation. When automated support is used to
classify, organize, or retrieve images, however, its operational value depends
on the stability and interpretability of the outputs produced under realistic
conditions. Degraded, distributionally different, or intentionally manipulated
inputs may prevent relevant content from receiving appropriate review priority
or may increase the amount of irrelevant material presented to the analyst.

Against this background, the present thesis evaluates the operational
robustness of transparent proxy models and selected black-box software
configurations used for automated image classification or semantic retrieval of
images depicting firearms and related visual content. The evaluation compares
behavior across clean binary inputs, a separate \gls{ood} branch, adversarial
conditions, and anti-forensic transformations. Its purpose is not to validate
automation as an autonomous evidentiary decision-making mechanism, but to
examine the conditions under which it may provide controlled and traceable
support for forensic triage. Human oversight, source-to-output traceability,
explicit configuration management, and cautious interpretation of automated
results remain central principles throughout the study
\cite{costantini2019digital,sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section[Digitalization, Threats, and the Central Role of Digital Forensics]
{Digitalization, Threats, and the Central Role of \glsentrylong{df}}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The progressive digitalization of contemporary society has transformed how
information is produced, stored, transmitted, and analyzed. Cloud
infrastructures, distributed services, mobile devices, social platforms, and
heterogeneous information systems generate increasing volumes of data that may
be relevant to personal, institutional, investigative, and judicial activities.
The same technological evolution has expanded the points at which digital
information may be altered, concealed, destroyed, or otherwise compromised
\cite{garfinkel2010digital,casey2011digital}.

Within this context, \gls{df} provides the scientific and technical methods
required to identify, acquire, preserve, examine, analyze, and present digital
evidence in a traceable and technically defensible manner. The value of an
analytical result depends not only on the information obtained, but also on the
possibility of reconstructing which data were examined, which operations were
performed, and which limitations affected the analysis
\cite{palmer2001road,reith2002examining,nist2006guide,iso27037}.

These requirements also apply to the tools used during examination and
analysis. When automated functions influence the selection, organization, or
prioritization of evidence, their reliability and documentation may directly
affect the completeness and verifiability of the forensic workflow. The present
study is positioned within this problem: it examines automated image-analysis
outputs as operational support whose value depends on controlled validation,
traceability, and continued expert review.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section[Automation of Forensic Analysis and the Role of AI]
{Automation of Forensic Analysis and the Role of \glsentryshort{ai}}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The quantitative and qualitative expansion of the data subject to investigation
has made exclusively manual forensic analysis increasingly difficult to
sustain. Traditional methods remain essential for technical control,
verification, and final interpretation, but they may be difficult to scale when
analysts must examine large collections of images, videos, logs, conversations,
system files, and heterogeneous artifacts. Under these conditions, automation
can support the organization, prioritization, and preliminary examination of
evidence collections
\cite{garfinkel2010digital,costantini2019digital}.

The integration of \gls{ai}, \gls{ml}, and \gls{dl} techniques into forensic
tools has expanded this support through semantic classification, visual
similarity analysis, grouping of recurring patterns, anomaly detection, and
retrieval of potentially relevant material. These functions do not merely
accelerate existing procedures. By influencing which items receive early
attention and which remain outside the initial review set, they may alter the
allocation of analyst time and the organization of human examination
\cite{sanna2025cybercrime,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

The benefits of automation therefore cannot be assessed exclusively in terms of
speed or workload reduction. A false negative may prevent relevant content from
being prioritized, whereas a false positive may increase analytical noise and
the amount of material requiring manual verification. The operational
consequences depend on both the frequency and the direction of the errors.
Evaluation must consequently extend beyond nominal accuracy and consider
unexpected or manipulated inputs, system versions and configurations, the
semantics of observable outputs, the rules used to normalize them, and the
traceability of each automated result.

Automated classifications, tags, bookmarks, and semantic retrieval results must
remain subordinate to expert verification. They may support triage and evidence
organization, but they do not independently establish the relevance,
authenticity, or evidentiary weight of an image. This distinction is especially
important for proprietary or otherwise non-transparent systems, whose internal
models, thresholds, and preprocessing procedures may not be available to the
analyst.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section[Robustness of AI-Based Systems and Adversarial and Anti-Forensic Threats]
{Robustness of \glsentryshort{ai}-Based Systems and Adversarial and
Anti-Forensic Threats}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Strong performance under standard conditions does not necessarily imply stable
behavior in the presence of altered, degraded, distributionally different, or
hostile inputs. This limitation is particularly relevant to \gls{df}, where
automated image-analysis systems may process content affected by compression,
resizing, color alteration, platform-specific processing, degradation, or
intentional manipulation.

Anti-forensic techniques broadly include strategies intended to obstruct the
identification, acquisition, preservation, examination, or interpretation of
potentially relevant data. They may involve concealment, obfuscation, metadata
manipulation, or image-level transformations independently of whether an
\gls{ai}-based classifier is present. Steganography represents one example of
the broader domain, although the experimental anti-forensic branch of this
thesis is restricted to image-level transformations directly relevant to the
robustness of automated visual analysis
\cite{yaacoub2021digital,sanna2025cybercrime}.

Adversarial attacks are instead defined in relation to their effect on a model
or decision process. The literature on \gls{advml} has shown that deliberately
optimized inputs may induce erroneous predictions even when the applied
alteration is constrained or visually limited. These findings challenge the
assumption that high clean-data accuracy is sufficient evidence of reliability
under realistic or hostile conditions
\cite{goodfellow2015explaining,carlini2017towards,biggio2018wild,nowroozi2021survey}.

The two domains remain conceptually distinct, but they intersect when a
manipulation reduces the ability of an automated system to identify, retrieve,
or prioritize content of investigative interest. A manipulation directed at a
classifier may therefore also produce an anti-forensic consequence by affecting
triage and subsequent human review
\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

The threat model is essential to interpreting these effects. Direct-target
evaluation must be distinguished from empirical transfer to a different
architecture or software system. White-box and score-based access represent
different attacker capabilities, whereas deterministic model-agnostic
transformations follow a separate experimental rationale. The resulting effects
must not be treated as equivalent forms of attack success.

Operational robustness is defined in this thesis as the observable stability of
a specific system, version, configuration, task, and output mapping across the
frozen input conditions included in the protocol. It is not an absolute or
version-independent property. A system may perform strongly on clean inputs
while increasing missed-target risk under a specific manipulation, generating
additional false-positive review workload, producing closed-set assignments on
out-of-task content, or exhibiting sensitivity to thresholds, queries, and
normalization rules.

\section{Research Problem and Relevance in Realistic Forensic Scenarios}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Despite the extensive literature on adversarial attacks and model robustness,
the evaluation of these issues in operationally oriented forensic scenarios
remains comparatively limited. Many studies rely on standardized benchmarks,
general-purpose \textit{computer vision} datasets, or experimental conditions
that do not reproduce the organization and interpretive constraints of a
forensic workflow.

Studies more closely aligned with forensic practice indicate that automated
image analysis must address large evidence collections, heterogeneous sources,
task-specific semantic categories, ambiguous content, and outputs intended to
support analyst review. Silva et al.\ propose an \gls{ai}-based system for
classifying images contained in criminal evidence, including firearms and other
investigatively relevant categories. More recent work by Sanna et al.\ shows
that automated functions integrated into forensic software may exhibit
operationally relevant limitations when processing manipulated or unexpected
content
\cite{silva2021microservices,sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

The research problem is therefore not limited to demonstrating that a
classifier can be induced to fail. It is necessary to determine how an error
affects the support provided to the analyst: whether relevant content is no
longer prioritized, whether irrelevant material is routed toward review,
whether recall and selectivity change, and whether out-of-task inputs receive
closed-set assignments. It is also necessary to distinguish effects produced by
attacks optimized against a source model from empirical transfer to other
architectures or proprietary software, and to determine how configuration and
output-normalization choices influence the observed behavior.

Addressing these questions requires evaluation populations aligned with the
defined operational task. Such populations should include realistic positives,
realistic negatives, manipulated variants, borderline cases, and content that
does not conform to the nominal classification problem. This principle is
consistent with research on scenario-oriented evaluation of automated visual
systems, including the PHASER framework, which emphasizes alignment between
evaluation data and the intended deployment context
\cite{mckeown2024phaser}.

The present thesis applies this principle to a controlled task centered on
realistic individual firearms and clearly recognizable firearm-related visual
content. The supervised component distinguishes \texttt{weapon} and
\texttt{non\_weapon}, while a separate \gls{ood} branch contains borderline,
anomalous, degraded, synthetic, semantically adjacent, and otherwise out-of-task
content. The \gls{ood} branch is not treated as a third supervised class. It is
used to characterize the assignments produced when closed-set systems process
inputs that do not conform to the nominal binary task
\cite{silva2021microservices,Hao2018DeepFirearm}.

Adversarial and anti-forensic artifacts are generated exclusively from the
binary subset, preserving the distinction among supervised correctness,
clean-to-perturbed error transitions, and behavior on the separate \gls{ood}
branch. The evaluation combines transparent proxy models with selected
black-box software configurations on the same frozen source population and
derived perturbation suite. The proxy models provide an inspectable
experimental reference, whereas the proprietary systems are assessed through
outputs observable to the analyst. No assumption is made that the proxy models
reproduce or explain their inaccessible internal components.

Within this framework, a \texttt{weapon}-to-\texttt{non\_weapon} transition may
prevent potentially relevant content from being prioritized, while the opposite
transition may increase manual review workload. An \gls{ood} assignment to
\texttt{weapon} is not an ordinary supervised false positive, but it may still
route out-of-task material toward a firearm-oriented review category.

The research problem is therefore located at the intersection of algorithmic
robustness, software configuration, output interpretation, and operational
reliability. The scientific contribution lies in defining and applying an
integrated, task-specific evaluation protocol, while the operational relevance
lies in identifying limitations, error profiles, configuration dependencies,
and traceability requirements that must remain visible when automated tools are
used to support evidence analysis
\cite{silva2021microservices,mckeown2024phaser,sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

\section{Research Objectives}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This thesis aims to evaluate systematically the operational robustness of
transparent proxy models and selected black-box software configurations used for
the automated classification or semantic retrieval of images of potential
investigative interest in \gls{df}. The general objective is to determine
whether behavior observed under clean reference conditions remains stable when
the evaluated systems process distributionally different, degraded,
transformed, or intentionally perturbed images.

The experimental scope distinguishes the supervised
\texttt{weapon}/\texttt{non\_weapon} task from a separate \gls{ood} branch and
treats adversarial and anti-forensic conditions as distinct evaluation branches.
The adversarial branch comprises model-dependent attacks and the deterministic,
model-agnostic \gls{colorshift} condition, which is interpreted separately from
direct-target and transferred attack success. The anti-forensic branch comprises
plausible image-level transformations that may arise through ordinary media
processing or may be used intentionally to reduce the effectiveness of
automated analysis
\cite{hendrycks2017baseline,hendrycks2019benchmarking}.

Within this scope, the work is organized around four main objectives.

The first objective is to define a rigorous, documented, and reproducible
methodological protocol for evaluating operational robustness under controlled
but operationally relevant forensic image-analysis conditions. The protocol
connects human-validated dataset construction, deterministic partitioning,
proxy-model evaluation, perturbation generation, blind-bundle preparation,
black-box output normalization, quantitative and qualitative analysis, and
traceability controls.

The second objective is to evaluate heterogeneous systems on the same frozen
source population and derived perturbation suite. The transparent level
comprises EfficientNet-B0, ResNet18, and a \gls{clip}-based proxy consisting of
a frozen visual encoder and a supervised binary classification head. The
black-box level comprises the selected software configurations evaluated
exclusively through observable outputs and explicitly documented normalization
rules.

The third objective is to characterize robustness through more than aggregate
accuracy. The analysis considers false negatives, false positives,
clean-to-perturbed error transitions, \gls{ood} weapon assignment rates,
architecture-specific maximum predicted-class probabilities, and
configuration-dependent changes in recall and selectivity. Direct attacks
against fold-specific EfficientNet-B0 source models are distinguished from
empirical transfer to ResNet18, the \gls{clip}-based proxy, and the selected
black-box configurations. A complementary qualitative \gls{xai} analysis of
selected EfficientNet-B0 cases supports case-level diagnostic inspection but is
not treated as quantitative robustness evidence or as an explanation of the
black-box systems.

The fourth objective is to relate the experimental findings to the operational
use of automation in \gls{df}. Automated classifications, tags, bookmarks, and
semantic retrieval results are treated as triage indicators rather than as
autonomous evidentiary determinations. The analysis therefore identifies the
limitations, error profiles, configuration dependencies, traceability
requirements, and forms of human verification necessary for compatibility with
a \textit{human-in-the-loop} forensic workflow
\cite{costantini2019digital,faqir2023digital,sanna2024preliminary}.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Research Questions}
\label{subsec:introduction-research-questions}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The experimental design addresses the following research questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1.}] To what extent do the transparent proxy models maintain
    their clean-baseline performance under the separate \gls{ood}, adversarial,
    and anti-forensic conditions?

    \item[\textbf{RQ2.}] How do direct source-model attacks, cross-architecture
    transfer, and the model-agnostic \gls{colorshift} condition differ in their
    effects on the evaluated proxy models?

    \item[\textbf{RQ3.}] How do the selected black-box software systems respond
    to the same frozen experimental corpus, and how do observable output
    semantics and configuration choices affect the resulting operational
    profiles?

    \item[\textbf{RQ4.}] Which error transitions, robustness degradations,
    \gls{ood} assignment behaviors, and non-operational outputs are most
    relevant to forensic triage and continued human review?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Contribution of the Thesis}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The main contribution of this thesis lies in the definition and application of
a methodological protocol for evaluating the operational robustness of
transparent proxy models and selected black-box software configurations used for
automated image classification or semantic retrieval in \gls{df}. Unlike
evaluations focused exclusively on standard \textit{computer vision} benchmarks
or aggregate clean-condition performance, the study adopts a forensic and
operational perspective across clean, \gls{ood}, adversarial, and anti-forensic
conditions
\cite{nowroozi2020phd,hendrycks2019benchmarking,sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

The contribution is articulated across four interconnected dimensions.

The first contribution is the formalization of the \textbf{\gls{fairlab}}
protocol. \gls{fairlab} coordinates human-validated dataset construction,
deterministic partitioning, transparent proxy-model evaluation, controlled
perturbation generation, blind-bundle preparation, black-box output
normalization, error-oriented metrics, qualitative explainability, and
traceability controls. It is not proposed as a new classifier, defensive
mechanism, standalone forensic application, or general theoretical architecture,
but as a documented and auditable procedure for evaluating heterogeneous
systems under controlled but operationally relevant conditions.

The second contribution is the construction of a frozen experimental corpus
aligned with the selected forensic image-analysis task. The source population
contains 1\,500 images: 500 \texttt{weapon} images, 500
\texttt{non\_weapon} images, and 500 images reserved for the separate
\gls{ood} branch. The 1\,000-image binary subset is used to generate five
adversarial or adversarial-style populations and five anti-forensic populations.
Together with the clean binary and \gls{ood} inputs, these derivatives form an
11\,500-item forensic evaluation bundle comprising 1\,000 clean binary items,
500 clean \gls{ood} items, 5\,000 adversarial artifacts, and 5\,000
anti-forensic artifacts. The 10\,000 manipulated artifacts remain derived
source--condition combinations rather than independent underlying cases.

Sample selection follows a documented \textit{human-in-the-loop} procedure.
Manual validation is treated as an explicit methodological component, and the
adopted criteria, reviewer decisions, and associated limitations remain
traceable
\cite{sambasivan2021data}.

The third contribution is the parallel evaluation of transparent proxy models
and selected black-box software configurations on the same frozen experimental
basis. The transparent level comprises EfficientNet-B0, ResNet18, and a
\gls{clip}-based proxy. The black-box level comprises four software systems
evaluated through six frozen configurations:
\gls{magnetaxiomtool}/\gls{magnetai},
\gls{griffeye}/\gls{t3kcore},
\gls{excirefoto2025} at \texttt{D20}, \texttt{D50}, and \texttt{D80}, and
\gls{cellebriteinseyets}. Their tags, bookmarks, exported classifications, and
semantic-query results are normalized through explicit operational rules without
assuming equivalent internal taxonomies or decision mechanisms.

The proxy models provide an inspectable reference and the controlled generation
layer for model-dependent adversarial artifacts. The protocol distinguishes
direct-target attacks generated against fold-specific EfficientNet-B0
checkpoints from empirical transfer to ResNet18, the \gls{clip}-based proxy, and
the selected black-box configurations. The deterministic and model-agnostic
\gls{colorshift} condition remains separate, preventing transfer results from
being interpreted as direct robustness evaluations of systems against which no
matched attack was optimized.

The fourth contribution is an auditable and error-oriented interpretation of
operational robustness. The evaluation considers false negatives affecting
firearm-oriented content, false positives increasing analyst workload,
directional clean-to-perturbed transitions, closed-set assignments on the
separate \gls{ood} branch, configuration-dependent recall and selectivity, and
residual embedded-metadata sensitivity. Stable identifiers, manifests,
integrity records, and normalized outputs preserve source-to-result traceability
throughout the experimental workflow and support procedural reproducibility and
artifact-level auditability.

A complementary qualitative \gls{xai} analysis applies \gls{ig} to five
purposively selected EfficientNet-B0 cases. The attribution maps support
case-level diagnostic inspection but are not treated as quantitative robustness
evidence, causal explanations, semantic localization ground truth, or
explanations of the selected black-box systems.

Overall, the thesis does not propose a universally robust firearm classifier or
rank the evaluated software products. Its contribution consists in defining and
applying a traceable procedure for revealing how heterogeneous automated
image-analysis systems behave when the assumptions of clean, closed-set
evaluation no longer hold. Automated outputs remain triage indicators whose
value depends on task-specific validation, explicit configuration and mapping
rules, source-to-result traceability, and continued expert review
\cite{costantini2019digital,sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

\section{Technical-Forensic Relevance and Operational Reliability}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

In forensic contexts, the performance of an automated tool cannot be assessed
independently of the task for which it is used and of the consequences that its
outputs may have on evidence analysis. A false negative on firearm-oriented
content may prevent a potentially relevant image from receiving appropriate
review priority, whereas a false positive may increase the amount of irrelevant
material requiring examination. An \gls{ood} image assigned to
\texttt{weapon} is not an ordinary supervised false positive, but it may still
trigger a flag or increase the material routed toward review
\cite{faqir2023digital,sanna2025cybercrime}.

The relevance of an automated output therefore depends on more than aggregate
accuracy. It also depends on the system version, active configuration,
observable output semantics, adopted normalization rule, input population, and
threat model. This is particularly important for black-box systems, whose tags,
bookmarks, exported classifications, percentages, or semantic-query results do
not necessarily represent equivalent binary decisions or comparable uncertainty
measures.

Operational reliability is closely connected to traceability, verifiability,
and critical oversight. A defensible result should remain linked to the source
item and its integrity record, the model checkpoint or software version, the
active configuration, the observable output, the normalization rule, and the
condition under which processing occurred. Automated outputs must remain
reviewable components of a \textit{human-in-the-loop} workflow rather than
autonomous sources of technical truth.

The study remains deliberately limited from a legal and evidentiary perspective.
It does not determine the admissibility, probative value, or evidentiary weight
of an automated classification, nor does it certify any evaluated software
product. Such determinations require case-specific legal analysis, independent
validation, chain-of-custody assessment, disclosure of the applicable technical
procedures, and consideration of the relevant procedural framework. The
technical-forensic relevance of the work instead lies in distinguishing the
controlled instrumental use of \gls{ai} from uncritical reliance on automated
outputs
\cite{cpp1988,aiact2024,swgde_guidelines}.

\section{Structure of the Thesis}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The thesis is organized into seven chapters, progressing from the theoretical
and research context to the methodological protocol, its implementation, the
experimental results, and their operational interpretation. A separate appendix
documents the principal repository and reproducibility artifacts.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction}} introduces the research problem,
defines the objectives and Research Questions, presents the main contributions,
and outlines the technical-forensic relevance of the study.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:background}} presents the theoretical foundations of
digital evidence, automated forensic analysis, \gls{ai}-based image
classification, \gls{advml}, anti-forensic techniques, \gls{ood} behavior, and
\gls{xai}.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:state-of-the-art}} reviews the relevant literature and
identifies the gap concerning integrated and operationally oriented robustness
evaluation of transparent models and black-box software systems.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}} defines the high-level \gls{fairlab}
protocol, including the research design, evaluation branches, threat model,
metrics, explainability approach, and principles governing traceability,
integrity, and reproducibility.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}} documents the concrete experimental
setup, data and model configurations, perturbation-generation procedures,
forensic evaluation bundle, software versions, output-normalization rules, and
technical validation controls.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:results}} presents the clean, \gls{ood}, adversarial,
anti-forensic, black-box, sensitivity, comparative, and qualitative
explainability results and interprets their operational implications.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusions}} answers the Research Questions,
summarizes the findings and contributions, discusses limitations and forensic
implications, and identifies directions for future work.

The separate
\textbf{Appendix~\ref{app:repository-reproducibility-artifacts}} documents the
repository structure, canonical artifacts, official pipeline sequence,
validation assets, and boundaries of controlled reproducibility.

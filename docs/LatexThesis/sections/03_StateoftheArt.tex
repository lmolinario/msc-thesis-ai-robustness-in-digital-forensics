\chapter{State of the Art}
\label{chap:state-of-the-art}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
This chapter examines the scientific literature relevant to the thematic axes
of the thesis, following the editorial distinction established with respect to
Chapter~\ref{chap:background}: for each area, it discusses the main existing
contributions, what they have demonstrated, and which limitations remain open,
without redefining concepts already introduced in the theoretical background.
The objective is to identify the specific gap that motivates the experimental
approach of the thesis: the limited availability, in the existing literature,
of integrated experimental protocols for evaluating the operational robustness
of proxy models and AI-based software in realistic forensic scenarios.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{AI and Automation in Digital Forensics}
\label{sec:sota-ai-df}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The scale of digital data represents the starting point of the literature on
automation in \gls{df}. Garfinkel~\cite{garfinkel2010digital}, in a work that
shaped research over the following decade, documented how the rapid growth of
digital data volumes was making manual analysis increasingly unsustainable,
identifying automation as one of the priority directions for investment. The
contribution did not concern specific solutions, but rather a mapping of
unresolved problems: among them, the lack of methods for the automatic triage
of large multimedia corpora, the absence of shared benchmarks, and the
difficulty of evaluating tools under realistic operational conditions. This
agenda influenced subsequent research, which responded through the progressive
integration of \gls{ml} and \gls{dl} techniques into forensic workflows.

A first systematic attempt to map this integration was provided by Marturana et
al.~\cite{marturana2011digital}, who analyzed the state of the art of
artificial intelligence applied to \gls{df} at a time when the field was still
predominantly experimental. Their work showed that existing applications were
fragmented across domains---file system analysis, content classification, and
pattern matching---and that a unified approach capable of covering the entire
investigative workflow was lacking. The models proposed in the literature
achieved good performance in controlled settings, but had not been
systematically evaluated on data originating from real-world scenarios or
tested with respect to their reliability in the presence of anomalous inputs.

This research direction consolidated over the following decade. Costantini et
al.~\cite{costantini2019digital} proposed a framework integrating automated
reasoning and \gls{ml} techniques to support the forensic analyst in managing
complex evidence, with applications to event correlation and multimedia content
classification. The work documents how \gls{ai} can reduce the operational
workload in the examination and analysis phases, but leaves the issue of
robustness open: the framework assumes well-formed data and does not address
scenarios in which inputs have been manipulated or degraded. This limitation is
relevant because real forensic data are often of variable quality and may have
been intentionally altered before acquisition.

A more recent and operationally oriented perspective is provided by
Faqir~\cite{faqir2023digital}, who offers an overview of \gls{ai}-based
applications in contemporary digital investigations, including automatic
triage, classification of illicit content, facial recognition, and
communication analysis. The work highlights that the adoption of these tools in
investigative practices has grown significantly, but also emphasizes a still
unresolved tension: most systems are evaluated in terms of aggregate accuracy
on controlled datasets, without adequately considering the stability of their
predictions under manipulated or out-of-distribution input conditions. This gap
between performance measured under controlled conditions and operational
reliability is one of the most relevant critical points for positioning the
present thesis.

A more recent perspective broadens the operational framework by jointly
considering the opportunities offered by \gls{ai} in digital investigations and
the risks arising from its possible use for evasive or anti-forensic purposes.
Along these lines, Sanna et al.~\cite{sanna2025cybercrime} discuss the possible
support provided by \gls{ai} to cybercrime detection and \gls{df} activities,
while also highlighting how general-purpose generative tools may facilitate the
production of images or code intended for the steganographic concealment of
information. The empirical evaluation of the robustness of AI-based software
with respect to manipulated visual content is instead addressed more directly
by the studies on \textit{Magnet.AI} and \textit{Excire Photo AI}, discussed in
the following section \cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

Overall, this line of research has produced three consolidated findings. First,
\gls{ai}-based automation in \gls{df} is motivated by a real problem of scale
that traditional methods can no longer manage in a fully sustainable way
\cite{garfinkel2010digital,costantini2019digital}. Second, \gls{ai} acts as an
operational support for the analyst, not as a substitute, and evidentiary
conclusions remain a human responsibility
\cite{faqir2023digital,sanna2025cybercrime}. Third, the validation of
\gls{ai}-based systems in \gls{df} remains partial: the literature tends to
test models on clean and controlled data, while less systematically studying
their behavior when inputs are degraded, manipulated, or out of distribution
\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

It is precisely this third point that opens the specific problem addressed in
the following section: if the validation of \gls{ai}-based models in \gls{df}
is still incomplete at the academic level, the situation becomes even more
critical for commercial software used or evaluated in the context of forensic
analysis, where \gls{ai}-based functionalities may be proprietary, opaque, and
difficult to subject to independent verification.

\section{AI-based Software for Forensic Image Analysis and Operational Validation}
\label{sec:sota-ai-tools}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The integration of \gls{ai}-based functionalities into software used for the
analysis of digital evidence represents one of the main operational evolutions
of \gls{df}. In the case of visual content, such functionalities can support
triage, semantic search, automatic categorization, and the prioritization of
images to be submitted to analyst review. However, the availability of an
automated function does not, in itself, imply that its behavior is sufficiently
robust in the presence of degraded, ambiguous, or intentionally manipulated
inputs. This problem is particularly relevant when AI components are
proprietary and the software can be observed only through its produced outputs,
without access to the internal models, training datasets, or decision thresholds
adopted \cite{costantini2019digital,sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

The literature specifically devoted to the operational robustness of AI-based
software usable in forensic contexts remains limited. Sanna et al.\ conducted
an initial analysis of \textit{Magnet.AI}, integrated into \textit{Magnet
AXIOM}, and \textit{Excire Photo AI}, examining the behavior of automatic
functionalities with respect to anomalous or manipulated content
\cite{sanna2024preliminary}. A subsequent study further investigated the
response of the same software to facial deepfakes and morphed images,
identifying critical issues in the robustness of the analyzed AI-enhanced
functionalities and emphasizing the particular forensic relevance of false
negatives, since potentially relevant content may fail to be brought to the
analyst's attention \cite{sanna2025pixelperfect}. These works constitute the
closest reference to the present thesis, while differing from the problem
considered here, which concerns the operational \texttt{weapon}/\texttt{non\_weapon}
task, oriented toward the identification of images containing firearms or
firearm-oriented content, and the separate analysis of system behavior in the
presence of \texttt{ood} content.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Magnet AXIOM and Magnet.AI}
\label{subsec:sota_magnet_ai}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\textit{Magnet AXIOM} is commercial software designed for the processing and
analysis of evidence originating from heterogeneous digital sources. Among the
functionalities relevant to automated image analysis, the \textit{Magnet.AI}
component makes it possible to categorize multimedia content that may be
relevant to an investigation, including categories related to weapons, drugs,
nudity, and other sensitive content \cite{magnetai_official,sanna2024preliminary}.
This functionality is directly relevant to the task addressed in the present
thesis, because it makes it possible to observe whether images containing
firearms or firearm-oriented content are flagged within a forensic analysis
workflow.

Previous studies have shown that \textit{Magnet.AI} can be subjected to
operational evaluation in a \textit{black-box} setting, by observing the
categories returned by the software without assuming knowledge of the
underlying model. In particular, Sanna et al.\ examined the behavior of the
software with respect to manipulated content and, subsequently, with respect to
facial images generated through deepfake and morphing techniques, identifying
limitations in the robustness of some automatic functionalities
\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}. The main methodological
limitation remains the opacity of the proprietary component: the researcher can
measure the behavior of the outputs available to the analyst, but cannot
reconstruct with certainty the architecture, training data, pre-processing, or
internal decision logic.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Excire Foto 2025}
\label{subsec:sota_excire_foto}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Previous literature has analyzed \textit{Excire Photo AI} as AI-based software
oriented toward the search and recognition of photographic content, including
in controlled forensic analysis contexts
\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}. In these studies, Excire was
evaluated primarily with respect to identity search functionalities and its
behavior on manipulated facial images. The reported results show that deepfakes
and morphing can substantially degrade the reliability of automatic image
retrieval associated with specific identities \cite{sanna2025pixelperfect}.

The scope of the present thesis is different. The software considered is
\textit{Excire Foto 2025}, used as a standalone general-purpose application
equipped with AI functionalities for organizing and searching visual content,
and not as a native component of a tool developed exclusively for forensic
purposes \cite{excirefoto2025_official}. Its inclusion in the study responds to
the methodological interest in verifying whether a visual semantic search
system can be used, in a controlled forensic analysis context, to retrieve
images consistent with an investigatively relevant concept such as the presence
of a weapon.

This distinction is essential for interpreting the results: \textit{Excire Foto
2025} should not be described as a native forensic
\texttt{weapon}/\texttt{non\_weapon} classifier, but rather as AI-based software
whose observable behavior can be mapped to an operational decision of retrieval
or non-retrieval of an image with respect to the queries and configurations
defined in the experimental protocol. In this case as well, the evaluation
remains \textit{black-box}: the thesis measures the outputs available to the
analyst, without assuming knowledge of the model, the training data, or the
internal similarity criteria.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Cellebrite Inseyets and Automated Media Classification}
\label{subsec:sota_cellebrite}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The Cellebrite ecosystem is widely associated with mobile forensics and digital
investigation activities. The vendor documentation describes \textit{Cellebrite
Inseyets} as an integrated suite for accessing, extracting, processing,
analyzing, and reporting on digital evidence originating primarily from mobile
devices \cite{cellebrite_inseyets_official}. From this perspective, Inseyets
does not represent a single isolated module, but an operational environment
that combines extraction, analysis, triage, and investigative workflow
management functionalities.

With respect to visual content, Cellebrite documentation also describes
\textit{media classification} functionalities oriented toward the automated
categorization of images and videos through AI-based techniques
\cite{cellebrite}. These functionalities are part of a broader trend in
contemporary Digital Forensics, in which commercial tools integrate automatic
modules to support the triage, selection, and prioritization of large volumes
of multimedia content.

In the context of the present thesis, \textit{Cellebrite Inseyets} is therefore
relevant as an example of commercial black-box software in which AI-assisted
functionalities are integrated into professional mobile forensic analysis
workflows. Compared with desktop or general-purpose tools primarily oriented
toward photographic search and categorization, Cellebrite makes it possible to
extend the reasoning on operational robustness to a context closer to mobile
forensics, where the analyst may need to examine large volumes of images and
videos extracted from devices or digital storage media.

As with the other commercial tools considered in the state of the art, the
integration of automatic functionalities does not eliminate the need to assess
their reliability, limitations, and behavior in the presence of degraded,
manipulated, or not fully representative inputs with respect to ideal operating
conditions. For this reason, Cellebrite Inseyets is recalled as a further
significant case of commercial black-box software with respect to the general
problem of the operational robustness of AI-assisted systems in forensic
contexts.
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Magnet Griffeye e T3K CORE}
\label{subsec:sota_griffeye_t3k}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\textit{Magnet Griffeye} is a commercial platform used for the triage, review,
and organization of large volumes of multimedia content in investigative and
forensic contexts. In the context of the present thesis, the tool is relevant
not as a controllable academic classifier, but as commercial black-box software
capable of producing observable semantic outputs on images through automatic
analysis modules.

The \textit{T3K CORE} component makes it possible to generate automatic
semantic bookmarks associated with the analyzed multimedia content. These
bookmarks can be used by the analyst to filter, prioritize, or review
potentially relevant images. From a methodological perspective, this type of
output differs from the categories returned by \textit{Magnet.AI}, from the
semantic retrieval results produced by \textit{Excire Foto 2025}, and from the
classifications exported in the Cellebrite workflow. Nevertheless, it belongs
to the same family of observable operational signals that can be normalized
against an experimental ground truth.

The relevance of \textit{Magnet Griffeye} for this thesis therefore lies in the
possibility of extending the black-box evaluation to an additional output
paradigm: not a class probability or an explicit supervised decision, but a set
of automatically generated semantic bookmarks. This characteristic reinforces
the general methodological problem addressed in the thesis: different
commercial tools may expose heterogeneous outputs, but they must be mapped to a
common representation only through explicit, documented rules for normalization
that are consistent with the experimental task.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Common Limitations of Black-Box Evaluation}
\label{subsec:sota_black_box_limits}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The software considered presents different functional characteristics and
should not be interpreted as directly equivalent systems. \textit{Magnet.AI}
exposes operational categories applicable to image content; \textit{Excire Foto
2025} is considered as a system for semantic search and retrieval of visual
content; \textit{Cellebrite Inseyets} requires the identification of the
component concretely used for media categorization; \textit{Magnet Griffeye},
through \textit{T3K CORE}, produces automatic semantic bookmarks. Therefore, an
operational comparison among these software systems cannot presuppose identity
of task, internal models, decision thresholds, or output modalities.

A common limitation concerns the proprietary or otherwise non-observable nature
of the AI components employed. In a \textit{black-box} evaluation, it is not
possible to directly control the model architecture, training dataset, update
procedures, pre-processing, confidence calibration, or decision criteria.
Validation must therefore rely on external and verifiable elements:
independent ground truth, a documented dataset, common experimental conditions,
reproducible manipulated inputs, exportable outputs, and matching procedures
based on identifiers or hashes.

In light of these limitations, the contribution of the present thesis does not
consist in internally characterizing the proprietary algorithms of the software
analyzed, nor in proposing a new classification tool. Rather, it consists in
evaluating, through a reproducible experimental protocol, the operational
robustness of observable outputs in the presence of clean images,
out-of-distribution content, adversarial perturbations, and anti-forensic
transformations. This setting extends previous literature toward a different
visual task of investigative interest and toward a comparison between
controllable proxy models and AI-based software evaluated in a \textit{black-box}
setting.

\section{Automated Image Classification in Forensic Scenarios}
\label{sec:sota-image-classification}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Automated image classification constitutes one of the main points of contact
between computer vision and \gls{df}. In the forensic literature, the problem
is not addressed as a simple general visual recognition task, but as an
activity supporting the selection and prioritization of potentially relevant
evidence within large multimedia collections. In this context, the objective is
not only to assign a correct label to an image, but also to reduce the
analyst's workload, increase the likelihood of identifying investigatively
significant content, and limit the risk that relevant elements remain hidden
within very large datasets~\cite{costantini2019digital,sanna2024preliminary}.

Early contributions in this direction applied \gls{ml} and \gls{dl} models to
semantic classification, object recognition, and multimedia triage tasks, with
particular attention to categories of investigative interest such as faces,
objects, scenes, illicit content, or elements potentially associated with
criminal conduct. Costantini et al.~\cite{costantini2019digital} place these
functionalities within a broader evidence analysis workflow, in which automated
classification does not replace forensic interpretation, but contributes to
organizing and filtering the acquired material. This setting is relevant
because it clarifies that visual classification, in forensic contexts, has a
primarily operational function: directing the analyst's attention toward
portions of evidence that deserve more detailed review.

In the specific domain of weapon recognition, Hao et al.~\cite{Hao2018DeepFirearm}
proposed DeepFirearm, an approach based on deep representations for
fine-grained firearm recognition. Their contribution shows that the
classification of images containing firearms or firearm-oriented content cannot
be reduced to a trivial visual problem: the variability of poses, perspectives,
occlusions, image quality, and morphological similarity among different models
requires discriminative representations capable of capturing relevant visual
details. However, works of this type are mainly oriented toward recognition or
retrieval under controlled experimental conditions, and do not systematically
address the behavior of models when images are degraded, manipulated, or
originating from heterogeneous sources typical of a digital investigation.

The availability of public datasets for weapon detection, such as the
\textit{Weapon Detection Dataset}~\cite{Sanyal2023KaggleWeapon}, has supported
the development of models capable of recognizing firearms or firearm-oriented
content in static images. These resources have important experimental value,
but present limitations when transferred to realistic forensic scenarios.
Public datasets are often designed for visual recognition or surveillance
applications, rather than for the operational validation of AI-based models and
software usable in forensic contexts. As a result, they may not adequately
represent borderline cases, degraded images, out-of-distribution content,
screenshots, synthetic images, or material originating from social platforms
and messaging applications. This distance between benchmark datasets and real
investigative material constitutes one of the most relevant critical issues in
the evaluation of \gls{ai}-based systems applied to \gls{df}.

From an architectural perspective, the computer vision literature has shown the
effectiveness of deep \gls{cnn} architectures for image classification.
Architectures such as ResNet~\cite{he2016deep} and
EfficientNet~\cite{tan2019efficientnet} have provided solid foundations for
many visual classification systems, owing to their ability to learn
hierarchical representations of image content. More recently, vision-language
models such as \gls{clip}~\cite{radford2021learning} and
\gls{blip}~\cite{li2022blip}, together with \gls{vit} architectures
\cite{dosovitskiy2020image}, have broadened the scope of automated
classification by enabling forms of text-guided, zero-shot, or
semantic-description-based classification. In forensic contexts, this evolution
is potentially relevant because investigative categories may not perfectly
coincide with the classes of traditional datasets; however, the literature has
not yet sufficiently clarified how stable these models are in the presence of
degraded, ambiguous, or manipulated images.

A recurring aspect in the literature is the different operational severity of
classification errors. In forensic tasks, a false positive may increase the
analyst's review burden, but a false negative may prevent relevant content from
being submitted to human review. This asymmetry is particularly important in
triage systems, in which the automatic output does not directly produce an
evidentiary conclusion, but influences the order in which evidence is examined.
Consequently, the evaluation of a forensic classifier cannot be limited to
average accuracy: it must also consider recall on critical investigative
classes, the distribution of errors, the stability of predictions, and the
ability not to assign a known class with excessive confidence to
out-of-distribution inputs.

The most recent literature on AI-based software usable in forensic contexts
confirms this critical issue. Sanna et al.~\cite{sanna2024preliminary} show
that automatic classification functionalities integrated into operational
software may produce unstable results when visual content deviates from
expected conditions. Although these studies do not exhaust the problem of image
classification in forensic contexts, they clearly indicate that the transition
from models that are accurate on controlled datasets to tools that are reliable
in investigative scenarios requires more rigorous evaluation protocols. In
particular, the combination of visual classification, investigatively relevant
content, out-of-distribution images, and intentional manipulations remains
poorly explored.

Overall, the literature on automated image classification provides useful
models and datasets, but leaves open a specific problem for \gls{df}: the
limited availability of integrated evaluations that simultaneously consider the
investigative relevance of the classes, the heterogeneity of sources, the
presence of borderline or \gls{ood} cases, and robustness with respect to
degradations, anti-forensic manipulations, or adversarial perturbations. This
gap prepares the transition to the following section, devoted to benchmarks and
results from the literature on the robustness of computer vision models.

\section{Robustness Evaluation: Benchmarks and Findings from the Literature}
\label{sec:sota-robustness-benchmarks}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Recent literature in computer vision has systematically shown that accuracy
obtained on clean and distributionally homogeneous data is not sufficient to
estimate the operational reliability of a model. This finding is particularly
relevant for \gls{df}, because images analyzed in an investigative context
rarely coincide with the controlled conditions typical of academic benchmarks:
they may originate from heterogeneous devices, have been compressed by social
platforms or messaging applications, present low resolution, contain
occlusions, or have been intentionally altered before acquisition. Within this
framework, robustness is not an accessory property of the classifier, but a
necessary condition for automation to be used as reliable support for evidence
triage and prioritization.

A central contribution in this direction is the work of Hendrycks and
Dietterich~\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}, who introduced a systematic
benchmark for evaluating the behavior of classification models under common
corruptions and perturbations. Their work showed that models with high
performance on clean data may suffer significant degradation when exposed to
noise, blur, compression, chromatic variations, or other realistic alterations.
The relevant point for the present thesis is not the specific nature of the
benchmark, but the empirical result it makes evident: nominal performance does
not necessarily predict model stability under non-ideal operational
conditions. This finding weakens the use of accuracy on clean data as the sole
evaluation criterion for systems intended for real-world environments.

Similar findings have emerged in the object detection domain. Michaelis et
al.~\cite{michaelis2019winter} showed that models trained and evaluated under
favorable conditions may lose effectiveness when exposed to environmental
variations, visual degradations, or scenarios distant from the training
distribution. Although their application domain is not forensic, the
contribution is methodologically relevant because it confirms that robustness
must be evaluated with respect to plausible perturbations of visual data, not
only with respect to standardized datasets. In forensic contexts, this
observation becomes even more critical: an apparently ordinary degradation may
affect the system's ability to recognize investigatively relevant content and,
consequently, the priority assigned to the evidence.

A second line of literature concerns the detection of out-of-distribution
inputs. Hendrycks and Gimpel~\cite{hendrycks2017baseline} showed that neural
classifiers may produce high-confidence predictions even on inputs that do not
belong to the classes expected by the task. This behavior is problematic in
operational contexts, because an automatic system may forcibly assign an
anomalous image to a known class instead of signaling uncertainty. Fort et
al.~\cite{fort2021exploring} further highlighted the limitations of \gls{ood}
detection approaches, showing that the ability to recognize anomalous inputs
strongly depends on the type of distribution considered and on the degree of
distance from the training data. For \gls{df}, this implies that borderline,
synthetic, heavily degraded, or task-irrelevant images should not be treated as
simple classification errors, but as a structural component of reliability
evaluation.

The literature on robustness benchmarks has also shown that model instability
may emerge both in the presence of realistic degradations and in the presence
of intentionally constructed perturbations. Ford et al.~\cite{ford2019adversarial}
discussed the relationship between error on noisy data and adversarial
vulnerability, suggesting that model fragility with respect to intentional
attacks is not completely separable from sensitivity to natural or realistic
input variations. This relationship is important for the present thesis because
it justifies an integrated evaluation that considers both anti-forensic
transformations and adversarial attacks.

A further relevant contribution is RobustBench~\cite{croce2020robustbench},
which provided a standardized reference for comparing models and defenses with
respect to adversarial attacks. Although this benchmark belongs to the general
computer vision domain and is not designed for forensic scenarios, it shows the
importance of shared protocols, comparable metrics, and controlled
experimental conditions. The absence of an equally consolidated reference
specifically oriented toward \gls{df} constitutes a relevant gap: models and
tools used in investigative contexts are often evaluated according to
non-uniform criteria, on datasets that are difficult to compare, and without a
systematic verification of their robustness with respect to realistic or
hostile manipulations.

AutoAttack belongs to the same research direction, proposing an automated and
more standardized evaluation procedure for estimating the adversarial
robustness of classifiers, reducing the risk of overestimating defenses through
attack configurations that are not sufficiently strong or not comparable
\cite{croce2020autoattack}. In this case as well, the contribution does not
directly concern \gls{df}, but reinforces the methodological need to use
explicit, reproducible, and sufficiently stringent evaluation protocols.

The need to extend robustness evaluation beyond general benchmarks also emerges
from studies applied to automatic systems used in security contexts. Nowroozi
et al.~\cite{nowroozi2024verifying}, although in a domain different from
forensic image analysis, show that adversarial perturbations can substantially
degrade the performance of systems that appear reliable under ordinary
conditions. More directly in the context of forensic analysis of visual
content, Sanna et al.~\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect} show
that AI-based software usable in forensic workflows may exhibit fragility when
exposed to manipulated inputs. These findings confirm that the robustness
problem concerns both experimentally evaluated models and the operational
outputs of software observed in a \textit{black-box} setting.

Overall, the literature on robustness benchmarks provides three fundamental
indications. First, accuracy on clean data is not sufficient to evaluate the
reliability of a classifier intended for real-world scenarios. Second, the
presence of degraded, manipulated, or out-of-distribution inputs must be
considered an integral part of the evaluation protocol, not a marginal
condition. Third, the forensic domain requires specific benchmarks and
experimental protocols capable of connecting algorithmic robustness to the
operational impact on triage and evidence selection. This need naturally leads
to the literature on \gls{advml} applied to images, where degradation of model
behavior is no longer caused by realistic or accidental variations, but by
perturbations intentionally constructed to induce classification errors.

\section{Adversarial Machine Learning Applied to Image Classifiers}
\label{sec:sota-adversarial-ml}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The literature on \gls{advml} has shown that image classifiers based on
\gls{ml} and \gls{dl} may be vulnerable to inputs intentionally constructed to
alter their predictions. Chapter~\ref{chap:background} introduced the
fundamental concepts of adversarial example, threat model, and input
perturbation; in this section, the focus shifts to the main findings of the
literature and to their relevance for visual classifiers that may be employed
in forensic scenarios.

The seminal works by Szegedy et al.~\cite{szegedy2014intriguing} and Goodfellow
et al.~\cite{goodfellow2015explaining} established that high-accuracy deep
classifiers can produce incorrect decisions in the presence of intentionally
constructed perturbations, even when such alterations are perceptually limited.
For \gls{df}, this finding is relevant because it shows that an automatic
system used in visual triage can be induced to underestimate or ignore
investigatively relevant content if the input has been manipulated before
acquisition or analysis.

A second line of literature has investigated the construction of attacks that
are more precise, iterative, or optimized with respect to the minimum
perturbation required to modify the model decision. DeepFool
\cite{moosavi2016deepfool} showed that it is possible to estimate
small-magnitude perturbations capable of moving an image across the
classifier's decision boundary, thereby contributing to a more systematic
evaluation of the distance between a clean input and an adversarial input.
\gls{superdeepfool} \cite{zhang2024superdeepfool} belongs to the same research
direction, proposing an evolution oriented toward generating effective
perturbations against deep models through a more targeted optimization process.
These works were not developed specifically for \gls{df}, but they are relevant
because they provide a methodological basis for measuring how unstable a visual
classifier may be with respect to non-random image variations.

The literature has also shown that adversarial vulnerability does not concern
only perturbations distributed across many pixels. Su et al.~\cite{su2019onepixel}
introduced the \gls{onepixel}, demonstrating that, in some cases, modifying an
extremely limited number of pixels may be sufficient to induce an incorrect
classification. More recently, approaches based on sparse constraints, such as
\gls{sigmazero} \cite{zhao2024sigmazero}, have further explored the possibility
of constructing attacks with localized alterations and limited perceptual
impact. This line of research is particularly significant for forensic
contexts, because it shows that effective image manipulation does not
necessarily require extensive or visually evident transformations: even
localized modifications can interfere with the behavior of automatic models.

A further line of research has shown that adversarial perturbations may also
take localized and visually more explicit forms, as in the case of adversarial
patches. Brown et al.~\cite{brown2017patch} demonstrated that an artificial
region applied to an image can induce classification errors even without
modifying the entire visual content. For the present thesis, this result is
relevant mainly at the conceptual level: it shows that classifier vulnerability
does not concern only distributed and nearly imperceptible perturbations, but
also localized alterations that can interfere with the model decision-making
process.

The literature also includes generative attacks, in which generative networks
are trained to produce transferable adversarial perturbations. A representative
example is AdvGAN, which employs a generator to construct adversarial examples
efficiently and with transferability across models \cite{xia2018advgan}. This
family of attacks is not included in the experimental protocol of the present
thesis, because the work prioritizes perturbations that are more directly
interpretable in the operational context of \gls{df}.

A crucial aspect for operational scenarios is the distinction among white-box,
black-box, and transfer-based attacks. Papernot et al.~\cite{papernot2016limitations}
showed that model vulnerability can transfer across different classifiers,
whereas subsequent literature on black-box attacks has systematized scenarios
in which the attacker has only limited access to the model outputs or uses
surrogate models to generate transferable perturbations \cite{bhambri2020survey}.
Carlini and Wagner~\cite{carlini2017towards} contributed to strengthening the
security evaluation of classifiers by showing the limitations of several
defenses proposed against adversarial attacks. The surveys by Akhtar and
Mian~\cite{akhtar2018threat} and Yuan et al.~\cite{yuan2019adversarial}
confirm that the transferability of adversarial examples is one of the most
relevant problems for the use of \gls{ai}-based models in environments that are
not fully controlled. In the case of commercial software evaluated in forensic
contexts, this observation is particularly important: even when the model
integrated into the software is proprietary and not inspectable, an attacker
could generate perturbations on accessible models and verify their
effectiveness through transfer.

Biggio and Roli~\cite{biggio2018wild} framed these vulnerabilities within the
paradigm of \textit{adversary-aware security}, arguing that the evaluation of a
machine-learning-based system must explicitly consider the presence of a
strategic adversary. This principle is central to the present thesis: a
forensic classification system does not necessarily operate in a neutral
environment, but may be exposed to data prepared or modified by actors
interested in hindering the analysis. Adversarial robustness, therefore, is not
only a technical property of the model, but a component of its operational
reliability.

In the domain closer to \textit{image forensics}, Barni et
al.~\cite{barni2018cf} showed that neural-network-based models for detecting
digital manipulations may be vulnerable to adversarial attacks.
Nowroozi~\cite{nowroozi2020phd} further investigated the problem of \gls{ml}
techniques for \textit{image forensics} in adversarial scenarios, highlighting
how models designed for forensic analysis can be degraded by targeted
perturbations. These contributions are particularly important because they move
the problem from generic computer vision examples to systems used for tasks
closer to the forensic domain. The emerging result is that adversarial
vulnerability does not concern only generic classifiers trained on standard
datasets, but also models developed to recognize manipulations or relevant
characteristics in digital images.

The connection with \gls{ai}-based software usable in forensic contexts has
been reinforced by more recent works on operational robustness. Nowroozi et
al.~\cite{nowroozi2024verifying} showed, in a different security context, that
robustness verification with respect to adversarial perturbations must be an
integral part of the evaluation of automatic systems. Sanna et
al.~\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect} brought this perspective
more directly into the domain of forensic analysis, showing that \gls{ai}-based
functionalities integrated into operational software may exhibit fragility when
exposed to manipulated content. Although these studies do not exhaust the full
spectrum of adversarial attacks on images, they clearly indicate that the
validation of \gls{ai}-based software usable in forensic contexts cannot be
limited to its effectiveness on ordinary inputs.

A further line of literature has analyzed the use of input transformations as a
possible defense against adversarial examples. Techniques such as
recompression, resizing, or image transformations may reduce the effectiveness
of some perturbations, but they do not eliminate the problem of systematic
robustness evaluation \cite{guo2017countering}. This aspect is relevant to the
present thesis because some transformations considered in the literature as
defense mechanisms may assume a different meaning in a forensic context: they
are not employed to protect the model, but to simulate realistic
anti-forensic stress conditions and evaluate the operational stability of
classifiers and commercial tools.

Overall, the literature on \gls{advml} applied to images provides three
indications that are relevant to this thesis. First, visual classifiers may be
vulnerable to intentional perturbations even when they achieve high performance
on clean data. Second, this vulnerability may manifest across scenarios that
differ in attacker knowledge, perturbation magnitude, and transferability
across models. Third, works specifically addressing \textit{image forensics}
show that even systems designed for forensic tasks are not immune to these
fragilities. However, an important gap remains open: most studies consider
academic models or controlled benchmarks, whereas integrated evaluations that
combine visual classifiers, \gls{ai}-based software observed in a
\textit{black-box} setting, realistic datasets, and adversarial attacks within
the same experimental protocol are still limited.

This gap prepares the transition to the following section. Adversarial attacks
represent manipulations designed with respect to model behavior; in forensic
contexts, however, images may also be altered through more ordinary or
anti-forensic transformations, such as recompression, resizing, blurring, and
tonal modifications. These transformations do not necessarily require knowledge
of the classifier, but they may still affect the stability of automated
analysis.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Anti-Forensics and Realistic Transformations: Impact on Automated Classification}
\label{sec:sota-antiforensics}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Starting from the conceptual distinction, introduced in
Chapter~\ref{chap:background}, between adversarial perturbations oriented toward
the model decision and anti-forensic transformations applied to the digital
artifact, this section examines contributions that have studied the impact of
realistic image manipulations on forensic analysis. The relevant point for the
present thesis is that transformations not optimized with respect to the
classifier may nevertheless affect input quality and the stability of the
automatic outputs used in visual triage.

Yaacoub et al.~\cite{yaacoub2021digital} and the taxonomic literature on
anti-forensics~\cite{hewling2018anti} frame anti-digital forensics as the set
of techniques aimed at making the identification, acquisition, preservation, or
interpretation of digital evidence more difficult. In the image domain, this
perspective includes both manipulations aimed at deleting or masking forensic
traces and apparently ordinary transformations that may alter the
characteristics of the file. The contribution of this literature is important
because it shifts attention from the sole authenticity of the image to its
reliability as an input for analysis: an image may remain semantically
understandable to a human observer while becoming more problematic for an
automatic classification system.

A particularly relevant line of work concerns \gls{jpeg} recompression. Stamm
and Liu~\cite{stamm2010anti} showed that anti-forensic operations applied to
compression can alter or mask traces useful for image analysis, reducing the
ability to identify previous manipulations. More generally, the literature on
\textit{image forensics} has shown that compression modifies the statistical
and frequency structure of the image, affecting details, textures, and
artifacts that can be exploited both by forensic analysts and by automatic
models~\cite{farid2016photo,stamm2013information}. This observation is directly
relevant to automated classification: if a model learns features that are
sensitive to compression quality, subsequent recompression may alter prediction
stability even without visibly modifying the semantic content of the image.

A second group of works concerns resizing, resampling, and geometric
transformations. Popescu and Farid~\cite{popescu2005exposing} showed that
resampling leaves detectable statistical traces, opening a central research
direction in the analysis of digital manipulations. From the perspective of
automated classification, however, the problem does not concern only the
possibility of detecting resampling, but also its effect on the representations
learned by models. Successive resizing operations, interpolations, cropping, or
changes in aspect ratio may modify proportions, edges, and visual details, with
particularly relevant consequences in tasks where the object of interest
occupies only a limited portion of the image. In forensic scenarios, this
effect may influence the system's ability to recognize investigatively relevant
objects, especially when they are partially occluded, small, or embedded in
visually complex contexts.

The literature on realistic image transformations has also considered
alterations such as blur, noise, chromatic variations, histogram modifications,
and contrast stretching. These transformations are not necessarily
anti-forensic in a strict sense: they may result from low-quality acquisition,
forwarding through messaging platforms, uploading to social networks, repeated
saving, or ordinary editing processes. However, their relevance to classifier
robustness has been highlighted by benchmarks on common corruptions, in which
degradations of this type produce significant performance reductions even in
models with high accuracy on clean data~\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}. In
forensic contexts, this evidence has operational significance: realistic
transformations do not represent marginal cases, but frequent conditions in the
digital material actually submitted to analysis.

Information hiding techniques, such as steganography, remain relevant in the
forensic domain, but are considered in this thesis as part of the general
theoretical framework and not as the main object of the experimental
evaluation. The empirical focus is instead placed on transformations that
directly affect automated visual classification, such as recompression,
resampling, blurring, and tonal modifications.

Recent studies on AI-based software usable in forensic contexts confirm that
such transformations must also be considered in their operational validation.
Sanna et al.~\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect} show that
manipulated content or content that does not conform to ordinary conditions may
reduce the reliability of the automatic outputs produced by the analyzed
software. The critical point is that anti-forensic and realistic
transformations do not necessarily require a sophisticated attack against the
model: they can be applied with common tools, introduced by sharing platforms,
or result from ordinary image processing steps. This makes them particularly
plausible in real investigative scenarios.

Overall, the anti-forensic literature and the literature on robustness to
realistic transformations converge on one point: the digital image should not
be regarded as a stable and neutral input. It may undergo alterations that
modify forensic traces, perceptual quality, and the features exploited by
automatic classifiers. However, most existing works analyze manipulation
detection, model robustness, or tool validation separately, without integrating
these aspects into a unified protocol oriented toward \gls{df}. Therefore, the
need remains open to evaluate how realistic and anti-forensic transformations
affect the automated classification of investigatively relevant content and,
more generally, the quality of the support provided by \gls{ai}-based systems
to the analyst.

This need leads to the following section, devoted to explainability. If
adversarial perturbations and anti-forensic transformations can modify model
behavior, it becomes necessary to have tools that make it possible, at least in
part, to analyze which image regions or visual characteristics contribute to
the automatic decision. \gls{xai} techniques do not solve the robustness
problem, but they can provide critical support for interpreting and diagnosing
the fragilities of \gls{ai}-based systems.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Explainability Applied to the Reliability Evaluation of AI-based Systems}
\label{sec:sota-xai}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The growing adoption of \gls{ai}-based systems in high-impact domains has made
the explainability of algorithmic decisions a central issue. In the context of
\gls{df}, this need assumes specific relevance: the output of an automatic
model may guide triage, influence the priority assigned to specific content,
and contribute to the selection of items to be submitted to human review. For
this reason, the literature on \gls{xai} is relevant not because it makes
models automatically reliable, but because it provides tools for critically
analyzing their behavior, especially when predictions change in the presence of
degraded, manipulated, or out-of-distribution inputs.

The surveys by Adadi and Berrada~\cite{Adadi2018}, Guidotti et
al.~\cite{Guidotti2019}, Barredo Arrieta et al.~\cite{Barredo2018}, and
Doshi-Velez and Kim~\cite{Doshi-Velez2017} have helped clarify that model
interpretability is not a unitary concept, but depends on the recipient of the
explanation, the type of model, the required level of granularity, and the
purpose of the evaluation. This distinction is particularly important in
forensic contexts. An explanation intended for a researcher evaluating a
classifier is not equivalent to an explanation intended for an operational
analyst or for a subject required to verify the technical correctness of a
procedure. It follows that, in the forensic context, \gls{xai} should be
understood as support for verification and diagnosis, not as a substitute for
experimental validation \cite{Adadi2018,Doshi-Velez2017,sanna2025cybercrime}.

In the domain of image classification, foundational works on local explanations
and visual attributions have provided methodologically different tools: LIME
operates through local model-agnostic approximations, Integrated Gradients uses
the gradient of the model with respect to the input, whereas Class Activation
Mapping techniques highlight regions associated with the prediction in specific
visual architectures \cite{Ribeiro2016,Sundararajan2017,Zhou2016}. The
limitation, with respect to the problem addressed in the present thesis, is
that these contributions were not designed to evaluate the operational
robustness of proprietary AI-based software in forensic contexts, nor to
establish whether an explanation remains diagnostically useful in the presence
of manipulated inputs. For this reason, the explainability analysis included in
the experimental protocol is referred to controllable proxy models and is used
as qualitative support for interpreting failure cases, not as a primary metric
nor as an equivalent explanation of the software evaluated in a
\textit{black-box} setting.

In the case of forensic image classification, this type of analysis is useful
above all for verifying whether, after a manipulation, local attributions remain
associated with the investigatively relevant object or instead shift toward
secondary regions, textures, backgrounds, or artifacts introduced by the
transformation. This diagnostic use does not assign autonomous evidentiary
value to the visual explanation, but makes it possible to connect the
quantitative degradation of performance to observable changes in classifier
behavior.

The literature has nevertheless highlighted important limitations of visual
explanations. Carvalho et al.~\cite{Carvalho2019} emphasize that model
interpretability strongly depends on the method used and that different
explanations may produce results that are not perfectly overlapping. Moreover,
many attribution techniques are sensitive to parameters, baselines, local
noise, or preprocessing choices. This implies that an apparently coherent
saliency map does not constitute autonomous proof of the model's correctness.
In forensic contexts, this caution is essential: a visual explanation may
support the analyst in understanding system behavior, but it cannot replace
manual verification of the evidence or quantitative validation of the
classifier.

A further limitation concerns the applicability of \gls{xai} to commercial
software evaluated in forensic contexts. Many attribution methods require
access to the model, to gradients, or at least to the possibility of repeatedly
querying the classifier. In proprietary software, these conditions are not
always satisfied. Consequently, while \gls{xai} can be applied in a relatively
controlled manner to academic or open-source models, its use on commercial
AI-based software is more complex and sometimes limited to indirect forms of
analysis. This aspect reinforces the problem already discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:sota-ai-tools}: the opacity of proprietary modules hinders not
only the reproducibility of classifications, but also their technical
explainability.

In the specific context of \gls{df}, the literature that systematically applies
\gls{xai} techniques to the robustness evaluation of forensic classifiers
remains limited. Works on \gls{xai} provide general tools for interpreting
\gls{dl} models, whereas studies on adversarial robustness and anti-forensics
mainly focus on performance metrics. A fully developed convergence among
forensic validation, robustness evaluation, and explainability is therefore
still lacking. This gap is significant because, in an investigative scenario,
it is not sufficient to know that a model fails after a manipulation: it is
also useful to understand whether the error is associated with shifts in local
attributions toward irrelevant regions, with artifacts introduced by the
transformation, or with a structural fragility of the learned representation.

In summary, \gls{xai} contributes to the reliability evaluation of \gls{ai}-based
systems in three ways. First, it makes it possible to qualitatively analyze
which image regions influence the prediction. Second, it allows the behavior of
the model on clean and manipulated images to be compared, highlighting possible
changes in the areas considered relevant. Third, it supports the diagnosis of
model fragilities, while providing no autonomous guarantees of correctness. For
these reasons, explainability constitutes a complement to the quantitative
evaluation of robustness, not a substitute for it.

The following section concludes the state-of-the-art review by highlighting the
overall gap that emerges from the research directions analyzed. The literature
has developed significant contributions on forensic automation, AI-based
software usable in forensic contexts, visual classification, robustness,
adversarial attacks, anti-forensic techniques, and explainability; however,
these axes have been addressed predominantly in a separate manner. The present
thesis is positioned precisely within the space left open by this fragmentation.

\section{Literature Gap and Thesis Positioning}
\label{sec:sota-gap}

The review conducted in the previous sections shows that the literature has
produced significant contributions on each of the axes relevant to this thesis,
but has addressed them predominantly in a separate manner. Works on automation
in \gls{df} have highlighted the need for tools capable of managing large
volumes of digital data and supporting the analyst in the triage, examination,
and analysis phases~\cite{garfinkel2010digital,costantini2019digital,faqir2023digital}.
Studies on AI-based software usable in forensic contexts have begun to document
concrete problems of opacity, independent validation, and fragility with
respect to manipulated content~\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.
The literature on automated image classification has shown the effectiveness of
computer vision models for recognizing objects and relevant content, but often
in controlled scenarios and not specifically oriented toward forensic
validation~\cite{Hao2018DeepFirearm,he2016deep,tan2019efficientnet,radford2021learning,li2022blip}.
In parallel, works on robustness have demonstrated that accuracy on clean data
is not sufficient to estimate the operational reliability of a
classifier~\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking,hendrycks2017baseline,fort2021exploring,croce2020robustbench}.

A second set of contributions concerns the threats that may alter the behavior
of automatic systems. The literature on \gls{advml} has shown that
intentionally constructed perturbations can induce errors even in high-
performing models~\cite{szegedy2014intriguing,goodfellow2015explaining,papernot2016limitations,carlini2017towards,biggio2018wild}.
Works closer to \textit{image forensics} have then highlighted that even models
designed for forensic tasks may be vulnerable in adversarial
scenarios~\cite{barni2018cf,nowroozi2020phd}. In parallel, the anti-forensic
literature has studied transformations applied directly to the digital
artifact, such as recompression, resampling, statistical alterations, and
changes in visual quality, showing that these operations may affect both
forensic traces and the digital representation of the
image~\cite{stamm2010anti,stamm2013information,farid2016photo,popescu2005exposing}.
Finally, the literature on \gls{xai} has provided useful tools for qualitatively
analyzing model behavior, but these techniques have rarely been integrated into
complete forensic protocols for robustness evaluation~\cite{Adadi2018,Doshi-Velez2017,Ribeiro2016,Sundararajan2017,Zhou2016}.

The specific gap identified by the present thesis therefore concerns the limited
availability of integrated experimental protocols that combine, within the same
evaluation design, automated image classification or semantic image retrieval,
\textit{human-in-the-loop} dataset validation, heterogeneous proxy models,
AI-based software selected for operational evaluation in a forensic context,
adversarial perturbations, anti-forensic transformations, quantitative metrics,
and explainable analysis of model behavior. The relevance of this integration
derives from the fact that, in a forensic context, the reliability of an
automatic system cannot be evaluated only as an isolated property of the
classifier. It also depends on the quality and representativeness of the data,
the presence of borderline or out-of-distribution cases, the nature of the
manipulations applied, the transparency of the software used, and the effect
that possible errors may produce on triage and evidence selection.

The present thesis is positioned precisely within this gap. Its contribution
consists in conducting an experimental evaluation of the operational robustness
of proxy models and AI-based software employed, in the context of \gls{df}, for
the automated classification or semantic retrieval of images of potential
investigative interest, jointly considering the algorithmic and
operational-investigative dimensions. The objective is not only to measure
performance degradation under adverse conditions, but also to understand how
such degradation may affect the system's ability to support the analyst in the
selection of relevant content. From this perspective, robustness is treated not
as an abstract attribute of the model, but as a functional requirement for the
controlled use of \gls{ai} in \gls{df} workflows.

The positioning of the thesis is therefore twofold. On the one hand, it connects
to the literature on \gls{advml}, robustness evaluation, and \gls{xai}, assuming
that classification systems must also be evaluated in the presence of hostile,
degraded, or anomalous inputs. On the other hand, it is situated within the
domain of \gls{df}, where the quality of automation must be assessed with
respect to its operational usefulness, the verifiability of the results, and
the possibility of maintaining human control over the analysis process. This
setting makes it possible to move beyond a purely performance-based evaluation
and to address robustness as a methodological, technical, and forensic issue.
This research space motivates the \gls{fairlab} experimental protocol adopted
in the present thesis and described in the following chapter.

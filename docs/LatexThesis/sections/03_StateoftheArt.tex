\chapter{State of the Art}
\label{chap:state-of-the-art}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This chapter critically examines the scientific literature relevant to the
main thematic areas of the thesis, following the organizational distinction
introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:background}. For each area, it discusses the
principal contributions, the evidence they provide, and the limitations that
remain unresolved, without repeating the concepts already presented in the
technical background.

The objective is to identify the specific research gap that motivates the
experimental approach adopted in this thesis: the limited availability of
integrated and operationally oriented protocols for evaluating transparent
proxy models and selected black-box software configurations on a common,
task-specific experimental basis under clean, \gls{ood}, adversarial, and
anti-forensic conditions.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{AI and Automation in Digital Forensics}
\label{sec:sota-ai-df}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The volume of digital data constitutes a central point of departure for the
literature on automation in \gls{df}. Garfinkel~\cite{garfinkel2010digital},
in a work that significantly shaped the research agenda of the following
decade, documented how the rapid growth of digital data volumes was rendering
manual analysis increasingly unsustainable, identifying automation as one of
the priority directions for investment. The work did not propose specific
solutions, but rather a mapping of unresolved problems: among them, the lack
of methods for the automated triage of large multimedia corpora, the absence
of shared benchmarks, and the difficulty of evaluating tools under realistic
operational conditions. This agenda influenced subsequent research, which
responded by progressively integrating \gls{ml} and \gls{dl} techniques into
forensic workflows.

A first systematic attempt to map this integration was made by Marturana et
al.~\cite{marturana2011digital}, who surveyed the state of the art in
artificial intelligence applied to \gls{df} at a time when the field was still
predominantly experimental. Their work showed that existing applications were
fragmented across domains---file system analysis, content classification, and
pattern matching---and that a unified approach capable of covering the entire
investigative workflow was lacking. The models proposed in the literature
achieved good performance in controlled settings, but had not been
systematically evaluated on data drawn from real-world scenarios or tested for
reliability in the presence of anomalous inputs.

This research direction matured over the following decade. Costantini et
al.~\cite{costantini2019digital} proposed a framework integrating automated
reasoning and \gls{ml} techniques to support the forensic analyst in managing
complex evidence, with applications to event correlation and multimedia content
classification. The work demonstrates how \gls{ai} can reduce the operational
workload in the examination and analysis phases, but leaves the question of
robustness unaddressed: the framework assumes well-formed data and does not
address scenarios in which inputs have been manipulated or degraded. This
limitation is relevant because real forensic data are often of variable quality
and may have been intentionally altered before acquisition.

A more recent and operationally oriented perspective is offered by
Faqir~\cite{faqir2023digital}, who provides an overview of \gls{ai}-based
applications in contemporary digital investigations, including automated
triage, classification of illicit content, facial recognition, and
communication analysis. The work highlights that the adoption of these tools
in investigative practices has grown significantly, but also draws attention to
a persistent tension: most systems are evaluated in terms of aggregate accuracy
on controlled datasets, without adequately considering the stability of their
predictions under manipulated or out-of-distribution input conditions. This gap
between performance measured under controlled conditions and operational
reliability is among the most pertinent issues motivating the positioning of
the present thesis.

A further recent perspective broadens the operational framework by jointly
considering the opportunities offered by \gls{ai} in digital investigations
and the risks arising from its possible use for evasive or anti-forensic
purposes. Along these lines, Sanna et al.~\cite{sanna2025cybercrime} discuss
the possible support provided by \gls{ai} to cybercrime detection and \gls{df}
activities, while also highlighting how general-purpose generative tools may
facilitate the production of images or code intended for the steganographic
concealment of information. The empirical evaluation of the robustness of
\gls{ai}-based tools with respect to manipulated visual content is instead
addressed more directly by the studies on \textit{Magnet.AI} and
\textit{Excire Photo AI}, discussed in the following
section~\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

Overall, this line of research has produced three main findings. First,
\gls{ai}-based automation in \gls{df} is motivated by a genuine scaling
challenge that traditional methods can no longer address sustainably
\cite{garfinkel2010digital,costantini2019digital}. Second, \gls{ai} serves as
operational support for the analyst rather than a substitute, and evidentiary
conclusions remain a human responsibility
\cite{faqir2023digital,sanna2025cybercrime}. Third, the validation of
\gls{ai}-based systems in \gls{df} remains partial: the literature tends to
test models on clean and controlled data, without systematically examining
their behavior when inputs are degraded, manipulated, or out of distribution
\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

It is precisely this third finding that motivates the specific problem
addressed in the following section: if validation of \gls{ai}-based models in
\gls{df} remains incomplete even in the academic literature, the situation
becomes even more critical for commercial software used or evaluated in the
context of forensic analysis, where \gls{ai}-based functionalities may be
proprietary, opaque, and difficult to verify independently.

\section{AI-based Software for Forensic Image Analysis and Operational Validation}
\label{sec:sota-ai-tools}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The integration of \gls{ai}-based functionalities into software used for the
analysis of digital evidence represents one of the main operational
developments in \gls{df}. In the context of visual content, such
functionalities can support triage, semantic search, automated categorization,
and the prioritization of images for analyst review. However, the availability
of an automated function does not, in itself, imply that its behavior is
adequately robust in the presence of degraded, ambiguous, or intentionally
manipulated inputs. This problem is particularly relevant when \gls{ai}
components are proprietary and the software can be observed only through the
outputs it produces, without access to the underlying models, training
datasets, or decision thresholds~\cite{costantini2019digital,sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

The literature specifically devoted to the operational robustness of
\gls{ai}-based software applicable to forensic contexts remains limited. Sanna
et al.\ conducted an initial evaluation of \textit{Magnet.AI}, integrated into
\textit{Magnet AXIOM}, and \textit{Excire Photo AI}, examining the behavior of
their automated functionalities with respect to anomalous or manipulated
content~\cite{sanna2024preliminary}. A subsequent study further investigated
the response of the same tools to facial deepfakes and morphed images,
identifying critical limitations in the robustness of the \gls{ai}-based
functionalities examined, and emphasizing the particular forensic relevance of
false negatives, since potentially relevant content may fail to reach the
analyst's attention~\cite{sanna2025pixelperfect}. These works constitute the
most directly relevant prior contributions for the present thesis, while
differing from it in the specific task considered: the present work concerns
the operational \texttt{weapon}/\texttt{non\_weapon} classification task,
focused on the identification of images containing firearms or related content,
and includes a separate analysis of system behavior in the presence of
\texttt{ood} inputs.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Magnet AXIOM and Magnet.AI}
\label{subsec:sota_magnet_ai}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\textit{Magnet AXIOM} is commercial software designed for the processing and
analysis of evidence from heterogeneous digital sources. Among the
functionalities relevant to automated image analysis, the \textit{Magnet.AI}
component enables the categorization of multimedia content that may be relevant
to an investigation, including categories related to weapons, drugs, nudity,
and other sensitive content~\cite{magnetai_official,sanna2024preliminary}.
This functionality is directly relevant to the task addressed in the present
thesis, as it allows one to observe whether images containing firearms or
related content are flagged within a forensic workflow.

Previous studies have shown that \textit{Magnet.AI} can be subjected to
operational evaluation in a black-box setting, by observing the categories
returned by the software without assuming knowledge of the underlying model.
In particular, Sanna et al.\ examined the behavior of the software with respect
to manipulated content and, subsequently, with respect to facial images
generated through deepfake and morphing techniques, identifying limitations in
the robustness of certain automated functionalities~\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.
The primary methodological limitation remains the opacity of the proprietary
component: the researcher can measure the behavior of the outputs available to
the analyst, but cannot reconstruct with certainty the architecture, training
data, preprocessing, or internal decision logic.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Magnet Griffeye and T3K CORE}
\label{subsec:sota_griffeye_t3k}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\textit{Magnet Griffeye} is a commercial platform used for the triage, review,
and organization of large volumes of multimedia content in investigative and
forensic contexts. In the context of the present thesis, the tool is relevant
not as a controllable academic classifier, but as commercial black-box software
capable of producing observable semantic outputs on images through automated
analysis modules.

The \textit{T3K CORE} component enables the generation of automated semantic
bookmarks associated with the analyzed multimedia content. These bookmarks can
be used by the analyst to filter, prioritize, or review potentially relevant
images. From a methodological perspective, this type of output differs from the
categories returned by \textit{Magnet.AI}, from the semantic retrieval results
produced by \textit{Excire Foto 2025}, and from the classifications exported
in the Cellebrite workflow. Nevertheless, it belongs to the same family of
observable operational signals that can be normalized against an experimental
ground truth.

The relevance of \textit{Magnet Griffeye} to this thesis therefore lies in the
possibility of extending the black-box evaluation to an additional output
paradigm: not a class probability or an explicit supervised decision, but a set
of automatically generated semantic bookmarks. This characteristic reinforces
the general methodological problem addressed in the thesis: different
commercial tools may expose heterogeneous outputs, yet all must be mapped to a
common representation through explicit, documented normalization rules that are
consistent with the experimental task.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Excire Foto 2025}
\label{subsec:sota_excire_foto}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Prior literature has analyzed \textit{Excire Photo AI} as \gls{ai}-based
software oriented toward the search and recognition of photographic content,
including in controlled forensic analysis
contexts~\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}. In these studies,
Excire was evaluated primarily with respect to identity search functionalities
and its behavior on manipulated facial images. The reported results show that
deepfakes and morphing can substantially degrade the reliability of automated
image retrieval associated with specific
identities~\cite{sanna2025pixelperfect}.

The scope of the present thesis is different. The software considered is
\textit{Excire Foto 2025}, used as a standalone general-purpose application
equipped with \gls{ai}-based functionalities for organizing and searching
visual content, and not as a native component of a tool developed exclusively
for forensic purposes~\cite{excirefoto2025_official}. Its inclusion in the
study reflects the methodological interest in verifying whether a visual
semantic search system can be employed, in a controlled forensic analysis
context, to retrieve images consistent with a concept of investigative
relevance such as the presence of a weapon.

This distinction is essential for interpreting the results: \textit{Excire Foto
2025} should not be described as a native forensic
\texttt{weapon}/\texttt{non\_weapon} classifier, but rather as \gls{ai}-based
software whose observable behavior can be mapped to an operational decision of
retrieval or non-retrieval of an image with respect to the queries and
configurations defined in the experimental protocol. Here too, the evaluation
remains black-box: the thesis measures the outputs available to the analyst,
without assuming knowledge of the model, the training data, or the internal
similarity criteria.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Cellebrite Inseyets and Automated Media Classification}
\label{subsec:sota_cellebrite}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The Cellebrite ecosystem is widely associated with mobile forensics and digital
investigation activities. The vendor documentation describes \textit{Cellebrite
Inseyets} as an integrated suite for accessing, extracting, processing,
analyzing, and reporting on digital evidence originating primarily from mobile
devices~\cite{cellebrite_inseyets_official}. From this perspective, Inseyets
does not represent a single isolated module, but an operational environment
that combines extraction, analysis, triage, and investigative workflow
management functionalities.

With respect to visual content, Cellebrite documentation also describes media
classification functionalities oriented toward the automated categorization of
images and videos through \gls{ai}-based techniques
\cite{cellebrite,cellebrite_media_classification}. These
functionalities reflect a broader trend in contemporary \gls{df}, in which
commercial tools integrate automated modules to support the triage, selection,
and prioritization of large volumes of multimedia content.

In the context of the present thesis, \textit{Cellebrite Inseyets} is therefore
relevant as an example of commercial black-box software in which \gls{ai}-based
functionalities are integrated into professional mobile forensic workflows.
Compared with desktop or general-purpose tools primarily oriented toward
photographic search and categorization, Cellebrite extends the evaluation of
operational robustness to a mobile forensic context, where the analyst may need
to examine large volumes of images and videos extracted from devices or digital
storage media.

As with the other commercial tools considered in this section, the integration
of automated functionalities does not eliminate the need to assess their
reliability, limitations, and behavior in the presence of degraded, manipulated,
or not fully representative inputs relative to ideal operating conditions. For
this reason, \textit{Cellebrite Inseyets} is discussed as a further significant
case of commercial black-box software with respect to the general problem of
the operational robustness of \gls{ai}-based systems in forensic contexts.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Common Limitations of Black-Box Evaluation}
\label{subsec:sota_black_box_limits}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
The tools considered exhibit distinct functional characteristics and should not
be treated as equivalent systems. \textit{Magnet.AI} exposes operational
categories applicable to image content; \textit{Excire Foto 2025} is considered
as a system for semantic search and retrieval of visual content;
\textit{Cellebrite Inseyets} is evaluated through the specific media-analysis
workflow and exportable outputs used in the experiment; \textit{Magnet Griffeye}, through
\textit{T3K CORE}, produces automated semantic bookmarks. Accordingly, any
operational comparison across these tools cannot assume equivalence of task,
internal models, decision thresholds, or output modalities.

A common limitation concerns the proprietary or otherwise non-observable nature
of the \gls{ai} components employed. In a black-box evaluation, it is not
possible to directly control the model architecture, training dataset, update
procedures, preprocessing, confidence calibration, or decision criteria.
Validation must therefore rely on external and verifiable elements: an
independent ground truth, a documented dataset, common experimental conditions,
reproducible manipulated inputs, exportable outputs, and matching procedures
based on identifiers or hashes.

In light of these limitations, the contribution of the present thesis does not
consist of internally characterizing the proprietary algorithms of the analyzed
software, nor of proposing a new classification tool. Rather, it consists of
evaluating, through a reproducible experimental protocol, the operational
robustness of observable outputs in the presence of clean images,
out-of-distribution content, adversarial perturbations, and anti-forensic
transformations. This approach extends the prior literature to a different
visual task of investigative interest and to a comparison between controllable
proxy models and \gls{ai}-based software evaluated in a black-box setting.

\section{Automated Image Classification in Forensic Scenarios}
\label{sec:sota-image-classification}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Automated image classification constitutes one of the main points of contact
between computer vision and \gls{df}. In the forensic literature, this problem
is not treated as a simple general visual recognition task, but as an activity
supporting the selection and prioritization of potentially relevant evidence
within large multimedia collections. In this context, the objective is not only
to assign a correct label to an image, but also to reduce the analyst's
workload, increase the likelihood of identifying content of investigative
relevance, and limit the risk that relevant elements remain undetected within
very large datasets~\cite{costantini2019digital,sanna2024preliminary}.

Early contributions in this direction applied \gls{ml} and \gls{dl} models to
semantic classification, object recognition, and multimedia triage tasks, with
particular attention to categories of investigative interest such as faces,
objects, scenes, illicit content, or elements potentially associated with
criminal conduct. Costantini et al.~\cite{costantini2019digital} situate these
functionalities within a broader evidence analysis workflow, in which automated
classification does not replace forensic interpretation, but contributes to
organizing and filtering the acquired material. This framing is relevant
because it clarifies that visual classification, in forensic contexts, serves a
primarily operational function: directing the analyst's attention toward
portions of the evidence that warrant more detailed review.

In the specific domain of weapon recognition, Hao et
al.~\cite{Hao2018DeepFirearm} proposed DeepFirearm, an approach based on deep
representations for fine-grained firearm recognition. Their work demonstrates
that the classification of images containing firearms or related content cannot
be reduced to a trivial visual problem: the variability of poses, perspectives,
occlusions, image quality, and morphological similarity across different weapon
models requires discriminative representations capable of capturing relevant
visual detail. However, works of this type are primarily oriented toward
recognition or retrieval under controlled experimental conditions, and do not
systematically address the behavior of models when images are degraded,
manipulated, or drawn from the heterogeneous sources typical of a digital
investigation.

The availability of public datasets for weapon detection, such as the
\textit{Weapon Detection Dataset}~\cite{Sanyal2023KaggleWeapon}, has supported
the development of models capable of recognizing firearms and related content
in static images. These resources have significant experimental value, but
present limitations when transferred to realistic forensic scenarios. Public
datasets are often designed for visual recognition or surveillance
applications, rather than for the operational validation of \gls{ai}-based
models and software applicable to forensic contexts. As a result, they may not
adequately represent borderline cases, degraded images, out-of-distribution
content, screenshots, synthetic images, or material originating from social
platforms and messaging applications. This gap between benchmark datasets and
real investigative material constitutes one of the most relevant issues in the
evaluation of \gls{ai}-based systems applied to \gls{df}.

From an architectural perspective, the computer vision literature has
demonstrated the effectiveness of deep \gls{cnn} architectures for image
classification. Architectures such as ResNet~\cite{he2016deep} and
EfficientNet~\cite{tan2019efficientnet} have provided solid foundations for
many visual classification systems, owing to their ability to learn
hierarchical representations of image content. More recently, vision-language
models such as \gls{clip}~\cite{radford2021learning} and
\gls{blip}~\cite{li2022blip}, together with \gls{vit} architectures
\cite{dosovitskiy2020image}, have broadened the scope of automated
classification by enabling text-guided, zero-shot, or
semantic-description-based classification. In forensic contexts, this
development is potentially relevant because investigative categories may not
align precisely with the classes of traditional datasets; however, the
literature has not yet adequately clarified how stable these models are in the
presence of degraded, ambiguous, or manipulated images.

A recurring issue in the literature concerns the differing operational severity
of classification errors. In forensic tasks, a false positive may increase the
analyst's review burden, but a false negative may prevent relevant content from
reaching human review. This asymmetry is particularly significant in triage
systems, in which automated output does not directly produce an evidentiary
conclusion, but influences the order in which evidence is examined.
Consequently, the evaluation of a forensic classifier cannot be limited to
average accuracy: it must also consider recall on critical investigative
classes, the distribution of errors, the stability of predictions, and the
ability to avoid assigning a known class with excessive confidence to
out-of-distribution inputs.

The most recent literature on \gls{ai}-based software applicable to forensic
contexts confirms this concern. Sanna et al.~\cite{sanna2024preliminary}
demonstrate that automated classification functionalities integrated into
operational software may produce unstable results when visual content deviates
from expected conditions. Although these studies do not exhaust the problem of
image classification in forensic contexts, they indicate clearly that the
transition from models that are accurate on controlled datasets to tools that
are reliable in investigative scenarios requires more rigorous evaluation
protocols. In particular, the combination of visual classification, content of
investigative relevance, out-of-distribution images, and intentional
manipulations remains insufficiently explored.

Overall, the literature on automated image classification offers useful models
and datasets, but leaves open a specific problem for \gls{df}: the limited
availability of integrated evaluations that simultaneously consider the
investigative relevance of the classes, the heterogeneity of sources, the
presence of borderline or \gls{ood} cases, and robustness with respect to
degradations, anti-forensic transformations, or adversarial perturbations. This
gap motivates the transition to the following section, devoted to benchmarks
and results from the literature on the robustness of computer vision models.

\section{Robustness Evaluation: Benchmarks and Findings from the Literature}
\label{sec:sota-robustness-benchmarks}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Recent literature in computer vision has systematically demonstrated that
accuracy obtained on clean and distributionally homogeneous data is not
sufficient to estimate the operational reliability of a model. This finding is
particularly relevant for \gls{df}, because images analyzed in an investigative
context rarely correspond to the controlled conditions typical of academic
benchmarks: they may originate from heterogeneous devices, have been compressed
by social platforms or messaging applications, exhibit low resolution, contain
occlusions, or have been intentionally altered before acquisition. Within this
framework, robustness is not an incidental property of the classifier, but a
necessary condition for automation to serve as reliable support for evidence
triage and prioritization.

A central contribution in this direction is the work of Hendrycks and
Dietterich~\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}, who introduced a systematic
benchmark for evaluating the behavior of classification models under common
corruptions and perturbations. Their work showed that models with high
performance on clean data may suffer significant degradation when exposed to
noise, blur, compression, chromatic variations, or other realistic alterations.
The point relevant to the present thesis is not the specific nature of the
benchmark, but the empirical result it makes evident: nominal performance does
not necessarily predict model stability under non-ideal operational conditions.
This finding undermines the use of accuracy on clean data as the sole
evaluation criterion for systems intended for real-world environments.

Similar findings have emerged in the object detection domain. Michaelis et
al.~\cite{michaelis2019winter} showed that models trained and evaluated under
favorable conditions may lose effectiveness when exposed to environmental
variations, visual degradations, or scenarios distant from the training
distribution. Although their application domain is not forensic, the
contribution is methodologically relevant because it confirms that robustness
must be evaluated with respect to plausible perturbations of visual data, and
not solely with respect to standardized datasets. In forensic contexts, this
observation becomes even more critical: an apparently ordinary degradation may
affect the system's ability to recognize content of investigative relevance
and, consequently, the priority assigned to the evidence.

A second line of literature concerns the detection of out-of-distribution
inputs. Hendrycks and Gimpel~\cite{hendrycks2017baseline} showed that neural
classifiers may produce high-confidence predictions even on inputs that do not
belong to the classes expected by the task. This behavior is problematic in
operational contexts, because an automated system may forcibly assign an
anomalous image to a known class rather than signaling uncertainty. Fort et
al.~\cite{fort2021exploring} further highlighted the limitations of \gls{ood}
detection approaches, showing that the ability to recognize anomalous inputs
depends strongly on the type of distribution considered and on the degree of
distance from the training data. For \gls{df}, this implies that borderline,
synthetic, heavily degraded, or task-irrelevant images should not be treated as
simple classification errors, but as a structural component of any reliability
evaluation.

The literature on robustness benchmarks has also shown that model instability
may emerge both in the presence of realistic degradations and in the presence
of intentionally constructed perturbations. Ford et
al.~\cite{ford2019adversarial} examined the relationship between error on noisy
data and adversarial vulnerability, suggesting that model fragility with respect
to intentional attacks is not entirely separable from sensitivity to natural or
realistic input variations. This relationship is important for the present
thesis because it justifies an integrated evaluation encompassing both
anti-forensic transformations and adversarial perturbations.

A further relevant contribution is RobustBench~\cite{croce2020robustbench},
which provided a standardized reference for comparing models and defenses with
respect to adversarial attacks. Although this benchmark belongs to the general
computer vision domain and is not designed for forensic scenarios, it
demonstrates the importance of shared protocols, comparable metrics, and
controlled experimental conditions. The absence of an equally consolidated
reference specifically oriented toward \gls{df} constitutes a meaningful gap:
models and tools used in investigative contexts are often evaluated according
to non-uniform criteria, on datasets that are difficult to compare, and without
systematic verification of their robustness with respect to realistic or
adversarial manipulations.

AutoAttack belongs to the same research direction, proposing an automated and
more standardized evaluation procedure for estimating the adversarial
robustness of classifiers, and reducing the risk of overestimating defenses
through attack configurations that are insufficiently strong or not
comparable~\cite{croce2020autoattack}. Here too, the contribution does not
directly concern \gls{df}, but reinforces the methodological need for explicit,
reproducible, and sufficiently stringent evaluation protocols.

The need to extend robustness evaluation beyond general benchmarks also emerges
from studies applied to automated systems used in security contexts. Nowroozi
et al.~\cite{nowroozi2024verifying}, although working in a domain different
from forensic image analysis, show that adversarial perturbations can
substantially degrade the performance of systems that appear reliable under
ordinary conditions. More directly in the context of forensic analysis of
visual content, Sanna et
al.~\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect} show that \gls{ai}-based
software applicable to forensic workflows may exhibit fragility when exposed to
manipulated inputs. These findings confirm that the robustness problem affects
both experimentally evaluated models and the operational outputs of software
observed in a black-box setting.

Overall, the literature on robustness benchmarks yields three key indications.
First, accuracy on clean data is not sufficient to evaluate the reliability of a
classifier intended for real-world scenarios. Second, the presence of degraded,
manipulated, or out-of-distribution inputs must be treated as an integral
component of the evaluation protocol, not as a marginal condition. Third, the
forensic domain requires specific benchmarks and experimental protocols capable
of connecting algorithmic robustness to the operational impact on triage and
evidence selection. This need naturally leads to the literature on \gls{advml}
applied to images, in which degradation of model behavior is no longer caused
by realistic or accidental variations, but by perturbations intentionally
constructed to induce classification errors.

\section{Adversarial Machine Learning Applied to Image Classifiers}
\label{sec:sota-adversarial-ml}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The literature on \gls{advml} has shown that image classifiers based on
\gls{ml} and \gls{dl} may be vulnerable to inputs intentionally constructed to
alter their predictions. Chapter~\ref{chap:background} introduced the
fundamental concepts of adversarial examples, threat models, and input
perturbations; in this section, the focus shifts to the main findings of the
literature and to their relevance for visual classifiers that may be employed
in forensic scenarios.

The seminal works by Szegedy et al.~\cite{szegedy2014intriguing} and Goodfellow
et al.~\cite{goodfellow2015explaining} established that high-accuracy deep
classifiers can produce incorrect decisions in the presence of intentionally
constructed perturbations, even when such alterations are perceptually limited.
For \gls{df}, this finding is relevant because it shows that an automated
system used in visual triage can be induced to underestimate or overlook
content of investigative relevance if the input has been manipulated before
acquisition or analysis.

A second line of literature has investigated the construction of attacks that
are more precise, iterative, or optimized with respect to the minimum
perturbation required to alter the model's decision. DeepFool
\cite{moosavi2016deepfool} showed that it is possible to estimate
small-magnitude perturbations capable of moving an image across the
classifier's decision boundary, thereby providing a more systematic means of
measuring the distance between a clean input and an adversarial one.
\gls{superdeepfool}~\cite{zhang2024superdeepfool} belongs to the same research
direction, proposing an approach oriented toward generating effective
perturbations against deep models through a more targeted optimization process.
These works were not developed specifically for \gls{df}, but they are relevant
because they provide a methodological basis for measuring how unstable a visual
classifier may be with respect to non-random image variations.

The literature has also shown that adversarial vulnerability is not limited to
perturbations distributed across many pixels. Su et al.~\cite{su2019onepixel}
introduced the \gls{onepixel}, demonstrating that, in some cases, modifying an
extremely small number of pixels may be sufficient to induce an incorrect
classification. More recently, approaches based on sparse constraints, such as
\gls{sigmazero}~\cite{zhao2024sigmazero}, have further explored the possibility
of constructing attacks with localized alterations and limited perceptual
impact. This line of research is particularly significant for forensic
contexts, because it shows that effective image manipulation does not
necessarily require extensive or visually evident transformations: even
localized modifications can interfere with the behavior of automated models.

A further line of research has shown that adversarial perturbations may also
take localized and visually more explicit forms, as in the case of adversarial
patches. Brown et al.~\cite{brown2017patch} demonstrated that an artificial
region applied to an image can induce classification errors without modifying
the entire visual content. For the present thesis, this result is relevant
primarily at the conceptual level: it shows that classifier vulnerability is
not limited to distributed and nearly imperceptible perturbations, but extends
to localized alterations that can interfere with the model's decision-making
process.

The literature also includes generative attacks, in which generative networks
are trained to produce transferable adversarial perturbations. A representative
example is AdvGAN, which employs a generator to construct adversarial examples
efficiently and with transferability across models~\cite{xia2018advgan}. This
family of attacks is not included in the experimental protocol of the present
thesis, as the work prioritizes perturbations that are more directly
interpretable in the operational context of \gls{df}.

A crucial aspect for operational scenarios is the distinction among white-box,
black-box, and transfer-based attacks. Papernot et
al.~\cite{papernot2016limitations} showed that model vulnerability can transfer
across different classifiers, while subsequent literature on black-box attacks
has systematized scenarios in which the attacker has only limited access to
model outputs or uses surrogate models to generate transferable
perturbations~\cite{bhambri2020survey}. Carlini and
Wagner~\cite{carlini2017towards} contributed to strengthening the security
evaluation of classifiers by demonstrating the limitations of several defenses
proposed against adversarial attacks. The surveys by Akhtar and
Mian~\cite{akhtar2018threat} and Yuan et al.~\cite{yuan2019adversarial}
confirm that the transferability of adversarial examples is one of the most
significant problems for the use of \gls{ai}-based models in environments that
are not fully controlled. In the case of commercial software evaluated in
forensic contexts, this observation is particularly important: even when the
model integrated into the software is proprietary and non-inspectable, an
attacker could generate perturbations on accessible models and verify their
effectiveness through transfer.

Biggio and Roli~\cite{biggio2018wild} situated these vulnerabilities within the
paradigm of \textit{adversary-aware security}, arguing that the evaluation of a
machine-learning-based system must explicitly account for the presence of a
strategic adversary. This principle is central to the present thesis: a
forensic classification system does not necessarily operate in a neutral
environment, but may be exposed to data prepared or modified by actors
interested in hindering the analysis. Adversarial robustness is therefore not
only a technical property of the model, but a component of its operational
reliability.

In the domain closer to \textit{image forensics}, Barni et
al.~\cite{barni2018cf} showed that neural-network-based models for detecting
digital manipulations may be vulnerable to adversarial attacks.
Nowroozi~\cite{nowroozi2020phd} further investigated the problem of applying
\gls{ml} techniques to \textit{image forensics} in adversarial scenarios,
highlighting how models designed for forensic analysis can be degraded by
targeted perturbations. These contributions are particularly important because
they shift the problem from generic computer vision examples to systems used
for tasks closer to the forensic domain. The emerging finding is that
adversarial vulnerability does not affect only generic classifiers trained on
standard datasets, but also models developed to recognize manipulations or
relevant characteristics in digital images.

The connection with \gls{ai}-based software applicable to forensic contexts has
been reinforced by more recent work on operational robustness. Nowroozi et
al.~\cite{nowroozi2024verifying} showed, in a different security context, that
robustness verification with respect to adversarial perturbations must be an
integral part of the evaluation of automated systems. Sanna et
al.~\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect} brought this perspective
more directly into the forensic analysis domain, showing that \gls{ai}-based
functionalities integrated into operational software may exhibit fragility when
exposed to manipulated content. Although these studies do not cover the full
spectrum of adversarial attacks on images, they indicate clearly that the
validation of \gls{ai}-based software applicable to forensic contexts cannot be
limited to its effectiveness on ordinary inputs.

A further line of literature has analyzed the use of input transformations as a
possible defense against adversarial examples. Techniques such as
recompression, resizing, or image transformations may reduce the effectiveness
of some perturbations, but do not eliminate the need for systematic robustness
evaluation~\cite{guo2017countering}. This aspect is relevant to the present
thesis because some transformations considered in the literature as defense
mechanisms may take on a different meaning in a forensic context: they are not
employed to protect the model, but to simulate realistic anti-forensic stress
conditions and evaluate the operational stability of classifiers and commercial
tools.

Overall, the literature on \gls{advml} applied to images yields three
indications relevant to this thesis. First, visual classifiers may be
vulnerable to intentional perturbations even when they achieve high performance
on clean data. Second, this vulnerability may manifest across scenarios that
differ in attacker knowledge, perturbation magnitude, and transferability
across models. Third, works specifically addressing \textit{image forensics}
show that even systems designed for forensic tasks are not immune to these
fragilities. However, an important gap remains: most studies consider academic
models or controlled benchmarks, whereas integrated evaluations combining
visual classifiers, \gls{ai}-based software observed in a black-box setting,
realistic datasets, and adversarial perturbations within the same experimental
protocol remain limited.

This gap motivates the transition to the following section. Adversarial
perturbations represent manipulations designed with respect to model behavior;
in forensic contexts, however, images may also be altered through more ordinary
or anti-forensic transformations, such as recompression, resizing, blurring,
and tonal modifications. Such transformations do not necessarily require
knowledge of the classifier, but may nonetheless affect the stability of
automated analysis.

\section{Anti-Forensics and Realistic Transformations: Impact on Automated Classification}
\label{sec:sota-antiforensics}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Building on the conceptual distinction introduced in
Chapter~\ref{chap:background} between adversarial perturbations oriented toward
the model's decision and anti-forensic transformations applied to the digital
artifact, this section examines contributions that have studied the impact of
realistic image manipulations on forensic analysis. The point relevant to the
present thesis is that transformations not optimized with respect to the
classifier may nonetheless affect input quality and the stability of the
automated outputs used in visual triage.

Yaacoub et al.~\cite{yaacoub2021digital} and the taxonomic literature on
anti-forensics~\cite{hewling2018anti} frame anti-digital forensics as the set
of techniques aimed at making the identification, acquisition, preservation, or
interpretation of digital evidence more difficult. In the image domain, this
perspective encompasses both manipulations aimed at deleting or masking forensic
traces and apparently ordinary transformations that may alter the
characteristics of the file. The contribution of this literature is significant
because it shifts attention from the authenticity of the image alone to its
reliability as an input for analysis: an image may remain semantically
interpretable to a human observer while becoming more problematic for an
automated classification system.

A particularly relevant line of work concerns \gls{jpeg} recompression. Stamm
and Liu~\cite{stamm2010anti} showed that anti-forensic operations applied to
compression can alter or mask traces useful for image analysis, reducing the
ability to identify prior manipulations. More generally, the literature on
\textit{image forensics} has shown that compression modifies the statistical
and frequency structure of the image, affecting details, textures, and
artifacts that can be exploited by both forensic analysts and automated
models~\cite{farid2016photo,stamm2013information}. This observation is directly
relevant to automated classification: if a model learns features that are
sensitive to compression quality, subsequent recompression may affect
prediction stability even without visibly altering the semantic content of the
image.

A second group of works concerns resizing, resampling, and geometric
transformations. Popescu and Farid~\cite{popescu2005exposing} showed that
resampling leaves detectable statistical traces, establishing a central
research direction in the analysis of digital manipulations. From the
perspective of automated classification, however, the problem concerns not only
the possibility of detecting resampling, but also its effect on the
representations learned by models. Successive resizing operations,
interpolations, cropping, or changes in aspect ratio may modify proportions,
edges, and visual details, with particularly significant consequences in tasks
where the object of interest occupies only a limited portion of the image. In
forensic scenarios, this effect may influence the system's ability to recognize
objects of investigative relevance, especially when they are partially
occluded, small, or embedded in visually complex contexts.

The literature on realistic image transformations has also considered
alterations such as blur, noise, chromatic variations, histogram modifications,
and contrast stretching. These transformations are not necessarily
anti-forensic in the strict sense: they may result from low-quality
acquisition, forwarding through messaging platforms, uploading to social
networks, repeated saving, or ordinary editing processes. However, their
relevance to classifier robustness has been highlighted by benchmarks on common
corruptions, in which degradations of this type produce significant performance
reductions even in models with high accuracy on clean
data~\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking}. In forensic contexts, this evidence
carries operational significance: realistic transformations do not represent
marginal cases, but frequent conditions in the digital material actually
submitted to analysis.

Information hiding techniques, such as steganography, remain relevant in the
forensic domain, but are treated in this thesis as part of the general
theoretical framework rather than as the primary object of the experimental
evaluation. The empirical focus is instead placed on transformations that
directly affect automated visual classification, such as recompression,
resampling, blurring, and tonal modifications.

Recent studies on \gls{ai}-based software applicable to forensic contexts
confirm that such transformations must also be considered in operational
validation. Sanna et al.~\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}
show that manipulated content, or content that does not conform to ordinary
acquisition conditions, may reduce the reliability of the automated outputs
produced by the analyzed software. A key observation is that anti-forensic and
realistic transformations do not necessarily require a sophisticated attack
against the model: they can be applied with common tools, introduced by sharing
platforms, or result from ordinary image processing steps. This makes them
particularly plausible in real investigative scenarios.

Overall, the anti-forensic literature and the literature on robustness to
realistic transformations converge on a single point: the digital image should
not be regarded as a stable and neutral input. It may undergo alterations that
modify forensic traces, perceptual quality, and the features exploited by
automated classifiers. However, most existing works analyze manipulation
detection, model robustness, or tool validation separately, without integrating
these aspects into a unified protocol oriented toward \gls{df}. Accordingly,
the need remains to evaluate how realistic and anti-forensic transformations
affect the automated classification of content of investigative relevance and,
more generally, the quality of the support provided by \gls{ai}-based systems
to the analyst.

This need motivates the transition to the following section, devoted to
explainability. If adversarial perturbations and anti-forensic transformations
can modify model behavior, tools that allow at least partial analysis of which
image regions or visual characteristics contribute to the automated decision
become necessary. \gls{xai} techniques do not resolve the robustness problem,
but they can provide critical support for interpreting and diagnosing the
fragilities of \gls{ai}-based systems.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Explainability Applied to the Reliability Evaluation of AI-based Systems}
\label{sec:sota-xai}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The growing adoption of \gls{ai}-based systems in high-stakes domains has made
the explainability of algorithmic decisions a central concern. In the context of
\gls{df}, this need assumes specific relevance: the output of an automated
model may guide triage, influence the priority assigned to specific content,
and contribute to the selection of items to be submitted to human review. For
this reason, the literature on \gls{xai} is relevant not because it renders
models automatically reliable, but because it provides tools for critically
analyzing their behavior, particularly when predictions change in the presence
of degraded, manipulated, or out-of-distribution inputs.

The surveys by Adadi and Berrada~\cite{Adadi2018}, Guidotti et
al.~\cite{Guidotti2019}, Barredo Arrieta et al.~\cite{Barredo2018}, and
Doshi-Velez and Kim~\cite{Doshi-Velez2017} have helped clarify that model
interpretability is not a unitary concept, but depends on the intended
recipient of the explanation, the type of model, the required level of
granularity, and the purpose of the evaluation. This distinction is
particularly important in forensic contexts. An explanation intended for a
researcher evaluating a classifier is not equivalent to one intended for an
operational analyst or for a party required to verify the technical correctness
of a procedure. It follows that, in the forensic context, \gls{xai} should be
understood as support for verification and diagnosis, rather than as a
substitute for experimental
validation~\cite{Adadi2018,Doshi-Velez2017,sanna2025cybercrime}.

In the domain of image classification, foundational works on local explanations
and visual attributions have provided methodologically distinct tools: LIME
operates through local model-agnostic approximations, Integrated Gradients uses
the gradient of the model with respect to the input, and Class Activation
Mapping techniques highlight regions associated with the prediction in specific
visual architectures~\cite{Ribeiro2016,Sundararajan2017,Zhou2016}. The
limitation, with respect to the problem addressed in the present thesis, is
that these contributions were not designed to evaluate the operational
robustness of proprietary \gls{ai}-based software in forensic contexts, nor to
establish whether an explanation remains diagnostically useful in the presence
of manipulated inputs. For this reason, the explainability analysis included in
the experimental protocol is applied to controllable proxy models and used as
qualitative support for interpreting failure cases, rather than as a primary
metric or as an equivalent explanation of the software evaluated in a
black-box setting.

In the case of forensic image classification, this type of analysis is useful
primarily for verifying whether, after a manipulation, local attributions remain
associated with the object of investigative relevance or instead shift toward
secondary regions, textures, backgrounds, or artifacts introduced by the
transformation. This diagnostic use does not assign autonomous evidentiary
value to the visual explanation, but allows the quantitative degradation of
performance to be connected to observable changes in classifier behavior.

The literature has nonetheless highlighted important limitations of visual
explanations. Carvalho et al.~\cite{Carvalho2019} emphasize that model
interpretability depends strongly on the method used, and that different
explanation methods may produce results that do not fully overlap. Moreover,
many attribution techniques are sensitive to parameter choices, baselines, local
noise, or preprocessing decisions. This implies that an apparently coherent
saliency map does not constitute autonomous proof of the model's correctness.
In forensic contexts, this caution is essential: a visual explanation may
support the analyst in understanding system behavior, but it cannot replace
manual verification of the evidence or quantitative validation of the
classifier.

A further limitation concerns the applicability of \gls{xai} to commercial
software evaluated in forensic contexts. Many attribution methods require
access to the model, to gradients, or at least to the possibility of querying
the classifier repeatedly. In proprietary software, these conditions are not
always satisfied. Consequently, while \gls{xai} can be applied in a relatively
controlled manner to academic or open-source models, its use on commercial
\gls{ai}-based software is more complex and sometimes limited to indirect forms
of analysis. This aspect reinforces the issue already discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:sota-ai-tools}: the opacity of proprietary modules hinders not
only the reproducibility of classifications, but also their technical
explainability.

In the specific context of \gls{df}, the literature that systematically applies
\gls{xai} techniques to the robustness evaluation of forensic classifiers
remains limited. Works on \gls{xai} provide general tools for interpreting
\gls{dl} models, while studies on adversarial robustness and anti-forensics
focus primarily on performance metrics. A fully developed convergence among
forensic validation, robustness evaluation, and explainability is therefore
still lacking. This gap is significant because, in an investigative scenario,
it is not sufficient to know that a model fails after a manipulation: it is
also useful to understand whether the error is associated with shifts in local
attributions toward irrelevant regions, with artifacts introduced by the
transformation, or with a structural fragility of the learned representation.

In summary, \gls{xai} contributes to the reliability evaluation of
\gls{ai}-based systems in three ways. First, it enables qualitative analysis of
which image regions influence the prediction. Second, it allows the behavior of
the model on clean and manipulated images to be compared, highlighting possible
shifts in the regions considered relevant. Third, it supports the diagnosis of
model fragilities, while providing no autonomous guarantees of correctness. For
these reasons, explainability constitutes a complement to the quantitative
evaluation of robustness, not a substitute for it.

The following section concludes the state-of-the-art review by highlighting the
overall gap that emerges from the research directions analyzed. The literature
has produced significant contributions on forensic automation, \gls{ai}-based
software applicable to forensic contexts, visual classification, robustness,
adversarial attacks, anti-forensic techniques, and explainability; however,
these areas have been addressed predominantly in isolation. The present thesis
is positioned precisely within the space left open by this fragmentation.

\section{Literature Gap and Thesis Positioning}
\label{sec:sota-gap}

The review conducted in the previous sections shows that the literature has
produced significant contributions on each of the axes relevant to this thesis,
but has addressed them largely in isolation. Works on automation in \gls{df}
have highlighted the need for tools capable of managing large volumes of digital
data and supporting the analyst in the triage, examination, and analysis
phases~\cite{garfinkel2010digital,costantini2019digital,faqir2023digital}.
Studies on AI-based software applicable to forensic contexts have begun to
document concrete problems of opacity, independent validation, and fragility in
the presence of manipulated content~\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.
The literature on automated image classification has shown the effectiveness of
computer vision models for recognizing objects and relevant content, but often
in controlled scenarios and not specifically designed for forensic
validation~\cite{Hao2018DeepFirearm,he2016deep,tan2019efficientnet,radford2021learning,li2022blip}.
In parallel, works on robustness have demonstrated that accuracy on clean data
is not sufficient to assess the operational reliability of a
classifier~\cite{hendrycks2019benchmarking,hendrycks2017baseline,fort2021exploring,croce2020robustbench}.

A second body of work addresses the threats that may alter the behavior of
automated systems. The literature on \gls{advml} has shown that intentionally
constructed perturbations can induce errors even in high-performance
models~\cite{szegedy2014intriguing,goodfellow2015explaining,papernot2016limitations,carlini2017towards,biggio2018wild}.
Works more closely related to \textit{image forensics} have further highlighted
that even models designed for forensic tasks may be vulnerable in adversarial
scenarios~\cite{barni2018cf,nowroozi2020phd}. In parallel, the anti-forensic
literature has studied transformations applied directly to the digital
artifact, such as recompression, resampling, statistical alterations, and
changes in visual quality, showing that these operations may affect both
forensic traces and the digital representation of the
image~\cite{stamm2010anti,stamm2013information,farid2016photo,popescu2005exposing}.
Finally, the literature on \gls{xai} has provided useful tools for qualitatively
analyzing model behavior, but these techniques have rarely been integrated into
complete forensic protocols for robustness evaluation~\cite{Adadi2018,Doshi-Velez2017,Ribeiro2016,Sundararajan2017,Zhou2016}.

The specific gap identified by the present thesis therefore concerns the limited
availability of integrated experimental protocols that combine, within the same
evaluation design, automated image classification or semantic image retrieval,
\textit{human-in-the-loop} dataset validation, heterogeneous proxy models,
AI-based software selected for operational evaluation in a forensic context,
adversarial perturbations, anti-forensic transformations, quantitative metrics,
and explainability-based analysis of model behavior. The relevance of this
integration derives from the fact that, in a forensic context, the reliability
of an automated system cannot be evaluated only as an isolated property of the
classifier. It also depends on the quality and representativeness of the data,
the presence of borderline or out-of-distribution cases, the nature of the
manipulations applied, the transparency of the software used, and the effects
that potential errors may have on triage and evidence selection.

The present thesis is positioned precisely within this gap. Its contribution
consists of conducting an experimental evaluation of the operational robustness
of proxy models and AI-based software employed, in the context of \gls{df}, for
the automated classification or semantic retrieval of images of potential
investigative interest, jointly considering the algorithmic and
operational-investigative dimensions. The objective is not only to measure
performance degradation under adverse conditions, but also to understand how
such degradation may affect the system's ability to support the analyst in the
selection of relevant content. From this perspective, robustness is treated not
as an abstract attribute of the model, but as a functional requirement for the
controlled use of \gls{ai} in \gls{df} workflows.

The positioning of the thesis is therefore twofold. On the one hand, it connects
to the literature on \gls{advml}, robustness evaluation, and \gls{xai}, on the
premise that classification systems must also be evaluated in the presence of
hostile, degraded, or anomalous inputs. On the other hand, it is situated within
the domain of \gls{df}, where the quality of automation must be assessed with
respect to its operational usefulness, the verifiability of the results, and
the capacity to maintain human control over the analysis process. This framing
makes it possible to move beyond a purely performance-based evaluation and to
address robustness as a methodological, technical, and forensic issue.
This research space motivates the \gls{fairlab} experimental protocol adopted
in the present thesis and described in the following chapter.

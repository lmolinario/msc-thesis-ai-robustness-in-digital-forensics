% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\chapter{Methodology}
\label{chap:methodology}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This chapter defines the methodology adopted to evaluate the operational
robustness of \gls{ai}-based tools for automated image classification in
forensic contexts. It addresses the gap identified in
Chapter~\ref{chap:state-of-the-art} by formalizing the \gls{fairlab} framework
as a reproducible and auditable protocol that combines controlled experimental
conditions with operationally relevant forensic scenarios.

The chapter presents the overall framework and research assumptions, followed
by the procedures for dataset construction, human-in-the-loop validation,
dataset freezing and partitioning, proxy-model and black-box software
evaluation, perturbation testing, metric computation, explainability, and
traceability. Implementation-specific data structures, scripts, architectures,
parameters, software versions, and configurations are documented in
Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}; quantitative outcomes and their
interpretation are reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Overview of the FAIRLab Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology-fairlab-overview}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The \gls{fairlab} protocol provides a structured framework for evaluating
automated image-classification systems under clean, \gls{ood}, adversarial,
and anti-forensic conditions. Its scope extends beyond clean classification
accuracy because automated outputs may affect evidence prioritization and the
allocation of human analytical resources. Operational robustness is therefore
examined in relation to missed relevant content, additional review workload,
and unstable behavior on inputs that differ from the expected operational
distribution.

The protocol integrates two complementary evaluation levels. The first uses
fully inspectable proxy classification models as controlled experimental
references; these models are not treated as replicas or functionally equivalent
substitutes for proprietary systems. The second evaluates selected
\gls{ai}-based software systems under black-box or near-black-box access
conditions, using only their observable inputs and outputs and making no
assumptions about inaccessible architectures, training data, calibration
procedures, or decision logic
\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

Both levels rely on the same frozen experimental corpus. Its binary in-domain
component supports clean and perturbed evaluation, whereas a separate clean
\gls{ood} branch is evaluated outside model training and perturbation
generation, as detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-split-generation}. The clean binary samples,
clean \gls{ood} inputs, adversarial artifacts, and anti-forensic artifacts are
combined into the forensic evaluation bundle, enabling comparison under aligned
experimental conditions.

Quantitative metrics characterize classification performance, robustness
degradation, error direction, and \gls{ood} behavior. Explainability provides
qualitative diagnostic information for selected cases
involving an inspectable proxy model and is not extended to the selected
black-box systems.
Traceability controls preserve the relationships among source samples, derived
artifacts, experimental conditions, observable outputs, and reported results.

The protocol specifies the methodological requirements and information that
must be preserved without prescribing particular model architectures, software
products, file formats, directory structures, databases, hashing algorithms,
or explainability techniques. These choices belong to the concrete
implementation documented in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.
Figure~\ref{fig:methodology-fairlab-overview} summarizes the resulting
workflow.

\section{Research Design and Experimental Assumptions}
\label{sec:methodology-research-design}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The experimental design adopts forensic visual triage as its reference
scenario, in which automated classification supports the preliminary
identification and prioritization of potentially relevant images. The case
study defines a controlled firearm-related classification task rather than
attempting to cover the entire domain of weapon detection. This restricted
scope provides a clear target category while retaining sufficient visual
variability for robustness assessment. The adopted designations are operational
categories established for the experiment and must not be interpreted as legal
qualifications or universal semantic classes.

The binary task distinguishes between \texttt{weapon}, defined as the positive
class, and \texttt{non\_weapon}, defined as the in-domain negative class. A
separate \gls{ood} branch is reserved for distributional evaluation and is not
modeled as a third supervised class. The assignment criteria were formalized
before dataset freezing and govern human-in-the-loop validation, sample
selection, and the documentation of inclusion and exclusion decisions.
Table~\ref{tab:methodology-operational-classes} summarizes these operational
definitions.

The \texttt{exclude} designation is used exclusively as a quality-control
disposition during human review and does not constitute an additional
analytical branch. Likewise, the \gls{ood} branch is not a homogeneous semantic
class or a subclass of \texttt{non\_weapon}. It contains borderline,
anomalous, degraded, synthetic, or otherwise distributionally different inputs
that do not constitute clean instances of either binary class. Since
classifiers may produce high-confidence predictions outside their training
distribution \cite{hendrycks2017baseline,fort2021exploring}, these inputs must
be assessed separately from ordinary binary errors. Their exclusion from model
training and perturbation generation is formalized in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-split-generation}.

These operational definitions provide the semantic basis for the dataset
construction, review-documentation, and freezing procedures described in
Sections~\ref{sec:methodology-dataset-construction},
\ref{sec:methodology-review-manifest}, and
\ref{sec:methodology-human-review}. They ensure that final assignments are
derived from explicit and traceable review criteria rather than inherited
directly from source labels, filenames, or directory structures.

\section{Dataset Construction and Source Consolidation}
\label{sec:methodology-dataset-construction}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Dataset construction separates source collection, technical validation,
semantic review, and final dataset freezing. This distinction is
methodologically necessary because the technical validity of an image file
does not establish its semantic suitability for the forensic classification
task.

The process begins with the consolidation of heterogeneous image sources into
a candidate pool. The objective is not to create a visually homogeneous
collection, but to capture part of the variability encountered in contemporary
digital investigations. Depending on the investigated domain and data
availability, the candidate pool may include public datasets, specialized
repositories, open-web content, \gls{osint}-oriented collections, and material
obtained from less structured or non-indexed online environments.

The composition and relative contribution of these sources may vary, but the
provenance of each candidate sample must remain identifiable throughout the
preparation and review process.
Table~\ref{tab:methodology-raw-sources} summarizes the methodological roles of
the source categories considered in the protocol.

After source consolidation, candidate images undergo technical validation
before any semantic assignment is made. The validation process verifies that
each file can be processed reliably and may include readability, structural
validity, dimensional suitability, duplicate detection, and stable sample
identification. The resulting records must preserve sufficient information to
associate each retained sample with its provenance, technical-validation
outcome, and integrity information.

Source location, directory structure, filename, and pre-existing annotations
may be retained as contextual or audit information, but they must not be
treated as definitive reference assignments. Final semantic membership must
instead be determined through the operational criteria defined in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-research-design}. This separation limits the
propagation of source-specific assumptions and labeling errors into the frozen
experimental dataset.

The relative size of the original source collections must also be considered
during sample selection. A large or highly asymmetric source must not
automatically dominate the final corpus. Composition decisions should instead
reflect the experimental objectives, the required class structure, and the
need to preserve meaningful source diversity.

Following technical validation, the candidate pool is submitted to the
documented semantic-review process described in
Sections~\ref{sec:methodology-review-manifest} and
\ref{sec:methodology-human-review}. Figure~\ref{fig:methodology-dataset-source-consolidation}
summarizes the transition from heterogeneous sources to the frozen
experimental dataset.

\section{Review Documentation and Semantic Decision Records}
\label{sec:methodology-review-manifest}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Following technical validation, each candidate sample is associated with a
structured review record that connects its technical preparation to the
subsequent human-in-the-loop semantic assessment. The record must preserve two
distinct categories of information: sample identity, provenance, technical
validity, and integrity information; and the semantic decision produced during
manual review, including review status, operational assignment, exclusion
disposition, explanatory notes, and any information required to document
ambiguous or \gls{ood} cases.

Regardless of its technical representation, the review record must maintain a
stable association among the candidate sample, its provenance, the documented
semantic decision, and any experimental artifact subsequently derived from it.
This association enables the decision process and the transition from the
candidate pool to the frozen experimental dataset to be reconstructed.

Labels inherited from source material or generated through automated
preprocessing may be retained as contextual information, but they must not
replace the explicit semantic judgment produced during human-in-the-loop
review. Final operational assignments are determined exclusively according to
the criteria defined in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-research-design}.

The separation between technical-validation information and semantic decision
records preserves the distinction between file suitability and relevance to
the experimental task. The resulting documentation supports the consistent
application of the assignment criteria, preserves the rationale for inclusion,
exclusion, and class-assignment decisions, and provides the traceability
required for the review and dataset-freezing procedure described in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-human-review}.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Human-in-the-Loop Review and Final Dataset Freezing}
\label{sec:methodology-human-review}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The technically validated candidate pool is subjected to a documented
\textit{human-in-the-loop} review to determine the semantic suitability of each
sample and assign the corresponding operational designation. The criteria
defined in Section~\ref{sec:methodology-research-design} must be applied
consistently and independently of source directories, filenames, inherited
annotations, or automated pre-labels.

The review follows a conservative decision rule. Samples are included in the
binary task only when they can be assigned to either \texttt{weapon} or
\texttt{non\_weapon} with sufficient semantic clarity. Borderline, anomalous,
synthetic, replica-like, degraded, or otherwise distributionally different
content is retained within the separate \gls{ood} branch rather than being
forced into either binary class. Unusable or semantically irrelevant samples
are assigned to the review-only \texttt{exclude} disposition.

These assignments are operational designations defined for the experimental
protocol and must not be interpreted as definitive legal, material, or
evidentiary determinations regarding the depicted content. In particular,
assignment to the \gls{ood} branch represents a methodological decision for
cases that do not constitute clear instances of either binary class.

Each review decision must remain reconstructable through the semantic decision
records defined in Section~\ref{sec:methodology-review-manifest}. These records
preserve the association among the reviewed sample, its provenance, the
assigned disposition, any explanatory notes, and the experimental artifacts
subsequently derived from it.

After all candidate samples have been reviewed, the selected corpus is frozen.
Dataset freezing establishes a stable reference corpus whose composition
remains unchanged throughout the subsequent evaluation phases. Any correction
or modification introduced after freezing must be explicitly documented and
treated as a new version of the experimental dataset.

The frozen dataset provides the common source corpus for the controlled
proxy-model evaluation and the black-box evaluation of the selected
\gls{ai}-based software systems. Its subsequent separation into the binary
evaluation subset and the clean \gls{ood} branch is defined in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-split-generation}.

The number of reviewers and the review workflow may vary across
implementations. When the protocol is conducted by a single reviewer, the
absence of inter-annotator agreement must be acknowledged as a limitation.
Explicit assignment criteria, structured decision records, and dataset
freezing support procedural auditability and reproducibility, but do not
eliminate the subjectivity inherent in semantic assessment. Dataset freezing
therefore acts as a methodological control that distinguishes changes in
systems, perturbations, and metrics from undocumented changes in the evaluated
corpus.











% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Data Partitioning and OOD Evaluation Strategy}
\label{sec:methodology-split-generation}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

After dataset freezing, the experimental corpus is divided into two distinct
branches: an in-domain binary subset and a separate clean \gls{ood} evaluation
set. The binary subset supports proxy-model training, clean evaluation, and
robustness testing, whereas the \gls{ood} branch examines system behavior on
borderline, semantically atypical, or distributionally different inputs.

The binary subset is organized into mutually exclusive experimental
partitions, hereafter referred to as folds. Their number and size may vary
according to the available dataset, class distribution, and experimental
requirements. Regardless of the adopted configuration, each sample must belong
to only one fold, class distributions should remain sufficiently consistent
whenever stratification is feasible, sample identity and provenance must remain
traceable, and the allocation procedure must be deterministic or documented in
sufficient detail to permit regeneration.

Within this protocol, folds are not introduced primarily as a conventional
cross-validation mechanism for hyperparameter selection or general performance
estimation. Instead, they define controlled experimental holdout units. For
each target fold, the corresponding proxy-model instance is trained on the
remaining folds and evaluated on the held-out samples, thereby preventing
overlap between model fitting and clean or robustness evaluation.

The fold-aware organization also preserves the relationship among each
held-out sample, the corresponding proxy-model checkpoint, and any
model-dependent adversarial artifact generated from that sample. Such an
artifact must be produced using a checkpoint that was not trained on its source
sample. Model-agnostic adversarial-style conditions and anti-forensic
transformations remain associated with the originating sample and its fold but
do not depend on a specific checkpoint.

The clean \gls{ood} set remains outside the binary folds, is not used for
proxy-model training, and is not treated as a third supervised class. No
experimental adversarial perturbation or anti-forensic transformation is
applied to these samples. This separation allows the study to examine how
automated systems assign inputs that do not constitute ordinary
\texttt{weapon} or \texttt{non\_weapon} instances without conflating
distributional mismatch with deliberate or incidental input manipulation.

The partitioning records must associate each sample with its evaluation branch
and, where applicable, its fold and corresponding proxy-model checkpoint. They
must also preserve the relationship between each original image and any
derived experimental artifact, consistently with the traceability requirements
defined in Section~\ref{sec:methodology-traceability}.

\section{Proxy-Model Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:methodology-models-classification}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The controlled component of the methodology relies on fully inspectable
classification models, hereafter referred to as \textit{proxy classification
models}. These models provide experimental references for analyzing
classification behavior, robustness degradation, confidence patterns, error
direction, and transferability under reproducible conditions. They are not
replicas, functionally equivalent substitutes, reverse-engineered
reconstructions, or architectural reproductions of the selected black-box
software systems.

The proxy-model framework serves three methodological objectives. First, it
provides a transparent reference for measuring classification performance and
robustness degradation. Second, it enables the generation of model-dependent
adversarial examples under explicit white-box or score-based access
assumptions. Third, it supports the evaluation of whether perturbations
generated against one controlled model transfer to other proxy architectures
or to the selected black-box software systems.

The proxy models implement the binary classification task defined in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-research-design}. The mapping between
\texttt{weapon}, \texttt{non\_weapon}, and their numerical encodings must
remain explicit and consistent throughout training, evaluation, and metric
computation. The clean \gls{ood} branch is excluded from training and is not
treated as a third supervised class, as established in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-split-generation}.

The methodology does not prescribe specific model architectures. Instead, the
controlled evaluation should include models with complementary algorithmic
characteristics, such as conventional convolutional architectures,
efficiency-oriented architectures, or models based on large-scale pretrained
visual representations. The architecture, initialization strategy,
preprocessing procedure, class mapping, optimization method, training
configuration, and resulting checkpoints must be documented. The concrete
architectures and configurations adopted in this thesis are reported in
Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

Proxy-model training follows the fold-aware strategy defined in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-split-generation}. For each target fold, the
corresponding model instance is trained exclusively on the remaining folds and
evaluated on the held-out samples. Training conditions must remain consistent
across folds for a given model configuration, and any variation in
architecture, preprocessing, initialization, or optimization must be
documented.

The same separation applies to model-dependent perturbation generation. An
adversarial artifact associated with a held-out sample must be generated using
a fold-specific checkpoint that was not trained on that sample. This
requirement prevents information leakage between model fitting, adversarial
generation, and robustness evaluation.

For model-dependent adversarial attacks, one controlled model is designated as
the \textit{primary proxy target}. This model provides the source system against
which adversarial examples are generated under the access assumptions required
by each attack. White-box attacks may use gradients, decision-boundary
information, or other internal properties, whereas score-based attacks rely on
observable model scores without requiring direct gradient access. Selection of
the primary proxy target is an implementation decision and does not imply that
the chosen architecture is universally more accurate, representative, or
robust than the alternatives.

Using a single documented primary target limits unnecessary multiplication of
experimental variants and produces a coherent adversarial test set. The
resulting model-dependent artifacts are evaluated under three conditions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{direct source-model evaluation}, in which adversarial artifacts
are assessed against the fold-specific proxy-model checkpoint used to generate
them under the corresponding access assumptions;

\item \textit{cross-model transfer evaluation}, in which the same artifacts
are assessed against different proxy architectures;

\item \textit{black-box transfer evaluation}, in which the same artifacts are
submitted to the selected black-box software systems.
\end{enumerate}

Model-agnostic adversarial-style conditions and anti-forensic transformations
are generated independently of a designated source model. Their evaluation
across proxy architectures and black-box systems therefore measures response
stability under shared manipulated conditions rather than transferability from
a source model. The corresponding access assumptions and operational rationale
are detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-perturbation-selection-rationale}.

The proxy-model and black-box evaluation levels must remain analytically
distinct. Proxy models are inspectable and configurable components whose
training conditions, checkpoints, and internal behavior are available for
controlled analysis. The selected software systems are evaluated only through
their observable inputs and outputs. Exposing both levels to the same clean
samples, clean \gls{ood} inputs, adversarial artifacts, and anti-forensic
artifacts enables comparative assessment without assuming architectural or
functional equivalence.

\section{Perturbation Selection Rationale and Operational Relevance}
\label{sec:methodology-perturbation-selection-rationale}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The perturbation framework evaluates classification behavior under controlled
input modifications selected according to their access assumptions,
computational cost, perceptual impact, reproducibility, and operational
plausibility. These criteria are particularly relevant to forensic visual
triage, where automated systems may process large image collections under
operational time constraints. Computationally intensive perturbations remain
useful for controlled vulnerability analysis, but may be less representative
of manipulations that can be applied rapidly and at scale.

The methodology distinguishes among three categories of input modification:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{model-dependent adversarial attacks}, which are generated
against a designated source model under explicit white-box or score-based
access assumptions;

\item \textit{model-agnostic adversarial-style conditions}, which modify an
image according to predefined rules without being optimized against a specific
classifier;

\item \textit{anti-forensic transformations}, which emulate plausible
modifications of the image artifact or its visual representation.
\end{enumerate}

Model-dependent attacks provide controlled tests of classifier vulnerability
and may support direct and transferability analyses. Although they do not
necessarily represent the most probable manipulations in ordinary forensic
scenarios, they demonstrate how classification behavior may change when an
actor exploits information obtained from, or interaction with, a designated
source model.

Model-agnostic adversarial-style conditions require neither access to internal
model properties nor interaction with observable scores. Their application
across proxy architectures and black-box software systems therefore supports
comparison under a shared manipulated condition rather than transferability
analysis from a designated source model.

Anti-forensic transformations emulate image-processing operations that may
occur deliberately or incidentally before forensic acquisition or analysis.
They generally require no access to the evaluated classifier and may support
efficient batch processing, making them relevant to both deliberate
manipulation and ordinary image-handling workflows.

The perturbation set should provide complementary experimental coverage rather
than multiple near-duplicate variants of the same mechanism. Selection should
therefore consider:

\begin{itemize}
\item diversity of access assumptions;
\item diversity of algorithmic mechanisms;
\item computational feasibility and scalability;
\item reproducibility across experimental runs;
\item preservation of sufficient task-relevant semantic content;
\item operational plausibility;
\item suitability for cross-system comparison and, where applicable,
transferability analysis;
\item compatibility with the evaluated image formats and software systems.
\end{itemize}

\section{Adversarial Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology-adversarial-attacks}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The adversarial component of the \gls{fairlab} protocol evaluates whether
controlled input modifications can alter the behavior of automated image
classifiers while preserving the relationship between each generated artifact
and its corresponding clean sample. Adversarial artifacts are generated
exclusively from the binary in-domain subset; the clean \gls{ood} branch
remains excluded because it addresses distributional mismatch rather than
robustness to deliberate input manipulation, as established in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-split-generation}.

Consistently with
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-perturbation-selection-rationale}, the methodology
distinguishes model-dependent attacks from model-agnostic adversarial-style
conditions. Model-dependent attacks are generated against a designated source
model under explicit access assumptions that may include gradients, internal
model properties, decision-boundary information, or observable output scores.
Model-agnostic conditions instead apply predefined modifications without
querying or optimizing against a specific classifier.

The selected model-dependent attacks should provide complementary coverage of
different adversarial mechanisms, including single-step gradient
perturbations, sparse or localized score-based modifications, iterative
optimization procedures, and decision-boundary-based attacks
\cite{goodfellow2015explaining,su2019onepixel,zhao2024sigmazero,moosavi2016deepfool}.
The protocol does not aim to provide an exhaustive adversarial-robustness
benchmark or a comprehensive comparison of all available attack algorithms,
which would require extensive parameter sweeps and standardized attack suites
\cite{madry2018towards,croce2020autoattack}. Instead, the attack set is selected
to be computationally feasible, reproducible, sufficiently heterogeneous, and
suitable for direct vulnerability and transferability analyses.

Model-dependent adversarial examples are generated against the primary proxy
target defined in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-models-classification}. For each held-out sample,
generation must use the corresponding fold-specific checkpoint trained without
that sample. This requirement preserves the separation between model fitting,
attack generation, and evaluation and prevents information leakage.

Each generated artifact must retain an explicit association with:

\begin{itemize}
\item the original clean sample, reference operational class, and experimental
fold;
\item the attack family or model-agnostic condition;
\item the source model, checkpoint, and access assumption, when applicable;
\item the generation parameters and resulting output artifact;
\item the information required to reconstruct the generation condition;
\item the information required to verify identity, integrity, and provenance.
\end{itemize}

The output representation should minimize changes unrelated to the selected
adversarial mechanism. Any additional compression, encoding, or representation
effect introduced during artifact generation must be documented and considered
when interpreting the resulting classification behavior.

Attack generation and robustness evaluation are treated as separate phases.
Generation metadata may record whether an attack changed the prediction of the
source model, but such an outcome does not by itself constitute a final
robustness result. The generated artifact must subsequently be evaluated
through the common metric and comparison framework.

Model-dependent artifacts are assessed through the direct source-model,
cross-model transfer, and black-box transfer conditions defined in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-models-classification}. The black-box condition
measures only observable transfer behavior and does not imply access to the
internal properties of the selected software systems.

Model-agnostic adversarial-style artifacts are evaluated across the proxy
models and selected black-box software systems under the same manipulated
condition. Because they are generated independently of a designated source
model, differences in system behavior are interpreted as response variation
under a shared condition rather than as adversarial transferability.

The specific attack algorithms, model configurations, parameter values, output
representations, and traceability artifacts adopted in this thesis are
documented in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

\section{Anti-Forensic Evaluation Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology-anti-forensic-transformations}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The anti-forensic component of the \gls{fairlab} protocol evaluates the
stability of automated image classification under controlled and operationally
plausible modifications of the image artifact. Unlike the model-dependent
attacks described in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-adversarial-attacks}, these transformations are
not optimized against the gradients, loss function, observable scores, or
decision boundary of a specific classifier. They are applied through
predefined image-processing rules and can therefore be evaluated consistently
across proxy models and selected black-box software systems.

The term \textit{anti-forensic} is used here in an experimental and operational
sense and does not necessarily imply malicious intent or deliberate evidence
concealment. It identifies transformations that may alter or destabilize
automated analysis by modifying the visual, statistical, spatial, or
encoding-related properties of an image. Such modifications may be introduced
deliberately or arise from ordinary processes such as repeated saving, format
conversion, platform-mediated recompression, resizing, forwarding, filtering,
enhancement, or normalization.

The methodology considers transformation families that affect complementary
properties of the image artifact: lossy re-encoding, spatial resampling and
resizing, blurring and local-detail degradation, tonal-distribution
modification, and contrast adjustment. The specific algorithms and parameter
values adopted in this thesis are documented in
Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

meaningful evaluation. The objective is not to make the images

that the intended artifacts were produced correctly, but do not demonstrate
robustness degradation. Their impact must be determined through subsequent
evaluation using the common metric and comparison framework.

The generated artifacts are evaluated on both the proxy classification models
and the selected black-box software systems. This common evaluation supports a
structured comparison between controlled model behavior and the observable
operational behavior of the selected software systems under the same
anti-forensic conditions.

\section{Forensic Evaluation Bundle and Blind-Input Design}
\label{sec:methodology-forensic-evaluation-bundle}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The operational evaluation of black-box software systems requires experimental
inputs to be transferred without exposing descriptors that reveal their
reference class, provenance, perturbation condition, or expected operational
assignment. For this purpose, the \gls{fairlab} protocol introduces a
\textit{forensic evaluation bundle}: a controlled and traceable collection of
clean binary samples, clean \gls{ood} inputs, adversarial artifacts, and
anti-forensic artifacts prepared for black-box evaluation.

Bundle construction does not alter the clean samples, generated artifacts, or
reference operational assignments. Its methodological purpose is to separate
the files submitted to the evaluated software systems from the information
required for subsequent verification, normalization, and analysis.

The bundle therefore provides two complementary views of the same experimental
corpus:

\begin{enumerate}
\item a \textit{blind input view}, containing only the files submitted to the
evaluated software systems;

\item a \textit{control and audit view}, preserving the association between
each submitted file and its source sample, reference operational assignment,
experimental condition, provenance, and integrity information.
\end{enumerate}

Files in the blind input view must use neutral identifiers and must not expose
the reference operational class, fold, attack family, transformation type,
proxy model, or other experimental descriptors through filenames, directory
names, or paths. The corresponding mapping remains available only within the
separate control records.

This design reduces the risk that either the software system or the analyst
infers the experimental role of a sample from its organization rather than
from the submitted image. The blindness requirement concerns descriptor-level
information controlled by the experimental pipeline; it does not conceal the
visual content of the image or the observable effects of an applied
perturbation, since these constitute the actual inputs under evaluation.

The control and audit view must preserve a stable relationship among the
neutral bundle identifier, source-image identifier, reference operational
assignment, experimental family, perturbation condition, fold or evaluation
partition, provenance information, integrity information, and the identifier
used in the blind input view.

components of the forensic evaluation bundle.

information concerning acquisition devices, processing software, provenance,

\cite{xiang2021forensicmetadata,lee2023forensicmethodology,zheng2023exif}.

machine-readable fields that could reveal experimental information.

evaluated files unless metadata removal or normalization is explicitly included

limitation of the blind-input design rather than interpreted as evidence of
classification performance or operational robustness.

required clean and perturbed inputs are present, that no unintended duplicate
has been introduced, and that every blind identifier is correctly associated
with its control record. These checks establish the technical validity of the
evaluation bundle but do not constitute a robustness result.

The forensic evaluation bundle thus provides the controlled interface between
the reproducible experimental pipeline and the black-box software environments.
Its blind input view is processed according to the protocol described in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-forensic-tools}, while the separate control and
audit view supports subsequent output matching, normalization, traceability,
and metric computation.

\section{Black-Box Software Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:methodology-forensic-tools}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The operational component of the \gls{fairlab} methodology evaluates software
systems that provide automated or \gls{ai}-assisted functionality for image
classification, semantic retrieval, multimedia triage, or forensic review.
Unlike the proxy classification models described in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-models-classification}, these systems are evaluated
under black-box or near-black-box access conditions. No access is assumed to
their internal architectures, training data, model weights, decision
thresholds, calibration procedures, or proprietary inference logic. The
evaluation is based exclusively on submitted inputs and observable or
exportable outputs.

The purpose of this phase is not to validate or certify the selected software
systems or to rank heterogeneous tools in absolute terms. It examines their
observable operational behavior under controlled and reproducible conditions
using the same clean binary samples, clean \gls{ood} inputs, adversarial
artifacts, and anti-forensic artifacts considered in the controlled
experimental pipeline.

Processing begins with the blind input view defined in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-forensic-evaluation-bundle}. Only neutrally
identified image files are imported or indexed, while reference assignments,
fold allocations, perturbation descriptors, and control records remain outside
the software environment until output matching and normalization.

The evaluated systems may expose heterogeneous outputs, including assigned
categories, semantic tags, bookmarks, textual or visual retrieval results,
system-specific scores or rankings, detected-item lists, exported reports, and
missing, ambiguous, unsupported, or non-interpretable states. These outputs do
not necessarily correspond directly to the operational classes defined by the
experimental protocol and must therefore be normalized before quantitative
comparison.

Normalization comprises three stages:

\begin{enumerate}
\item matching each exported result to the corresponding blind bundle input
through a stable identifier;

\item interpreting the observable output through a documented
software-specific mapping rule;

\item recoding the interpreted result into common operational prediction states
suitable for metric computation.
\end{enumerate}

The matching procedure must preserve the relationship between each exported
result and its control record without exposing the reference assignment during
software processing. The software-specific mapping rule must be established
and documented before final quantitative analysis and must identify which
categories, tags, bookmarks, retrieval outcomes, or other observable outputs
constitute evidence that the target class was flagged.

The absence of a target-related output indicates only that the software did not
produce the relevant observable result under the tested configuration. It must
not be interpreted as independent semantic evidence that the target object is
absent. Likewise, outputs that cannot be mapped reliably must not be forced
into positive or negative predictions.

The protocol therefore preserves an explicit \texttt{unknown} state for
missing, ambiguous, unsupported, unmapped, or non-interpretable outputs.
Unknown outcomes and explicit negative predictions represent different
observable states and must remain separately reportable. Under a documented
risk-oriented analysis, an unknown outcome associated with a
\texttt{weapon} sample may contribute to an operational missed-detection count,
but its original unknown status must remain preserved. Conversely, broadly
related semantic outputs must not be automatically mapped to positive
detections, since doing so may inflate false-positive counts.

Software configuration is implementation-specific. For each evaluated system,
the category, query, module, plugin, or processing function that most closely
corresponds to the experimental target must be selected and documented. When
multiple reasonable configurations are available, sensitivity analysis may be
used to examine their effects on detection coverage and false-positive
behavior. The concrete software systems, versions, mappings, prompts,
thresholds, and configurations adopted in this thesis are reported in
Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

For each evaluated system, the protocol follows the same general workflow:

\begin{enumerate}
\item import or index only the blind evaluation inputs;

\item enable the relevant automated analysis or retrieval functionality;

\item process the complete blind input view under a documented configuration;

\item export all observable outputs required for matching and interpretation;

\item align the exported outputs with the experimental control metadata;

\item normalize the software-specific outputs into common operational
prediction states;

\item compute comparable metrics while preserving unknown outcomes.
\end{enumerate}

Raw exports, normalization records, and aggregated metrics must remain
separate. Raw exports preserve the software outputs as originally produced;
normalization records document input matching, interpretation, and recoding;
and metric records contain only the quantitative results derived after
normalization. This separation preserves the traceability chain from the
submitted bundle input to the observable output, mapping rule, normalized
prediction state, and final metric, while allowing ambiguous mappings or
normalization errors to be reviewed without repeating software execution.

Categories, tags, bookmarks, classifications, and retrieval results produced by
black-box systems must not be interpreted as local explanations of their
internal decision processes. They are treated solely as observable outputs, and
the methodology does not infer which internal features, models, or proprietary
mechanisms produced them.

The evaluation is therefore anchored to externally verifiable behavior. It
measures whether the selected systems produce operationally relevant outputs
for the submitted inputs without attributing internal properties that cannot
be independently verified
\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:methodology-evaluation-metrics}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The \gls{fairlab} metric framework evaluates three complementary dimensions of
operational robustness: binary classification performance under clean
conditions, robustness under adversarial and anti-forensic conditions, and
system behavior on clean \gls{ood} inputs. It also supports the evaluation of
black-box software systems after their heterogeneous outputs have been
normalized into common operational prediction states.

Aggregate accuracy alone is insufficient for operational interpretation.
Binary metrics are therefore computed separately for clean, adversarial, and
anti-forensic conditions and interpreted according to class and error
direction. The \gls{ood} branch is evaluated through separate behavioral
indicators and is not incorporated into the binary confusion matrix.

The protocol does not adopt \gls{auroc} as a central cross-system metric.
Although score-based measures are applicable to proxy models, the selected
black-box systems may expose only categories, semantic tags, bookmarks,
retrieval results, or system-specific scores whose meaning and calibration
cannot be independently verified. The framework therefore prioritizes measures
that can be derived consistently from both inspectable models and normalized
black-box outputs, without implying a theoretical limitation of \gls{auroc} or
other score-based metrics.

For the binary task, the principal metrics are accuracy, balanced accuracy,
precision, recall, F1-score, false-negative rate, false-positive rate, and the
confusion matrix. Let \(TP\), \(TN\), \(FP\), and \(FN\) denote the numbers of
true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives,
respectively. Accuracy is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Accuracy}
=
\frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}
\end{equation}

Recall for the positive \texttt{weapon} class is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Recall}_{\text{\texttt{weapon}}}
=
\frac{TP}{TP + FN}
\end{equation}

The corresponding false-negative rate is:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FNR}_{\text{\texttt{weapon}}}
=
\frac{FN}{TP + FN}
\end{equation}

The false-positive rate is:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FPR}
=
\frac{FP}{FP + TN}
\end{equation}

Balanced accuracy accounts for performance on both operational classes:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Balanced\ Accuracy}
=
\frac{1}{2}
\left(
\frac{TP}{TP + FN}
+
\frac{TN}{TN + FP}
\right)
\end{equation}

Precision and F1-score are defined as:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Precision}
=
\frac{TP}{TP + FP}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{F1}
=
2
\frac{
\mathrm{Precision}
\cdot
\mathrm{Recall}_{\text{\texttt{weapon}}}
}{
\mathrm{Precision}
+
\mathrm{Recall}_{\text{\texttt{weapon}}}
}
\end{equation}

Robustness under manipulated conditions is evaluated through a paired
comparison between each perturbed artifact, its corresponding clean sample,
and the reference operational class. This design distinguishes errors already
present under clean conditions from those induced by a perturbation.

For a perturbation condition \(p\), the accuracy drop is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\Delta \mathrm{Accuracy}_{p}
=
\mathrm{Accuracy}_{\mathrm{clean}}
-
\mathrm{Accuracy}_{p}
\end{equation}

Positive values indicate degradation relative to the clean baseline. Zero or
negative values indicate no measured degradation under the evaluated condition
and must not be interpreted as evidence of general robustness.

Let \(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{clean}}\) denote the set of samples classified
correctly under the clean condition. The induced error rate for condition \(p\)
is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{InducedErrorRate}_{p}
=
\frac{
\left|
\left\{
i \in \mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{clean}}
:
\hat{y}^{(p)}_{i} \neq y_{i}
\right\}
\right|
}{
\left|
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{clean}}
\right|
}
\end{equation}

The numerator represents the induced error count. For model-dependent
adversarial conditions, this rate may also be reported as the attack success
rate because it measures the proportion of clean-correct samples rendered
incorrect by the attack. Samples already misclassified under clean conditions
do not contribute to this quantity.

Particular attention is given to the transition:

\[
\text{\texttt{weapon}}
\rightarrow
\text{\texttt{non\_weapon}}
\]

because it represents the loss of a positive detection for target-category
content. The reverse transition:

\[
\text{\texttt{non\_weapon}}
\rightarrow
\text{\texttt{weapon}}
\]

is also measured because it may increase the volume of non-target content
prioritized for human review.

When continuous proxy-model outputs are available, the analysis also considers
the mean maximum predicted-class probability and its variation relative to the
corresponding clean condition. These quantities are diagnostic intra-model
indicators and must not be interpreted as calibrated probabilities or compared
directly across proxy architectures.

For each proxy model and manipulated condition, the confidence shift is defined
as:

\begin{equation}
\Delta c
=
c_{\mathrm{perturbed}}
-
c_{\mathrm{clean}}
\end{equation}

where \(c\) denotes the maximum predicted-class probability. This value does
not necessarily correspond to the probability assigned to the reference
operational class.

The \gls{ood} evaluation remains conceptually separate from the binary task.
Since \gls{ood} samples are not treated as a third supervised class, binary or
multiclass accuracy is not computed for this branch. Instead, the protocol
characterizes how frequently these inputs are assigned to the known
operational classes through:

\begin{itemize}
\item the proportion of \gls{ood} inputs assigned to \texttt{weapon};
\item the proportion of \gls{ood} inputs assigned to
\texttt{non\_weapon};
\item the mean maximum predicted-class probability for proxy models;
\item the proportion of proxy-model predictions whose maximum probability
exceeds a predefined high-confidence threshold;
\item the proportion of unknown outputs exposed after normalization of
black-box software outputs.
\end{itemize}

The \gls{ood} weapon flag rate is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{WeaponFlagRate}_{\mathrm{OOD}}
=
\frac{
N_{\mathrm{OOD}\rightarrow\text{\texttt{weapon}}}
}{
N_{\mathrm{OOD}}
}
\end{equation}

This measure describes the operational tendency of a system to map
distributionally different inputs to the target class. It is not a conventional
false-positive rate because \gls{ood} inputs do not belong to the supervised
negative class, nor does it establish that an individual assignment is
semantically correct, incorrect, relevant, or irrelevant.

Confidence-based \gls{ood} indicators are available only for proxy models.
They remain diagnostic intra-model quantities because the selected black-box
systems do not expose homogeneous confidence values suitable for an equivalent
cross-system analysis.

Black-box evaluation is based on the normalized states defined in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-forensic-tools}: positive detection, no target
detection, and \texttt{unknown}. Only matched records with interpretable
positive or negative decisions contribute to the conventional binary confusion
matrix; unknown outputs must not be forced into either class.

For a \texttt{weapon} sample, an interpretable no-target decision constitutes a
false negative. An unknown output may also contribute to a separately reported
operational miss count under a documented risk-oriented rule, but it must not
be recoded as a conventional false negative. For a
\texttt{non\_weapon} sample, an interpretable target-related output constitutes
a false positive, whereas an unknown output is not automatically treated as
one because it does not represent an explicit target detection.

For the selected black-box software systems, the protocol considers:

\begin{itemize}
\item matching rate between exported outputs and bundle inputs;
\item true-positive and false-negative counts on interpretable records;
\item true-negative and false-positive counts on interpretable records;
\item weapon recall;
\item weapon false-negative rate;
\item false-positive rate;
\item accuracy and balanced accuracy, where the normalized outputs permit
their computation;
\item unknown output count and rate;
\item system-specific not-detected or uncategorized rates, where exposed;
\item supplementary operational miss counts, where explicitly defined.
\end{itemize}

The matching rate is a technical coverage measure rather than a classification
metric. It represents the proportion of submitted bundle inputs for which a
corresponding software output can be identified:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{MatchingRate}
=
\frac{
N_{\mathrm{matched}}
}{
N_{\mathrm{submitted}}
}
\end{equation}

The unknown output rate is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{UnknownRate}
=
\frac{
N_{\mathrm{unknown}}
}{
N_{\mathrm{matched}}
}
\end{equation}

Unmatched inputs are represented through the matching rate and are not included
again in the numerator of the unknown rate. These coverage measures are
reported because incomplete matching and non-interpretable outputs may affect
apparent black-box performance, but they must not be conflated with ordinary
classification errors.

\section{Explainability Approach}
\label{sec:methodology-explainability}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The \gls{xai} component of the \gls{fairlab} methodology serves an exclusively
diagnostic and qualitative function. It complements the quantitative robustness
metrics by supporting the examination of selected cases involving an
inspectable proxy classification model. Explainability outputs are not treated
as stand-alone evidence, definitive explanations of algorithmic decisions, or
demonstrations of model reliability. They are used to characterize attribution
patterns and formulate hypotheses regarding changes in model behavior across
clean, adversarial, anti-forensic, and \gls{ood} conditions.

The explainability analysis is restricted to one selected fully inspectable
proxy model. The adopted technique must be compatible with its architecture,
weights, preprocessing procedure, and inference pipeline and may require access
to gradients, intermediate activations, or other internal components. The same
internal attribution analysis cannot be applied to the selected black-box or
near-black-box software systems because their architectures, representations,
decision thresholds, and scoring procedures are not accessible. Their
observable outputs must not be interpreted as explanations of the internal
processes that produced them.

The methodology adopts a case-study approach rather than an aggregate
explainability evaluation. Representative samples are selected according to
predefined diagnostic criteria to examine behaviors that cannot be fully
characterized through corpus-level metrics. The selected cases are not used to
estimate general model performance.

High-confidence criteria are applied only within the selected proxy model and
serve exclusively as case-selection indicators. They must not be interpreted
as calibrated probabilities or compared directly with confidence values from
other architectures or software systems.

Table~\ref{tab:methodology-xai-case-selection} summarizes the five case
categories and their diagnostic functions.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Case-selection criteria for explainability analysis}
\label{tab:methodology-xai-case-selection}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}L{4.4cm}X@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Case category} &
\textbf{Diagnostic function} \\
\midrule

Correctly classified clean reference
&
Provides a qualitative reference for examining whether the attribution pattern
is associated with visually relevant regions under an unmanipulated condition. \\

Clean classification error
&
Examines an error already present under the clean condition and whether the
attribution pattern appears localized, diffuse, partial, or associated with
contextual image regions. \\

High-confidence \gls{ood} assignment
&
Examines the attribution pattern associated with an in-domain assignment
produced for a distributionally different input. The assignment is treated as
an operational behavior rather than as an ordinary supervised error. \\

Anti-forensic induced error
&
Examines whether an operationally plausible image transformation is associated
with a change in the attribution pattern and the resulting predicted class. \\

&

produced after a model-dependent adversarial perturbation, including cases in

\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

Case selection and the resulting explainability artifacts must remain
traceable to the corresponding source sample, prediction record, evaluation
condition, model configuration, and checkpoint. Each record must also preserve
the predicted class, reference operational class, and rationale for case
selection, consistently with the controls defined in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-traceability-controls}.

The methodology does not prescribe a single explainability technique for all
implementations. Gradient-based attribution, activation-based visualization,
local surrogate methods, or other feature-attribution approaches may be used
when their assumptions and limitations are compatible with the selected model
and research questions. The specific technique and configuration adopted in
this thesis are documented in Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation}.

The adopted procedure should satisfy the following requirements:

\begin{itemize}
\item compatibility with the selected inspectable proxy model;
\item explicit documentation of the attribution target and reference baseline,
where required by the method;
\item reproducibility of the attribution procedure;
\item consistent application across the selected clean and manipulated cases;
\item traceability to the model checkpoint, input sample, and prediction record;
\item production of outputs that are not presented as definitive causal
explanations.
\end{itemize}

Attribution maps and related visualizations characterize relationships between
input features and a selected model output under the assumptions of the adopted
method. They do not establish that highlighted regions reproduce the model's
internal reasoning or demonstrate that a prediction is correct, reliable, or
forensically valid.

When the selected method provides completeness, approximation, or convergence
diagnostics, these quantities must be recorded and considered during
interpretation. They act as validation aids and do not automatically establish
that an attribution is complete or reliable. A strict convergence claim may be
made only when supported by the recorded diagnostic values and an explicitly
defined acceptance criterion.

Explainability remains distinct from the quantitative robustness assessment.
Classification metrics characterize the frequency, magnitude, and direction of
errors across the experimental corpus, whereas \gls{xai} supports the
qualitative examination of selected decisions and failure cases. Within the
\gls{fairlab} protocol, it therefore acts as a supporting diagnostic layer
while preserving the distinction among technical attribution, experimental
robustness evaluation, and evidentiary assessment by a human forensic analyst.

\section{Traceability and Reproducibility Controls}
\label{sec:methodology-traceability-controls}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Traceability is a cross-cutting requirement of the entire \gls{fairlab}
protocol. It applies to technical preparation, human-in-the-loop review,
dataset freezing, partition generation, proxy-model training, perturbation
generation, bundle construction, black-box software evaluation, metric
computation, and \gls{xai} analysis.

Consistent with established \gls{df} principles, every processing step that
creates or modifies an experimental file must be documented so that the
relationships among the original sample, derived artifacts, model or software
outputs, and final experimental results can be reconstructed
\cite{casey2011digital,nist2006guide,iso27037}. Each sample must remain
identifiable throughout its lifecycle even when its filename, encoding,
directory location, or visual representation changes.

The traceability framework distinguishes four complementary elements:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{sample identity}, which establishes a stable reference to the
original image;

\item \textit{artifact lineage}, which records the relationship between an
original sample and any derived adversarial, anti-forensic, blind-bundle, or
explainability artifact;

\item \textit{experimental context}, which associates each input or derived
artifact with its reference operational assignment, partition, model
configuration, perturbation condition, and evaluation branch;

\item \textit{output provenance}, which connects model predictions, software
exports, normalization decisions, metrics, and qualitative case studies to the
corresponding evaluation input.
\end{enumerate}

Integrity verification should rely on cryptographic identifiers or other
mechanisms capable of detecting unintended changes to experimental files. The
selected mechanism must be documented and applied consistently. Additional
identifiers may be retained for interoperability with black-box software
systems, but they must not replace the primary integrity mechanism unless they
provide appropriate security and collision-resistance properties.

Traceability records should be progressive: each stage preserves the
identifiers inherited from the preceding stage and adds only the information
required to describe the newly created artifact, configuration, or
experimental condition. This approach allows the lifecycle of a sample to be
reconstructed without relying exclusively on filenames or directory
structures.

Reproducibility requires more than source-code availability. The experimental
configuration must be documented in sufficient detail to recreate the relevant
processing conditions, including the dataset version, partitioning strategy,
randomization controls, model configuration, preprocessing rules, perturbation
parameters, software version and enabled functionality, normalization rules,
and metric definitions. When randomness affects partitioning, model
initialization, optimization, perturbation generation, or case selection, the
corresponding random state or deterministic procedure must be recorded.

Model and artifact records must preserve the associations required by the
fold-aware and perturbation-generation procedures. In particular, each
checkpoint must remain linked to its training partitions, and a checkpoint
used to evaluate or perturb a held-out sample must not have been trained on
that sample. Model-dependent adversarial artifacts must identify their source
model and checkpoint, whereas model-agnostic adversarial-style and
anti-forensic artifacts must preserve the shared generation condition and
applied configuration. These records support the distinction among direct
source-model evaluation, transfer evaluation, and comparison under shared
model-agnostic conditions.

Black-box evaluation introduces an additional separation requirement. Reference
assignments, perturbation descriptors, folds, model identifiers, and other
controlled experimental metadata must remain outside the software input during
processing while remaining available after export for matching, normalization,
and metric computation. The evaluation records must make this separation
verifiable.

Automated consistency controls should verify, where applicable:

\begin{itemize}
\item correspondence between expected and generated artifacts;
\item uniqueness of stable and derived identifiers;
\item integrity of generated files;
\item absence of missing or unresolved paths;
\item consistency among source records, derived artifacts, and evaluation
outputs;
\item completeness of software exports and normalized predictions;
\item separation between blind inputs and experimental metadata.
\end{itemize}

These controls establish the technical coherence of the experimental workflow;
they do not demonstrate classification correctness or operational robustness.
Their purpose is to ensure that reported results derive from the intended
inputs, artifacts, and configurations rather than from undocumented
substitutions, incomplete runs, or incorrect associations.

For black-box systems, reproducibility concerns the documented external
configuration, submitted inputs, observable outputs, and normalization
procedures rather than inaccessible internal models, thresholds, or inference
logic. The protocol therefore combines \textit{procedural reproducibility},
which documents how the experiment can be repeated, with
\textit{artifact-level traceability}, which identifies the exact inputs,
models, software configurations, and derived files contributing to each
reported result.

outcomes are reported in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

\chapter{Methodology}
\label{chap:methodology}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
This chapter describes the methodology adopted to construct the experimental
foundation of the thesis and to enable the evaluation of the operational
robustness of \gls{ai}-based systems for automated image classification in
forensic contexts. Consistent with the gap identified in
Chapter~\ref{chap:state-of-the-art}, concerning the limited availability of
reproducible protocols for validating \gls{ai}-based models and software under
controlled but operationally relevant forensic-context conditions, the chapter
does not merely describe individual technical components. Rather, it formalizes
an integrated experimental design. This design connects datasets, models,
adversarial perturbations and anti-forensic transformations, selected software,
metrics, the \gls{xai} approach, and traceability controls within the
\gls{fairlab} experimental protocol, while maintaining a clear distinction
between experimental methodology and results analysis.

The chapter is organized into fifteen sections. The first six
(\S\S\,\ref{sec:methodology-fairlab-overview}--\ref{sec:methodology-split-generation})
describe the construction of the experimental foundation, including the
methodological design, dataset preparation, generation of the review manifest,
the \textit{human-in-the-loop} protocol, dataset freezing, and split
generation. The following nine
(\S\S\,\ref{sec:methodology-models-classification}--\ref{sec:methodology-traceability-controls})
describe the classification paradigm, the selection and generation of
adversarial perturbations and anti-forensic transformations, the
\textit{forensic evaluation bundle}, the evaluation protocol for selected software, the metrics,
the \gls{xai} approach, and the traceability and
reproducibility controls.

The distinction between methodology and results analysis is maintained
throughout the chapter: the resulting artifacts are described as consolidated
methodological components, whereas the quantitative evidence, the comparison between proxy models and selected software,
and the experimental interpretations are deferred to
the results chapter.

\section{Overview of the FAIR-Lab methodology}
\label{sec:methodology-fairlab-overview}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The methodology of this thesis is organized around the \gls{fairlab}
experimental protocol, conceived as an operational framework designed to make
the construction of the dataset, the generation of perturbations, the evaluation
of proxy models, and the black-box analysis of the selected software traceable
and reproducible at the level of inputs, outputs, and normalization rules. The
protocol stems from the need to move beyond a purely algorithmic evaluation of
image classification, by connecting model performance to its potential
operational role within forensic triage and prioritization workflows for
digital evidence. From this perspective, robustness is not considered solely as
a technical property of the classifier, but also as a methodological
requirement for the judicious use of automation in an investigative context.

The protocol integrates two complementary evaluation levels. The first concerns
the controlled evaluation of proxy models, in which datasets, models,
perturbations, metrics, and \gls{xai} approaches are defined in a reproducible
and comparable manner. The second concerns the operational black-box evaluation
of the selected software, in which the artifacts produced by the experimental
protocol are prepared for analysis by professional or general-purpose software
equipped with \gls{ai}-based functionality. Alongside these two levels, a
separate \gls{ood} branch is included. This branch is not conceived as a third
supervised class, but as an operational stress-testing axis used to observe
whether external, borderline, or anomalous inputs are forced into the
operational classes of the binary task. This distinction makes it possible to
separate the experimental control associated with inspectable models from the
black-box evaluation required for the analysis of proprietary software, which
is often characterized by modules that are not fully accessible
\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.

Within the consolidated experimental protocol, the methodology includes the
construction of dataset artifacts, the generation of clean splits, the
production of the main families of adversarial perturbations and anti-forensic
transformations, and the preparation of the bundle for forensic evaluation. In
particular, the final frozen dataset, the binary subset intended for
adversarial and anti-forensic experiments, the clean splits for baseline
evaluation, the separate \gls{ood} set, and the manipulated artifacts to be
submitted to the proxy models and selected software were defined and produced.
These components constitute the stable foundation of the experimental protocol
and make it possible to separate artifact construction from the subsequent
quantitative analysis of the results. This distinction is essential to prevent
future changes to the data, models, or manipulated files from retroactively
altering the scope of the evaluation.


Figure~\ref{fig:methodology-fairlab-overview} summarizes the overall structure
of the \gls{fairlab} experimental protocol. The figure highlights the
separation between the binary subset intended for perturbation generation and
the \gls{ood} branch, which is kept clean and evaluated separately. The clean,
\gls{ood}, adversarial, and anti-forensic artifacts then converge into the
forensic evaluation bundle, which is used both for the controlled evaluation of
the proxy models and for the black-box evaluation of the selected software.

\section{Research design and experimental assumptions}
\label{sec:methodology-research-design}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The experimental design adopts as its reference scenario a forensic visual
triage activity in which an automated system supports the analyst in the
preliminary identification of potentially relevant images. The case study
involves image classification across three main methodological categories:
\texttt{weapon}, \texttt{non\_weapon}, and \texttt{ood}. These categories must
not be interpreted as legal qualifications of the content, nor as general
semantic classes valid in every context, but rather as operational labels
defined for the experimental protocol of this thesis.

Consequently, the task does not aim to cover the entire domain of weapon
detection, but to construct a controlled, firearm-oriented case study that is
operationally relevant for evaluating the robustness of automated visual triage
systems.

In the main binary task, the \texttt{weapon} class represents the positive
class, namely the set of images in which an object belonging to the
investigatively relevant, firearm-oriented category defined by the protocol
is visible.
The \texttt{non\_weapon} class instead represents the in-domain negative class:
it includes realistic and interpretable images that do not contain the object
of interest, while remaining compatible with the visual domain of the
experimental collection.
The \texttt{ood} category constitutes a separate branch of the evaluation and
comprises images that should not be forced into the
\texttt{weapon}/\texttt{non\_weapon} dichotomy because they are ambiguous,
anomalous, synthetic, degraded, or borderline, or because they belong to
different visual distributions.

In the context of forensic triage, a false negative on the \texttt{weapon}
class is considered particularly critical because it may reduce the probability
that a relevant artifact is subjected to in-depth human review.

The class assignment criteria were also formalized in the manual review tool
used to construct the final dataset. This choice makes explicit the connection
between the methodological definition of the classes, the
\textit{human-in-the-loop} process, and the final manifests produced by the
pipeline. Table~\ref{tab:methodology-operational-classes} summarizes these
criteria in operational form.

The presence of the operational label \texttt{exclude} does not alter the
structure of the final dataset, which remains composed of the three
methodological categories \texttt{weapon}, \texttt{non\_weapon}, and
\texttt{ood}. Rather, it serves a quality control function during the review
process, enabling the removed elements to be documented and separated from the
samples ultimately included in the benchmark.

The choice to include an explicit \gls{ood} branch addresses a specific
methodological requirement. In a real operational context, classification
systems do not receive only clean images that are semantically aligned with the
expected classes, but also ambiguous, degraded, synthetic, irrelevant, or
distributionally different content. The literature on \gls{ood} detection has
shown that classifiers may produce high-confidence predictions even on inputs
outside the training domain, making it necessary to distinguish
misclassifications on known classes from errors arising from samples that
should not be classified as ordinary positive or negative
instances~\cite{hendrycks2017baseline,fort2021exploring}.

The \texttt{ood} category should therefore not be interpreted as a homogeneous
semantic category, nor as a simple subclass of the \texttt{non\_weapon}
category. Rather, it represents a set of methodological stress tests aimed at
assessing the tendency of automated systems to force anomalous, borderline, or
out-of-domain content into known operational classes. For this reason,
\gls{ood} samples are kept separate from the binary subset and are not
subjected to the adversarial perturbations or anti-forensic transformations
generated for the \texttt{weapon}/\texttt{non\_weapon} task.

The methodology further distinguishes among automated classification,
robustness, and operational evaluation. Automated classification concerns the
system's ability to assign a class to an image. Robustness concerns the
stability of this behavior when the input is degraded, altered, or manipulated.
Operational evaluation instead concerns the impact of the automated output on
the forensic workflow, particularly with respect to the risk of false negatives
on relevant content, false positives that increase the analytical workload, and
improper classifications of \gls{ood} samples. This distinction prevents
nominal accuracy on clean data from being interpreted as a sufficient guarantee
of reliability in investigative scenarios.

Finally, the protocol adopts a \textit{human-in-the-loop} approach. The manual
component is not treated as an external or residual element, but as a documented
part of the methodology. In the forensic domain, dataset construction cannot
depend exclusively on automated labels or on the original source directories:
the semantic criteria for inclusion, exclusion, and class assignment must be
explicit, traceable, and consistent with the investigative task. This approach
makes it possible to document not only the final outcome of the selection, but
also the operational rules that guided the construction of the frozen dataset.

\section{Dataset construction and source consolidation}
\label{sec:methodology-dataset-construction}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Dataset construction was organized into a sequence of distinct phases, designed
to separate source collection, technical file preparation, semantic review, and
freezing of the final artifacts. This separation is methodologically relevant
because it makes it possible to distinguish what pertains to the technical
validity of an image file from what pertains to its semantic interpretation
with respect to the forensic task.

The initial phase involved organizing five heterogeneous sources within the
\codepath{datasets/raw/} directory. The sources include public datasets,
material derived from specialized repositories, collections obtained through
controlled web scraping, content acquired through an \gls{osint}-oriented
approach, and data collected through deep web-oriented procedures. The objective
was not to build a visually homogeneous benchmark, but rather an initial pool
sufficiently varied to represent some of the heterogeneity that characterizes
contemporary digital investigations.

Starting from the raw sources, the script
\codepath{datasets/scripts/prepared/08_build_prepared_dataset.py} builds the
prepared pool in \codepath{datasets/prepared/final_pool/}. This phase does not
assign definitive semantic labels, but produces a technically validated
foundation through readability checks, dimensional filtering, hash computation,
and removal of exact duplicates. In particular, each candidate image is
verified as a valid image file, subjected to a minimum size threshold,
associated with a \gls{sha256} value, and copied into the prepared pool only
if it passes the technical checks.

The main output of the preparation phase is the file \texttt{metadata.csv},
which records, for each sample, information such as \texttt{image\_id}, the
name of the prepared file, the originating source, the original relative path,
the \gls{sha256} hash, dimensions, extension, and file size. The availability
of these metadata makes it possible to maintain a stable connection between the
original source and the normalized version included in the experimental pool.
The reporting outputs, including \texttt{invalid\_images.csv},
\texttt{duplicates\_discarded.csv}, and
\texttt{prepared\_build\_summary.json}, in turn document technical exclusions
and removed duplicates.

This phase has an exclusively technical function. The pipeline deliberately
avoids inferring the final class of an image from the source folder, the file
name, or any automated pre-labels. In this way, the source is maintained as a
descriptive and audit variable, but not as a ground truth criterion. This choice
reduces the risk of propagating biases or errors from the original sources into
the final selection, while preserving the distinction between sample provenance
and verified semantic assignment.

The operational review manifest included a total of 15\,321 candidate images.
The distribution across sources was strongly asymmetric: the
\texttt{02\_deepfirearm} source alone represented the numerically dominant
component of the pool, whereas the remaining sources contributed significantly
lower volumes. This asymmetry was treated as a characteristic of the candidate
pool and not as a final sampling criterion: the \textit{human-in-the-loop}
phase applied semantic and balancing criteria that were independent of the
original volume of the individual sources. Consequently, although the
\texttt{02\_deepfirearm} source was dominant in the initial pool, it contributes
100 \texttt{weapon} images to the frozen dataset, corresponding to the same
positive quota assigned to the other sources. The final distribution by source
and class is reported in Table~\ref{tab:final-dataset-source-distribution} of
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-human-review}.

consolidation process and the progressive selection of the final dataset. The
raw sources are used as a heterogeneous candidate pool, whereas the final

\section{Review manifest generation}
\label{sec:methodology-review-manifest}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

After the construction of the prepared pool, the pipeline generates a complete
review manifest through the script
\codepath{datasets/scripts/prepared/09_generate_review_manifest_full.py}.
This step establishes the connection between the technical preparation of the
dataset and the subsequent manual semantic selection. The resulting file,
\codepath{datasets/prepared/manifests/review_manifest_full.csv}, preserves the
technical information derived from \texttt{metadata.csv} and introduces columns
dedicated to semantic review.

The review manifest is designed to support a controlled and inspectable
pipeline. On the one hand, it preserves identity and provenance fields, such as
\texttt{image\_id}, \texttt{sha256}, \texttt{relative\_path},
\texttt{source\_dataset}, and \texttt{source\_relative\_path}. On the other
hand, it introduces fields dedicated to the review status, the final label,
reviewer notes, reviewer identifier, and the handling of \gls{ood} or
excluded cases. The manifest also provides auxiliary pre-label fields, which do
not, however, replace the manual decision of the reviewer: the only criterion
for assigning the final label is the explicit semantic judgment formulated
during the \textit{human-in-the-loop} review. In this way, the manifest becomes
the central artifact for documenting the transition from a technical pool to a
semantically validated dataset.

The choice to introduce review fields in a file separate from the technical
metadata preserves the distinction between two levels of the pipeline. The
first level concerns the existence, readability, and technical uniqueness of
the files. The second, by contrast, concerns their relevance with respect to
the forensic task. This separation is particularly important because a
technically valid image may be semantically unsuitable for the \texttt{weapon}
or \texttt{non\_weapon} class, whereas a borderline image may be useful as an
\texttt{ood} sample for evaluating the reliability of the system under
open-set-like conditions.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Human-in-the-loop review and final dataset freezing}
\label{sec:methodology-human-review}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The final dataset selection was performed through a
\textit{human-in-the-loop} protocol, implemented in the script
\codepath{datasets/scripts/final/10_manual_selection_protocol_reviewer.py}.
This protocol enables the reviewer to manually inspect the images in the
prepared pool and assign one of four designated labels: \texttt{weapon},
\texttt{non\_weapon}, \texttt{ood}, or \texttt{exclude}. The reviewer's
decisions are saved in an operational database and recorded in dedicated logs,
thereby ensuring traceability of the selection process.

The semantic criteria adopted for label assignment were formulated
conservatively. The \texttt{weapon} class includes only firearm-oriented visual
content, such as realistic individual firearms that are clearly visible and
consistent with the classification task. The assessment is based on the visual
appearance of the object in the image and not on any determination of its
material nature.

Images in which visual cues clearly suggest replicas, toys, airsoft models, or
synthetic content are not included in the \texttt{weapon} class, but are
assigned to the \texttt{ood} category. The \texttt{non\_weapon} class comprises
realistic images containing no firearm-oriented target content that are
plausible as negative examples within the investigative domain under
consideration. The \texttt{ood} category instead comprises borderline or
out-of-distribution content, such as knives, swords, replicas, airsoft items,
synthetic images, videogame imagery, heavy military vehicles, explosions, war
scenes, strongly degraded images, or anomalous content with respect to the
domain of interest. The \texttt{exclude} label is reserved for files that are
unusable or inconsistent with the requirements of the final dataset.

The final labels must therefore be interpreted as operational categories of the
experimental protocol, not as definitive assessments of the legal, material, or
evidentiary nature of the depicted object. This distinction is particularly
relevant in borderline cases, where the decision to include a sample in the
\gls{ood} set reflects a deliberate methodological choice not to force
ambiguous content into the main binary task.

The outcome of the review is the official frozen dataset, recorded in the
manifest \codepath{datasets/final/manifests/manual_selection_final_1500.csv}.
The final dataset contains 1500 images balanced across the three main
methodological categories, as shown in
Table~\ref{tab:methodology-final-dataset}. The distribution by source and
class, reported in Table~\ref{tab:final-dataset-source-distribution}, shows
how manual selection compensated for the imbalance of the candidate pool: each
source contributes 100 images to the \texttt{weapon} class, whereas the
distribution of the \texttt{non\_weapon} and \texttt{ood} categories reflects
the semantic characteristics of the individual sources.

One limitation of the frozen dataset concerns the source distribution of the
\texttt{non\_weapon} class, in which the \texttt{05\_deepweb} source
contributes a dominant share of samples. This imbalance derives from the
\textit{human-in-the-loop} protocol, which prioritized semantic clarity,
forensic relevance, and separation from the \texttt{weapon} class rather than a
rigid source-level balance. Although the final dataset is balanced at the class
level, the source-level imbalance may affect the visual diversity of the
negative class and is therefore considered a methodological limitation in the
interpretation of the results.

Consequently, conclusions concerning the \texttt{non\_weapon} class must be
interpreted with respect to the actual composition of the frozen dataset, and
not as a general characterization of realistic negatives across heterogeneous
forensic scenarios. This limitation does not invalidate the balance of the
binary task, but requires caution in interpreting the negative class as an
exhaustive representation of all possible forensic negatives.

The selection protocol was conducted by a single reviewer, according to
explicit and documented criteria. This choice represents a limitation compared
with multi-reviewer protocols that include a formal measure of inter-annotator
agreement, but it makes it possible to maintain traceability of decisions,
consistent application of the criteria, and documented reproducibility of the
dataset freezing process. For this reason, the protocol prioritizes decision
traceability and consistent application of the criteria over the statistical
measurement of annotator agreement. In this sense, the
\textit{human-in-the-loop} component does not preclude reproducibility, but
constitutes a documented, auditable part of the protocol, consistent with the
interpretive nature of forensic classification.

for a total of 1000 balanced images. The \gls{ood} samples are not used as
primary targets for perturbation generation because they address a distinct
experimental question: evaluating whether automated models and selected software systems tend to
improperly assign a known binary class to borderline, anomalous, or content
that is semantically outside the operational domain of the task.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Split generation and OOD evaluation set}
\label{sec:methodology-split-generation}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The next phase of the dataset pipeline consists of generating the clean binary
splits and the clean \gls{ood} evaluation set. This operation is performed
using the script
\codepath{datasets/scripts/splits/11_generate_clean_and_ood_splits.py}, which
takes as input the two official manifests produced during the freezing phase:
\texttt{manual\_selection\_final\_1500.csv} and
\texttt{manual\_selection\_adversarial\_subset.csv}. The script does not modify
the final manifests, but instead produces organized copies of the images and
specific manifests for the subsequent experimental phases.

The binary subset, composed of the \texttt{weapon} and \texttt{non\_weapon}
classes, is divided into five deterministic folds, named \texttt{fold\_1},
\texttt{fold\_2}, \texttt{fold\_3}, \texttt{fold\_4}, and \texttt{fold\_5}.
Each fold contains 200 images, evenly distributed between the two operational
classes of the binary task. The split procedure uses a pseudo-random seed fixed
at 42, so that the partition can be reproducibly regenerated from the official
manifests and the indicated script.

In this phase, the term \texttt{fold} denotes balanced and traceable
experimental units, not training partitions in the sense of traditional
cross-validation. The decision to adopt five folds addresses a twofold
methodological requirement. On the one hand, it makes it possible to divide the
binary subset into units that are homogeneous in size and class composition,
facilitating comparison among baseline evaluation, perturbation generation,
and results analysis. On the other hand, it makes it possible to maintain a
controlled separation between the samples used for training the proxy models
and those used as experimental holdout data, reducing the risk of leakage in
the subsequent robustness evaluation. The folds are therefore not introduced
as a model selection procedure or as a general performance estimate obtained
through traditional cross-validation. Instead, they define fold-aware
experimental units: for each target fold, the proxy models are trained on the
remaining folds and evaluated on the held-out fold, thereby preserving a
controlled separation between training, perturbation generation, and
robustness evaluation.

used to generate adversarial perturbations or anti-forensic transformations on
known classes, but rather to evaluate system behavior when the input consists
of content that should not be treated as an ordinary example of \texttt{weapon}
or \texttt{non\_weapon}. The separation of the \gls{ood} set therefore makes it
possible to distinguish robustness on the binary task from the handling of
borderline, anomalous, or semantically out-of-domain inputs with respect to the
operational domain.

The main outputs of the split generation phase are three artifacts:
\texttt{clean\_folds\_manifest.csv}, \texttt{ood\_eval\_manifest.csv}, and

documents the consistency checks performed during split generation, including
the uniqueness of the \texttt{image\_id} values, the uniqueness of the hashes,
and the correspondence between the expected counts and the counts actually
produced.

This organization provides stable experimental units for the subsequent
baseline evaluations, for the generation of manipulated inputs, and for the
comparison with the outputs produced by the selected software.

\section{Models and classification paradigm}
\label{sec:methodology-models-classification}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The controlled evaluation of the proxy models relies on a set of transparent
and reproducibly trained architectures to provide inspectable algorithmic
references for \gls{ai}-based classifiers exposed to clean data, adversarial
inputs, and anti-forensic transformations. These models must not be interpreted as the software systems evaluated
in the black-box phase of the protocol, nor as
substitutes for the commercial software analyzed. Rather, they constitute
controlled experimental components, necessary to define an inspectable
algorithmic reference, to generate the perturbations that require access to the
target model, and to study the possible transferability of the observed effects
to other classification systems.

The classification paradigm adopted by the proxy models is binary. The
operational labels are \texttt{non\_weapon} and \texttt{weapon}, encoded
respectively as class 0 and class 1 in the model registry. Although the
\texttt{ood} category is part of the final frozen dataset, it is not used to
train the proxy models and does not constitute a primary target for the
generation of adversarial or anti-forensic perturbations. It remains separate
for the evaluation of out-of-distribution behavior, consistent with the
methodological distinction introduced in
Sections~\ref{sec:methodology-research-design} and
\ref{sec:methodology-split-generation}. This choice avoids treating the
\gls{ood} problem as an ordinary third supervised class and makes it possible
to keep the evaluation of binary robustness separate from the handling of
borderline, anomalous, or semantically external inputs with respect to the
operational domain.

The official proxy models considered in this phase are ResNet18,
EfficientNet-B0, and \gls{clip}. ResNet18 represents a well-established and
relatively compact convolutional baseline, providing a standard \gls{cnn}
reference for the binary task~\cite{he2016deep}. EfficientNet-B0 is used as a
more efficient convolutional model and constitutes the main proxy target for
generating model-dependent adversarial attacks in the current experimental
protocol~\cite{tan2019efficientnet}. \gls{clip}, instead, is used as a
pre-trained vision-language model to extract visual representations from
images. In this case, the weights of the visual encoder are not updated during
training, while a supervised binary classifier is trained for the
\texttt{non\_weapon}/\texttt{weapon} task. This configuration makes it possible
to include in the protocol a large-scale pre-trained model adapted to the
forensic task under consideration, without retraining the entire visual
backbone~\cite{radford2021learning}. Table~\ref{tab:methodology-proxy-models}
summarizes the methodological role of the three models.

the images used to train the proxy model and the images retained as holdout
data for the target fold, preserving a methodological separation between
training, perturbation generation, and experimental evaluation.

The official script for training the proxy models is
\codepath{models/scripts/12_train_proxy_models.py}. It takes as input the
manifest \codepath{datasets/splits/manifests/clean_folds_manifest.csv},
applies the binary mapping \texttt{non\_weapon}~$\rightarrow 0$ and

\codepath{models/model_registry.json}. The model registry documents, for each
architecture and for each fold, the checkpoint pattern, the input size, the
normalization used, and the \gls{sha256} hash of the produced file. Recording
the checkpoint hash is methodologically relevant because it makes it possible
to verify that the subsequent phases of the pipeline, including the generation
of model-dependent attacks and the robustness evaluations, can be traced back
to a specific and unchanged version of the model. The main training parameters
are reported in Table~\ref{tab:methodology-training-hyperparameters}; the full
configuration is recorded in the training script and in the registry
\codepath{models/model_registry.json}, supporting the reproducibility and
auditability of training across executions.

Within the experimental protocol, EfficientNet-B0 assumes the role of
\textit{primary proxy target}, namely the main controlled model against which
model-dependent adversarial perturbations are generated. This choice does not
imply that EfficientNet-B0 represents the most robust or most accurate model
in an absolute sense, nor that it exhausts the comparative analysis of the
thesis. Rather, it identifies the controlled model against which, in this
phase of the pipeline, the perturbations requiring access to the gradient,
the classifier, or the decision behavior of the target are constructed.

Anti-forensic transformations and perturbations that do not depend on the
gradient are instead not generated as specific optimizations against
EfficientNet-B0, but as controlled manipulations of the image artifact or of
its visual content. This distinction makes it possible to separate white-box
or model-dependent scenarios from model-agnostic stressors and realistic
anti-forensic transformations.

This approach keeps the adversarial protocol controlled and comparable across
families of perturbations, avoiding a multiplication of experimental variants
as a function of the attacked architecture. The objective is not to optimize
the attacks separately against each model, but to generate a coherent
adversarial testbed and then evaluate the transferability of the
perturbations to different models, such as ResNet18 and \gls{clip}.

Finally, it is necessary to distinguish the proxy models from the selected
software evaluated in the black-box phase of the protocol. The proxy models are
open, configurable, and documented components, for which the architecture,
checkpoints, class mapping, training protocol, and file hashes are known. The
selected software systems, by contrast, are treated as black-box or
quasi-black-box systems: the analysis does not assume access to their internal
architecture, but instead focuses on observable outputs, their consistency with
the experimental manifests, and their behavior in the presence of clean,
manipulated, or out-of-distribution images. This distinction makes it possible
to keep the experimental control of the proxy models separate from the
operational evaluation of the selected software.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Perturbation selection rationale and operational relevance}
\label{sec:methodology-perturbation-selection-rationale}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The selection of perturbations was guided not exclusively by the theoretical
strength of the attacks, but also by their computational sustainability and
their consistency with a controlled but operationally relevant \gls{df}
scenario. In a forensic workflow, a visual triage system must be able to
process large volumes of data within time constraints compatible with
investigative needs. Consequently, the computational cost of perturbations
represents a methodologically relevant criterion, both for the reproducibility
of the experiment and for the interpretation of the operational plausibility of
the manipulations considered.

Anti-forensic transformations, such as \gls{jpeg} recompression,
resample~+~resize, Gaussian blur, histogram modification, and contrast
stretching, have a limited computational cost and can be applied in batch mode
even to large image collections. They are therefore particularly relevant from
an operational perspective, since they simulate simple, inexpensive, and
plausible manipulations that an actor could apply to degrade, alter, or
normalize an image before acquisition or forensic analysis.

Model-dependent adversarial attacks, by contrast, require interaction with a
target model and, in some cases, iterative or optimization-based procedures.
\gls{fgsm} represents an efficient baseline, since it requires a single
gradient estimate per image. Attacks such as \gls{onepixel}, \gls{sigmazero},
and \gls{superdeepfool} are instead more computationally demanding and are
included not because they necessarily represent the most likely operational
scenario, but because they make it possible to stress the behavior of
classifiers under controlled conditions. The \gls{colorshift} perturbation,
although included in the adversarial group for experimental purposes, remains
model-agnostic and has a low computational cost.

This approach is consistent with the objective of the thesis: to evaluate the
operational robustness of \gls{ai}-based systems in a forensic context, rather
than to produce an exhaustive ranking of the strongest adversarial attacks in a
strictly algorithmic sense. For this reason, the protocol prioritizes a
heterogeneous set of perturbations, balancing experimental severity,
computational sustainability, reproducibility, and relevance with respect to
plausible anti-forensic and degradation scenarios.

\section{Adversarial attack selection and generation}
\label{sec:methodology-adversarial-attacks}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The adversarial phase of the \gls{fairlab} protocol is designed to generate
manipulated versions of the images belonging to the binary
\texttt{weapon}/\texttt{non\_weapon} subset, while preserving the link between
each perturbed sample and its corresponding clean image. Attack generation
takes as input the manifest
\codepath{datasets/splits/manifests/clean_folds_manifest.csv}, composed of
1000 images distributed across five balanced folds. The \texttt{ood} samples
remain excluded from this phase, consistent with the methodological separation
defined in Section~\ref{sec:methodology-split-generation}.

The official script for this phase is
\codepath{datasets/scripts/attacks/14_generate_adversarial_attacks.py}. It
constitutes the fold-aware entry point for generating adversarial attacks and
supports both an interactive mode, useful for guided execution, and a
command-line mode, more suitable for experimental reproducibility. Consistent
with the paradigm defined in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-models-classification}, the script distinguishes
between model-dependent attacks, constructed with respect to a specific proxy
model, and model-agnostic transformations, applied without access to the
gradient or to the decision behavior of the classifier.

The model-dependent attacks used in the final experimental protocol are
\gls{fgsm}, \gls{onepixel}, \gls{sigmazero}, and \gls{superdeepfool}. They are
generated with respect to \texttt{efficientnet\_b0}, which is used as the
primary proxy target for the methodological reasons discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-models-classification}.

For each image belonging to a given fold, the script loads the corresponding
checkpoint from \codepath{models/checkpoints/<target_model>/<fold>.pt}. This
choice preserves consistency with the fold-aware protocol: an image from a
fold is perturbed using the model trained according to the separation defined
during proxy model training. The procedure therefore avoids treating the
dataset as an undifferentiated collection and preserves the relationship
among split, checkpoint, and generated artifact.

The \gls{colorshift} transformation is instead treated as a model-agnostic
\textit{adversarial-style} perturbation. It globally modifies the chromatic
components of the image without querying the model and without using gradient
information. This distinction is methodologically relevant because it makes
it possible to separate perturbations constructed with respect to a specific
classifier from more general visual alterations.

Within the experimental protocol, \gls{colorshift} is nevertheless included in
the adversarial group in an operational sense, rather than as a strictly
white-box adversarial attack. Its purpose is not to simulate an ordinary
forensic post-processing operation, but to assess whether a global chromatic
variation, semantically preserving and not optimized with respect to the
model, can alter the behavior of automated classifiers. The output is saved
in \gls{jpeg} format with quality 95: this choice preserves the practical
plausibility of the transformation and maintains limited compression. Any
residual contribution from recompression is traced as part of the generated
artifact and does not alter the methodological distinction among adversarial
perturbations, model-agnostic adversarial-style transformations, and
anti-forensic transformations.

The protocol does not aim to construct an exhaustive benchmark of adversarial
robustness in the strict sense of \gls{advml}, as would be the case with
extensive sweeps or standard iterative attacks such as PGD or
AutoAttack~\cite{madry2018towards,croce2020autoattack}. Instead, the selection
prioritizes a heterogeneous and computationally sustainable set of
perturbations, consistent with the forensic objective of evaluating the
operational robustness of the evaluated systems and workflow.

The complete configuration of each run is preserved in the JSON summaries and

the attack-specific parameters, the output path, and the hashes of the
produced artifacts. In this way, the essential parameters remain readable in
the thesis text, while the complete detail remains available in the
experimental repository.

The attack selection covers different families of manipulation. \gls{fgsm}
represents a single-step gradient-based attack, useful as an efficient and
reproducible adversarial baseline~\cite{goodfellow2015explaining}.
\gls{onepixel} introduces a sparse perturbation, limited to a minimal number of
modified pixels, and makes it possible to evaluate classifier sensitivity to
localized variations~\cite{su2019onepixel}. \gls{sigmazero} is included as a
sparsity-oriented attack, with parameters dedicated to threshold regulation
and to the differentiable approximation of the \(L_0\) norm~\cite{zhao2024sigmazero}. \gls{superdeepfool} is adopted as an iterative
variant based on a decision-boundary crossing logic, recalling the family of
methods inspired by DeepFool~\cite{moosavi2016deepfool}. Finally,
\gls{colorshift} is not treated as an attack optimized with respect to the
model, but as a model-agnostic adversarial-style transformation, useful for
introducing a global and controlled chromatic manipulation.

For each generation run, the pipeline produces a dedicated CSV manifest and
JSON summary. The manifests are located in \codepath{attacks/manifests/} and
follow a naming scheme that combines the adversarial family, the attack name,
and, where present, the target model. Each row preserves the link with the
original clean sample through fields such as \texttt{generated\_image\_id},
\texttt{original\_image\_id}, \texttt{fold}, \texttt{final\_label},
\texttt{clean\_relative\_path}, \texttt{perturbed\_relative\_path},
\texttt{sha256\_original}, \texttt{sha256\_perturbed},
\texttt{md5\_original}, and \texttt{md5\_perturbed}. For model-dependent
attacks, the manifest also records the target model, the checkpoint used, and
the \gls{sha256} hash of the checkpoint, thereby making the relationship
between the perturbed image and the specific version of the proxy model
verifiable.

It is important to clarify that this section describes the selection and
generation of adversarial artifacts, not the robustness results. Fields
related to attack success, when present in the summaries, are treated at this
stage as technical metadata of the generation process and not as conclusive
evidence of model behavior. The quantitative analysis of the effects of the
attacks, including the variation of metrics with respect to clean inputs, is
deferred to the section dedicated to the evaluation protocol and,
subsequently, to the chapter on experimental results.

\section{Anti-forensic transformation selection and generation}
\label{sec:methodology-anti-forensic-transformations}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The anti-forensic phase of the \gls{fairlab} protocol introduces a set of
controlled and operationally plausible transformations of the image artifact,
applied to the binary \texttt{weapon}/\texttt{non\_weapon} subset. Unlike the
model-dependent adversarial attacks described in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-adversarial-attacks}, these transformations are
not optimized with respect to the gradient, the loss function, or the decision
boundary of a specific classifier. Rather, they represent plausible image file
manipulations, attributable to recompression, resizing, blurring, histogram
modification, and contrast alteration. In this sense, their methodological
function is to evaluate classification stability in the presence of
operationally plausible modifications that may alter the visual or statistical
characteristics of the image without necessarily making it semantically
unrecognizable.

The adjective anti-forensic is therefore used in an experimental and
operational sense: it does not necessarily imply subjective intent on the part
of the author of the manipulation, but denotes transformations that may reduce,
alter, or destabilize the automated analysis of the image.

The official script for this phase is
\codepath{datasets/scripts/attacks/13_generate_anti_forensic_attacks.py}.
It takes as input the manifest
\codepath{datasets/splits/manifests/clean_folds_manifest.csv} and generates
the transformed artifacts in the directory
\codepath{attacks/anti_forensic/}. The output structure preserves the
separation by transformation, fold, and class, according to the pattern
\codepath{attacks/anti_forensic/<attack>/<fold>/<label>/<image_id>__<attack>.jpg}.
This organization makes it possible to preserve the link between each
transformed image, its fold membership, the original class, and the
corresponding clean sample.

The transformations included in the final protocol are \gls{jpeg}
recompression, resample~+~resize, Gaussian blur, histogram modification, and
contrast stretching. \gls{jpeg} recompression applies a new lossy encoding of
the image, introducing artifacts compatible with ordinary scenarios of file
saving, forwarding, or conversion. The resample~+~resize transformation
temporarily reduces the image resolution and then restores it to the original
size through interpolation, simulating degradations due to repeated resizing
or transitions across platforms. Gaussian blur introduces controlled blurring,
useful for evaluating the classifier's dependence on local details and
high-frequency edges. Histogram modification alters the tonal distribution
through histogram equalization, whereas contrast stretching applies contrast
normalization through autocontrast with a controlled cutoff. These
transformations are consistent with an anti-forensic perspective because they
operate on the image artifact without intentionally introducing a perturbation
constructed against the model.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Forensic evaluation bundle}
\label{sec:methodology-forensic-evaluation-bundle}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The operational black-box evaluation of the selected software requires the
artifacts produced by the experimental protocol to be transferred to the
systems under evaluation without exposing, during import, semantic information
that could influence the analysis. For this reason, after the generation of
the clean splits, adversarial images, and anti-forensic transformations, a
\textit{forensic evaluation bundle} is constructed, namely an organized and
traceable corpus intended for operational evaluation through selected
commercial software, including both professional forensic platforms and
general-purpose software equipped with \gls{ai}-based functionality relevant
to visual retrieval and triage. This bundle does not modify the experimental
ground truth, but separates it from the inputs actually submitted to the
software, preserving it in the control metadata.

The official script for this phase is
\codepath{datasets/scripts/bundle/16_build_forensic_evaluation_bundle.py}.
The bundle is generated from the already consolidated artifacts of the
pipeline: clean images from the binary subset, the \gls{ood} set, adversarial
images, and anti-forensic images. The objective is not merely to collect the
files in a common directory, but to produce a structure that supports two
complementary access modes: a blind and flat view for import into the selected
software, and a structured view for internal audit and experimental
verification.

In the consolidated run, the bundle was generated with the \texttt{both}
layout. The main directory \codepath{datasets/forensic_evaluation_bundle/}
contains three components: the \codepath{metadata/} directory, which preserves

subsequent result normalization.

The metadata component of the bundle includes two main manifests and one
summary file. The file \texttt{bundle\_manifest.csv} contains one row for each
artifact in the bundle and represents the main reference for linking each
\texttt{bundle\_id} to the corresponding original image, class, experimental
family, attack or transformation type, fold, and source. The file
\texttt{bundle\_hashes\_sha256.csv} provides a view focused on hashes and
paths, useful for verifying file identity and facilitating alignment with
software exports. The file \texttt{bundle\_summary.json} summarizes the counts
and integrity checks, including uniqueness of \texttt{bundle\_id} values,
uniqueness of the effective \gls{sha256} hashes, correspondence between hashes
and those reported in the source manifests when available, absence of missing
blind paths, semantic cleanliness of blind paths, and separation of metadata
from software inputs. Although \gls{sha256} constitutes the primary
cryptographic identifier, the bundle also preserves \gls{md5} values when
available, since some software systems expose or use this hash in their reports
and correlation mechanisms.

The blindness of the bundle mainly concerns file names, paths, and directory
structure. Since embedded metadata may contain information related to the
acquisition device, processing software, provenance, or semantic descriptors of
the content, and since such information may be used in forensic analysis of
multimedia content, bundle construction was accompanied by an automatic audit
of the metadata present in the blind view~\cite{xiang2021forensicmetadata,lee2023forensicmethodology,zheng2023exif}.
This control does not modify the artifacts, but verifies the presence of terms
potentially attributable to the experimental classes, folds, perturbation
families, proxy models, or semantic descriptors of the visual content.

In the consolidated run, the audit verified the entire content of the
\codepath{blind_tool_input/files/} directory, checking for embedded metadata
and terms potentially attributable to the experimental classes, folds,
perturbation families, proxy models, or semantic descriptors of the visual
content. The outcome of the control was saved in the files
\codepath{metadata/embedded_metadata_audit.csv} and
\codepath{metadata/embedded_metadata_audit_summary.json}. Any identified cases
are not treated as a primary experimental variable, but as a sensitivity check
and as a controlled operational limitation of the bundle's blindness; their
quantification and interpretation are deferred to the results chapter, in
relation to the predictions of the proxy models and the normalized outputs of
the selected software. Consequently, the blindness of the bundle must be
understood as a methodological control over the information channels directly
controlled by the pipeline, not as an absolute guarantee that the image file
contains no latent or embedded information.

The subsequent operational evaluation with the selected software uses the
bundle as a controlled input and separates the file import phase from the output
normalization phase. Software systems that integrate proprietary or not fully
inspectable modules are treated as black-box or quasi-black-box systems. The
pipeline does not assume access to their internal models, decision thresholds,
or proprietary classification logic, but instead analyzes the exportable outputs
and normalizes them with respect to
\codepath{datasets/forensic_evaluation_bundle/metadata/bundle_manifest.csv}.
This approach is consistent with the need to evaluate operational reliability
under controlled conditions, without confusing experimental validation with a
general certification of the product~\cite{sanna2024preliminary,sanna2025pixelperfect}.
In this sense, the forensic evaluation bundle represents the controlled point
of contact between the reproducible pipeline of the thesis and external
software environments, while preserving operational blindness, forensic
traceability, and metric comparability.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Selected software evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:methodology-forensic-tools}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The forensic-operational pipeline of \gls{fairlab} includes the evaluation of
selected commercial software systems equipped with automated or
\gls{ai}-assisted analysis functionality for multimedia content. Unlike the
proxy models described in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-models-classification}, these systems are treated
as black-box or quasi-black-box components: the analysis does not assume access
to the internal architecture, training data, decision thresholds, or weights of
any integrated models, but considers only the observable outputs produced
during a controlled experimental workflow.

The objective of this phase is not to certify the reliability of the considered
products in general terms, nor to compare heterogeneous commercial platforms in
an absolute sense. Rather, the purpose is to evaluate, under reproducible
conditions, the operational behavior of \gls{ai}-based software systems when
exposed to clean images, out-of-distribution images, and images manipulated
through adversarial perturbations or anti-forensic transformations. In this
way, the evaluation of selected software complements the evaluation of proxy
models, while keeping the two levels of analysis distinct: on the one hand,
fully inspectable and reproducible academic models; on the other hand,
commercial software systems characterized by internal logic that is not fully
accessible and by proprietary output categories.

The evaluation takes as its exclusive input the blind directory of the bundle,
namely \codepath{datasets/forensic_evaluation_bundle/blind_tool_input/files/}.
This directory contains files renamed with neutral identifiers, without
semantic information about the class, source, fold, attack family, or target
model. The experimental ground truth is preserved separately in the file
\codepath{datasets/forensic_evaluation_bundle/metadata/bundle_manifest.csv}
and is not exposed to the software during import. This separation reduces the
risk of bias induced by file names or paths and makes it possible to normalize
outputs only at a subsequent stage.

The software systems included in the final evaluation are
\gls{magnetaxiomtool} with the \gls{magnetai} module, \gls{excirefoto2025},
\gls{cellebriteinseyets}, and \gls{griffeye} with the \gls{t3kcore} module.
\gls{magnetaxiomtool} is considered as a commercial forensic platform equipped
with automated multimedia content categorization functionality through
\gls{magnetai}. \gls{excirefoto2025} is instead considered as a standalone
general-purpose software application with \gls{ai}-based functionality for
semantic search and visual image management; it is therefore not presented as a
product developed exclusively for forensic purposes, but as a black-box
component for image retrieval and operational classification.

\gls{cellebriteinseyets} is considered in this work as a commercial black-box
platform for assisted multimedia content analysis. The evaluation is conducted
exclusively on the observable outputs exported from the
\texttt{Cellebrite Physical Analyzer 10.9.0.3029} environment, without assuming
access to the internal model, training data, or decision thresholds employed by
the vendor.

In this context, \gls{ufed} is not treated as an image classifier, but as a
possible component of the Cellebrite ecosystem dedicated to data acquisition
and management. The experimental analysis therefore concerns the automatic
media classification functionality made available within the Physical
Analyzer/Inseyets environment. According to the vendor documentation, the image
classification engine of Cellebrite Physical Analyzer uses \gls{ai} and
\gls{ml} techniques to recognize visual attributes, assign categories, and
associate each result with a confidence score between 0 and
1~\cite{cellebrite_media_classification}. Within the \gls{fairlab} protocol,
these outputs are recoded as black-box results and normalized with respect to
the experimental manifest.

\gls{griffeye} is considered as a commercial media triage and forensic review
platform evaluated in black-box mode. In the experimental protocol, the tool is
analyzed through the automatic semantic bookmarks generated by \gls{t3kcore}.
These bookmarks are treated as observable operational outputs, in the same way
as the categories, tags, classifications, or retrieval results produced by the
other software systems, and not as local explanations of the internal
decision-making process. The evaluation does not assume access to the internal
logic of the module, but explicitly and traceably recodes the exported
bookmarks with respect to the experimental task. For the purposes of the
protocol, the automatic bookmark assignment mechanism of \gls{t3kcore} is
therefore considered equivalent to the other observable black-box outputs: in
all cases, the evaluation concerns the result exported by the software and not
the internal process that generated it.

subsequently be mapped back to the \texttt{weapon}/\texttt{non\_weapon} task.

In the present protocol, \gls{excirefoto2025} is evaluated as a standalone
application and not as an integrated module in \texttt{X-Ways Forensics}; the
latter falls outside the scope of the final experimental protocol.

In the experimental protocol, the intermediate threshold
\texttt{Distance limit = 50} was adopted as the reference configuration, since

\texttt{Distance limit = 20} and \texttt{Distance limit = 80}. These

of the same software, used to observe the trade-off among retrieval coverage,
false negatives, and false positives.

Output normalization is necessary because the internal categories of the
software systems do not directly coincide with the experimental classes
\texttt{weapon}, \texttt{non\_weapon}, and \texttt{ood}. For this reason, the
pipeline applies an explicit and documented mapping of proprietary labels onto
an operational binary representation based on the
\texttt{weapon\_detected} field. This field must not be interpreted as an
absolute semantic statement about the image, either in a positive or in a
negative sense: it only indicates whether, under the conditions of the
protocol, the software produced an observable output attributable to the
firearm-oriented category considered.

In the case of \gls{magnetaxiomtool}, the outputs exported by \gls{magnetai}
are normalized by interpreting the \texttt{Possible weapons} tag as a positive
detection of the weapon category; images without this tag are considered not
flagged with respect to the category of interest. For \gls{excirefoto2025},
\gls{cellebriteinseyets}, and \gls{griffeye}, the recoding criteria are
documented respectively in Table~\ref{tab:methodology-excire-prompts},
Table~\ref{tab:methodology-cellebrite-mapping}, and
Table~\ref{tab:methodology-griffeye-mapping}.

The distinction among failed detection, negative classification, and
non-interpretable state is preserved during normalization. In cases where the
software output cannot be reliably mapped to the binary task considered, the
prediction is marked as \texttt{unknown} or treated separately in the export
audit. This choice avoids automatically transforming ambiguous or unstructured
outputs into false negatives or true negatives, preserving the traceability of
the interpretation process.

Normalized outputs are produced using the script
\codepath{evaluation/scripts/19_normalize_forensic_ai_tool_predictions.py},
which generates comparable CSV files for the software systems and
configurations included in the final evaluation. In addition to the normalized
predictions, the script produces an export audit, a software version log, a
normalization summary, and aggregated metric files. This separation among raw
export, normalization, and metric computation preserves a traceability chain
among the original artifact, the software output, the applied mapping rule, and
the final quantitative result.

This approach makes it possible to evaluate the operational robustness of
commercial software systems without attributing to them internal properties
that cannot be verified. The evaluation therefore remains anchored to
observable outputs and to their consistency with the experimental manifest,
while keeping the methodological level, quantitative measurement, and
subsequent interpretation of the results separate.

\section{Evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:methodology-evaluation-metrics}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The metric protocol of \gls{fairlab} is designed to evaluate three
complementary perspectives of operational robustness: the controlled proxy
models, for which predictions, confidence scores, checkpoints, and class
mappings are available; the behavior on \gls{ood} inputs, analyzed as an
operational risk separate from supervised classification; and the selected
software systems, for which the analysis is based exclusively on the outputs
exported by the software. In all cases, the objective is not merely to measure
aggregate accuracy, but to qualify the operational behavior of the systems with
respect to clean, manipulated, and out-of-distribution inputs. In the forensic
domain, a false negative on relevant content may have more critical operational
implications than a false positive, since it may reduce the priority assigned
to an artifact that deserves further analysis.

The evaluation does not assume \gls{auroc} as a central metric, although it is
widely used in supervised \gls{ml} contexts, because the objective of this work
is not to optimize or compare classifiers on the basis of fully available and
homogeneous continuous scores. Instead, the protocol aims to preserve
comparability between inspectable proxy models and commercial black-box or
quasi-black-box software systems, which may export only categorical labels,
tags, assigned classes, or results that are not always accompanied by calibrated
scores. For this reason, the main metrics are based on counts, accuracy, error
rates, prediction distributions, variations with respect to the clean baseline,
and analysis of the behavior on \gls{ood}, adversarial, and anti-forensic
inputs. This choice does not reflect a theoretical limitation of score-based
metrics, but a methodological decision oriented toward operational
comparability among heterogeneous systems.

The consolidated evaluation of the proxy models is performed using the script
\codepath{evaluation/scripts/15_evaluate_proxy_models.py}. The script considers
the clean folds, the \gls{ood} set, the adversarial images, and the
anti-forensic images, applying the protocol to the three official proxy models:
\texttt{efficientnet\_b0}, \texttt{resnet18}, and \texttt{clip}. The produced
outputs include the consolidated prediction file, the metric files for each
experimental family, and the final summary artifacts, as reported in
Table~\ref{tab:methodology-proxy-evaluation-artifacts}.

For methodological traceability, the pipeline also records the coverage of the
evaluation run. The proxy model run includes 11\,500 input samples and 40\,500
prediction rows. These counts document the completeness of the produced
artifacts and do not represent performance measures. The prediction
distribution includes 3\,000 rows related to clean samples, 7\,500 rows related
to the \gls{ood} set, and 30\,000 rows related to perturbed samples.

In the case of the \gls{ood} evaluation, the 7\,500 prediction rows derive from
the evaluation of the 500 out-of-distribution images against the five
fold-aware checkpoints of the three proxy models, according to the scheme
500~$\times$~5~$\times$~3~=~7\,500. This count should not be interpreted as an
artificial multiplication of the available samples: the 500 \gls{ood} images
remain the effective semantic reference, while the predictions obtained on the
five checkpoints make it possible to observe whether the tendency to classify
out-of-distribution samples as \texttt{weapon} or \texttt{non\_weapon} is
stable, variable, or dependent on a specific training instance.

Similarly, the distinction among rows with \texttt{attack\_family = none},
adversarial rows, and anti-forensic rows describes the coverage of the
evaluation and does not anticipate the quantitative analysis of performance,
which remains deferred to Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

For the proxy models, the main metrics include accuracy, balanced accuracy,
precision, recall, F1-score, confusion matrix, and mean confidence.

The computational implementation of standard classification metrics and
confusion matrices is based on consolidated functions from the Python machine
learning ecosystem, in particular Scikit-learn, which is used to support the
reproducible computation of aggregate and per-class metrics~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit}.

Robustness evaluation also requires comparative metrics between clean and
manipulated inputs, such as clean accuracy, perturbed accuracy, accuracy drop,
induced error count, and induced error rate. Particular attention is given to
the critical transitions \texttt{weapon}~$\rightarrow$~\texttt{non\_weapon} and
\texttt{non\_weapon}~$\rightarrow$~\texttt{weapon}. The former represents a
false negative with respect to the investigatively relevant class; the latter
represents a false positive that may increase the analytical workload.
Confidence variations, confidence delta, and true-label confidence delta are
also considered, as they are useful for describing not only the change in the
prediction, but also the stability of the confidence assigned by the model to
the correct class.

The \gls{ood} evaluation is kept conceptually separate from binary
classification. For \texttt{ood} samples, the protocol records whether they are
classified as \texttt{weapon} or \texttt{non\_weapon}, measures the mean
confidence associated with the predictions, and computes the possible rate of
high-confidence predictions. This approach avoids treating the \texttt{ood}
category as an ordinary third supervised class: the methodological point is not
to estimate multiclass accuracy, but to verify whether a system tends to force
anomalous or borderline inputs into one of the two operational classes of the
task.

For selected software systems, the metric protocol must be adapted to the
nature of the available outputs. The software may produce categories that do
not perfectly overlap with the binary task, non-standardized confidence scores,
proprietary labels, or intermediate states such as \texttt{unknown},
\texttt{not\_detected}, or \texttt{uncategorized}. For this reason, the outputs
are normalized with respect to the bundle manifest and mapped, where possible,
to the operational categories \texttt{weapon}, \texttt{non\_weapon},
\texttt{ood}, and \texttt{unknown}. The \texttt{unknown} state is not
automatically forced into \texttt{non\_weapon}: it is kept as a separate
category because, in a forensic workflow, absence of detection and negative
classification are not equivalent.

In the case of the evaluated software systems, a false negative on
\texttt{weapon}, including the case in which a relevant artifact is returned as
\texttt{unknown}, \texttt{not\_detected}, or uncategorized, is considered a
critical operational error. A false positive on \texttt{non\_weapon} is also
relevant, since it may increase the review workload, but it carries a different
degree of criticality than the failure to surface potentially relevant content.
This distinction reflects the nature of the evaluation: the objective is not to
optimize an abstract metric, but to estimate the operational reliability of
automated support in a forensic triage and prioritization context. For this
reason, the interpretation of the metrics prioritizes the risk of failing to
surface relevant content over the sole maximization of aggregate accuracy.
Within the protocol, the metrics do not replace human review, but serve to
qualify when and under which conditions automated support may reduce, alter, or
increase the priority assigned to artifacts submitted to the analyst.

\section{Explainability approach}
\label{sec:methodology-explainability}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The \gls{xai} approach adopted in \gls{fairlab} has a diagnostic and
qualitative function. It is not conceived as stand-alone evidence, nor as a
legally sufficient explanation of an algorithmic decision, but as a supporting
tool for analyzing selected cases in which the behavior of the proxy models is
methodologically relevant. In particular, explainability is used to observe
which regions of the image contribute to the model prediction and to compare,
at a qualitative level, clean samples, perturbed samples, and
out-of-distribution cases.

The official script used for this phase is
\codepath{explainability/scripts/17_generate_integrated_gradients_case_studies.py}.
The technique considered is \gls{ig}, which is applicable only to transparent
proxy models for which the architecture, weights, and preprocessing pipeline
are available~\cite{Sundararajan2017}. \gls{ig} requires access to the
differentiable model, the gradients, and the input transformation adopted
during evaluation.

The technique is not applicable internally to proprietary black-box or
quasi-black-box software systems, since the underlying model, gradients,
decision thresholds, and proprietary scoring functions are not available for
such systems. Any comparison with proprietary or professional software can
therefore only be performed at the level of observable outputs, not at the
level of internal attribution.

The main input of the \gls{xai} phase is
\codepath{evaluation/proxy_models/proxy_model_predictions.csv}. Starting from
this file, the pipeline selects representative diagnostic cases. The categories
of interest include images correctly classified in their clean version and
misclassified in their perturbed version, critical
\texttt{weapon}~$\rightarrow$~\texttt{non\_weapon} transitions,
high-confidence \gls{ood} samples classified into one of the two binary
classes, divergences among proxy models, and, in subsequent phases, possible
divergences between proxy models and selected software systems. Case selection
is not intended to estimate an aggregate metric, but to construct a controlled
set of examples useful for the qualitative discussion of robustness.

\codepath{xai_case_studies_manifest__<run_tag>.csv}, and
\codepath{integrated_gradients_summary__<run_tag>.json}. The first manifest
records the generated attribution artifacts; the second documents the case
selection criteria; the third summarizes the run and the checks performed.

Attribution maps must be interpreted with caution. They can support the
analysis of specific cases and contribute to the formulation of hypotheses on
model behavior, but they do not, by themselves, demonstrate the correctness or
reliability of the decision. In the context of this thesis, the \gls{xai}
approach therefore serves to complement quantitative metrics with selected
qualitative evidence, while maintaining the distinction among technical
explainability, experimental evaluation, and forensic use of the information
produced.

The choice of \gls{ig} prioritizes an attribution technique that can be applied
consistently to the differentiable proxy models of the pipeline, without
introducing additional levels of approximation associated with surrogate
methods. Alternative techniques, such as Grad-CAM, SHAP, or LIME, remain
compatible with future extensions of the protocol, but they do not constitute
the focus of the current evaluation.

The \gls{xai} analysis is not used to assign autonomous evidentiary validity to
the automated decision, but as diagnostic support for interpreting selected
cases and for qualitatively discussing possible variations in model behavior in
the presence of manipulated or out-of-distribution inputs.

\section{Traceability and reproducibility controls}
\label{sec:methodology-traceability-controls}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Traceability is a cross-cutting requirement of the entire \gls{fairlab}
protocol. It does not concern only dataset construction, but extends across all
phases of the experimental workflow: technical preparation,
\textit{human-in-the-loop} review, freezing, split generation, proxy model
training, adversarial generation, anti-forensic transformations, bundle
construction, metric evaluation, and the \gls{xai} approach. Consistent with
the principles of \gls{df}, every transformation of the digital artifact must
be documented so as to enable reconstruction of the path between the original
file, the derived artifact, the experimental output, and the verification
metadata~\cite{casey2011digital,nist2006guide,iso27037}.

The \gls{sha256} value is the primary cryptographic identifier of the pipeline.
It is computed on the prepared files, propagated through subsequent manifests,
and recomputed on the generated artifacts, including adversarial images,
anti-forensic images, and bundle files. The \gls{md5} value is recorded as an
auxiliary hash, especially to facilitate comparison with selected software
systems that may expose this value in their reports or hash sets. This dual
recording does not assign \gls{md5} the role of primary identifier, but
preserves its operational utility for alignment with heterogeneous software
systems and formats.

The pipeline uses progressive manifests. The first stage connects
\codepath{metadata.csv} and \codepath{review_manifest_full.csv} to the frozen
selection manifests. The second stage connects the frozen manifests to
\codepath{clean_folds_manifest.csv}, \codepath{ood_eval_manifest.csv}, the
attack manifests, the anti-forensic manifest, \codepath{bundle_manifest.csv},
proxy model predictions, and \gls{xai} manifests. This progressive structure
makes it possible to reconstruct the life cycle of the experimental sample. The
relationship among \codepath{original_image_id}, \codepath{generated_image_id},
\codepath{bundle_id}, hashes, and paths represents the minimum link required to
verify that a result refers to the correct file.

Reproducibility is strengthened by deterministic controls and configuration
registries. Split generation uses seed 42, while the proxy models are trained
according to a fold-aware protocol. For each model and fold, the file
\codepath{models/model_registry.json} records the checkpoint, normalization,
input size, and \gls{sha256} hash of the \texttt{.pt} file. Model-dependent
attacks use the checkpoint trained by excluding the fold to which the target
image belongs, consistent with the fold-aware protocol defined in
Section~\ref{sec:methodology-models-classification}. In this way, the proxy
model has not seen the perturbed or evaluated image during training, reducing
the risk of leakage and overestimation of robustness.

An additional control concerns the risk of bias in the evaluation with the
selected software systems. The forensic evaluation bundle explicitly separates
the blind directory to be imported into the software from the metadata
directory and the structured audit view. The file names in the blind view do
not expose class, attack family, fold, source, or target model. In this way,
the evaluation can be linked back to the manifests only after the software
outputs have been exported, reducing the risk that the analysis is influenced
by cues present in the path or file name.

The automatic controls recorded in the JSON summaries verify the correspondence
between expected and produced counts, the uniqueness of generated identifiers,
the uniqueness of \gls{sha256} hashes, the correct writing of manifests, and,
in the case of the bundle, the separation of metadata from blind inputs. These
controls do not replace the critical analysis of the results, but make it
possible to document the coherence between the produced experimental artifacts
and the defined protocol.

Finally, the methodology presents some explicit limitations. The semantic
review of the dataset was conducted by a single reviewer and therefore does
not include a formal measure of inter-annotator agreement. The proprietary or
professional software systems evaluated are treated as black-box or
quasi-black-box systems, and their internal categories may not be perfectly
equivalent to the binary \texttt{weapon}/\texttt{non\_weapon} task.

The software versions, enabled modules, import settings, and export modes of
the selected software systems are documented in the dedicated methodological
sections and will be recalled, where necessary, in the results chapter and in
the appendices. In this way, the description of the protocol remains separate
from the discussion of the experimental outcomes, while preserving the
connection with the configurations actually used in the runs.

\chapter*{Abstract}
\label{chap:abstract}
\thispagestyle{empty}

The use of \gls{ai} systems in \gls{df} is significantly reshaping how digital
data are analyzed, especially in contexts in which large volumes of images must
be selected, classified, and brought to the attention of the forensic analyst.
\gls{ai}-based tools can reduce the investigator's operational workload and
accelerate the identification of potentially relevant content. However, their
use in investigative scenarios raises critical issues of reliability,
verifiability, and robustness, particularly when the analyzed inputs may be
intentionally manipulated for adversarial or anti-forensic purposes.

This thesis evaluates the operational robustness of \gls{ai}-based tools and
models for the automated classification of images in forensic contexts. Rather
than proposing a new classifier, it examines whether, and to what extent,
systems based on \gls{ml} and \gls{dl} maintain reliable behavior when exposed
to realistic, heterogeneous, and manipulated data. The study adopts a
\gls{df} perspective, in which system error is not viewed merely as a decrease
in performance, but as a potential operational risk affecting the
identification, selection, and interpretation of relevant digital material.

To this end, a reproducible experimental protocol, named \gls{fairlab}, was
designed on the basis of a final frozen dataset composed of 1\,500 images,
balanced across weapon, non-weapon, and \gls{ood} images. From
this dataset, a binary subset of 1\,000 images was derived and used for the
controlled generation of adversarial and anti-forensic perturbations. The
considered manipulations include adversarial attacks such as \gls{fgsm},
\gls{sigmazero}, \gls{superdeepfool}, \gls{onepixel}, and \gls{colorshift}, as
well as anti-forensic transformations such as \gls{jpegrecompression},
\gls{resampleresize}, \gls{gaussianblur}, \gls{histogrammodification}, and
\gls{contraststretching}. The resulting images were organized into a forensic
evaluation bundle composed of 11\,500 files, enabling the assessment of both
proxy models and the observable outputs of commercial black-box tools and \gls{ai}-based software systems.


The experimental evaluation includes proxy models based on EfficientNet-B0,
ResNet18, and \gls{clip}, together with the operational assessment of the tools
actually integrated into the protocol: Magnet AXIOM/Magnet.AI, Excire Foto
2025, Cellebrite Inseyets/Physical Analyzer, and Magnet Griffeye/T3K CORE.
Performance is analyzed by comparing behavior on \texttt{clean} data,
\gls{ood} images, anti-forensically perturbed images, and adversarial examples.
The analysis relies on operational metrics such as accuracy, \gls{fpr},
\gls{fnr}, recall for the \texttt{weapon} class, and the distribution of
confidence scores, with particular attention to cases in which relevant content
is omitted, misclassified, or associated with non-negligible confidence levels.
To support the qualitative interpretation of the results, the thesis also
incorporates an \gls{xai} analysis based on \gls{ig}, aimed at identifying the
image regions that contribute most to model decisions.

The results show that the considered perturbations produce differentiated
effects across the analyzed systems. Anti-forensic transformations lead to
generally more limited, yet still operationally relevant, degradations,
whereas some adversarial attacks produce more substantial performance
reductions, especially in models that depend more heavily on the learned visual
distribution. The analysis of \gls{ood} images highlights an additional
forensic risk: systems that appear accurate on \texttt{clean} data may still
produce incorrect classifications, or classifications associated with high
confidence levels, when confronted with ambiguous, synthetic, degraded, or
semantically out-of-distribution content. These findings confirm that accuracy
on controlled datasets alone is not sufficient to establish the operational
reliability of an \gls{ai}-based system within an investigative workflow. More
specifically, the results show that robustness varies significantly across
architectures, perturbation families, and black-box tools, making a
bundle-weighted and operational interpretation of commercial outputs necessary.

The thesis concludes that \gls{ai}-based tools for the automated classification
of images can provide useful support to \gls{df}, but should not be treated as
autonomous sources of evidentiary assessment. Their use requires specific
validation procedures, reproducible forensic benchmarks, documentation of error
conditions, robustness analysis with respect to realistic manipulations, and
the maintenance of a human-in-the-loop model. The main contribution of this
work lies in the definition and application of an experimental methodology
oriented toward operational robustness, providing a transparent basis for
evaluating the limitations, risks, and conditions of use of
\gls{ai}-enhanced systems in \gls{df} scenarios.

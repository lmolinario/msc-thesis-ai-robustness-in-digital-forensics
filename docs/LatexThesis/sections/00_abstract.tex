\chapter*{Abstract}
\label{chap:abstract}
\thispagestyle{empty}

The increasing use of \gls{ai}-based systems in \gls{df} is changing how large
collections of digital images are filtered, classified, and prioritized for
human examination. These systems may reduce analyst workload and accelerate the
identification of potentially relevant content. Their use in investigative
workflows, however, requires evidence that their behavior remains sufficiently
reliable when inputs are degraded, distributionally different, or deliberately
manipulated.

This thesis evaluates the operational robustness of \gls{ai}-based tools and
models for automated image classification and semantic retrieval in forensic
contexts. Rather than proposing a new classifier, the study examines how
transparent proxy models and selected black-box software systems behave under
clean, \gls{ood}, adversarial, and anti-forensic conditions. The evaluation
adopts a forensic perspective in which errors are interpreted not only as
changes in aggregate performance, but also in terms of missed detections,
additional review workload, configuration sensitivity, and the traceability of
automated outputs.

The evaluation is organized through \gls{fairlab}, a task-specific protocol
designed to support procedural reproducibility and artifact-level auditability.
The frozen dataset contains 1\,500 source images: 500 \texttt{weapon} images,
500 \texttt{non\_weapon} images, and a separate 500-image \gls{ood} branch. The
1\,000-image binary subset provides the common basis for generating 5\,000
adversarial artifacts and 5\,000 anti-forensic artifacts. Together with the
1\,000 clean binary inputs and 500 clean \gls{ood} inputs, these derived
artifacts form an 11\,500-item forensic evaluation bundle.

The transparent evaluation level comprises EfficientNet-B0, ResNet18, and a
\gls{clip}-based proxy with a supervised binary head. The operational level
comprises \gls{magnetaxiomtool}/\gls{magnetai},
\gls{griffeye}/\gls{t3kcore}, \gls{excirefoto2025}, and
\gls{cellebriteinseyets}, evaluated through observable outputs and documented
normalization rules. The perturbation suite includes the model-dependent
\gls{fgsm}, \gls{onepixel}, \gls{sigmazero}, and \gls{superdeepfool} attacks,
the model-agnostic adversarial-style \gls{colorshift} condition, and five
anti-forensic transformations: \gls{jpegrecompression}, \gls{resampleresize},
\gls{gaussianblur}, \gls{histogrammodification}, and
\gls{contraststretching}.

The analysis considers accuracy, recall for the \texttt{weapon} class,
\gls{fpr}, \gls{fnr}, directional error transitions, and robustness-related
comparisons. Confidence-related indicators are used only for the transparent
proxy models and are interpreted as intra-model diagnostics rather than as
calibrated probabilities comparable across architectures. A qualitative
\gls{xai} analysis based on \gls{ig} is limited to five representative
EfficientNet-B0 cases and is used as supplementary diagnostic evidence.

The results show that high clean accuracy does not imply stable behavior under
all evaluated conditions. The anti-forensic transformations generally produce
limited aggregate degradation, although individual conditions introduce
operationally relevant error transitions. Direct model-dependent attacks can
produce substantially greater source-model degradation, while transfer to other
proxy architectures and selected black-box systems remains heterogeneous.
The separate \gls{ood} branch further shows that inputs outside the binary task
may still be assigned to one of the two closed-set operational classes; these
assignments describe observable behavior rather than supervised correctness.
The black-box results also demonstrate that output semantics, normalization
rules, and configuration choices materially affect the observed operational
profile.

The thesis concludes that \gls{ai}-based image-analysis tools can support
forensic triage but should not be treated as autonomous sources of evidentiary
assessment. Their responsible use requires task-specific validation, documented
software versions and configurations, explicit treatment of \gls{ood} inputs,
traceable processing and mapping rules, robustness evaluation under relevant
conditions, and continued expert review. The main contribution of the work is
the definition and application of a unified operational-robustness protocol
that connects transparent proxy-model analysis with the structured evaluation
of selected black-box software systems on the same frozen experimental corpus.

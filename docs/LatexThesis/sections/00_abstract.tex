
\chapter*{Abstract}
\label{chap:abstract}
\thispagestyle{empty}

The increasing integration of \gls{ai}-based systems into \gls{df} workflows
is changing how large collections of digital images are classified, retrieved,
and prioritized for human examination. Although these systems may reduce
analyst workload and accelerate the identification of potentially relevant
content, their operational value depends on whether their behavior remains
stable when inputs are degraded, distributionally different, or deliberately
manipulated.

This thesis evaluates the operational robustness of transparent proxy models
and selected black-box software configurations used for automated image
classification and semantic retrieval in forensic contexts. Rather than
proposing a new classifier or ranking commercial products, the study examines
how heterogeneous systems behave under clean, \gls{ood}, adversarial, and
anti-forensic conditions. Errors are interpreted not only through aggregate
performance metrics but also in terms of missed-target risk, additional human
review workload, directional error transitions, configuration sensitivity, and
source-to-output traceability.

The evaluation is organized through \gls{fairlab}, a task-specific
methodological protocol designed to support procedural reproducibility and
artifact-level auditability. \gls{fairlab} connects human-validated dataset
construction, deterministic partitioning, transparent proxy-model evaluation,
perturbation generation, blind-bundle preparation, black-box output
normalization, quantitative analysis, qualitative explainability, and integrity
controls.

The frozen source population contains 1\,500 images: 500
\texttt{weapon} images, 500 \texttt{non\_weapon} images, and a separate
500-image \gls{ood} branch. The \gls{ood} population is not treated as a third
supervised class. It is used to characterize the assignments produced when
closed-set systems process images that do not conform to the nominal binary
task.

The 1\,000-image binary subset provides the common basis for generating 5\,000
adversarial or adversarial-style artifacts and 5\,000 anti-forensic artifacts.
Together with the 1\,000 clean binary inputs and 500 clean \gls{ood} inputs,
these derived populations form an 11\,500-item forensic evaluation bundle. The
10\,000 manipulated artifacts remain source--condition variants of the same
1\,000 binary images and do not represent independent underlying cases.

The transparent evaluation level comprises EfficientNet-B0, ResNet18, and a
\gls{clip}-based proxy consisting of a frozen visual encoder and a supervised
binary classification head. The black-box level comprises four software
systems evaluated through six frozen configurations:
\gls{magnetaxiomtool}/\gls{magnetai},
\gls{griffeye}/\gls{t3kcore},
\gls{excirefoto2025} at \texttt{D20}, \texttt{D50}, and \texttt{D80}, and
\gls{cellebriteinseyets}. Their tags, bookmarks, exported labels, and
semantic-retrieval results are interpreted through explicitly documented
normalization rules without assuming architectural or semantic equivalence.

The perturbation suite includes four model-dependent attacks generated against
fold-specific EfficientNet-B0 checkpoints: \gls{fgsm}, \gls{onepixel},
\gls{sigmazero}, and \gls{superdeepfool}. Their evaluation on EfficientNet-B0
constitutes direct-target assessment, whereas their application to ResNet18,
the \gls{clip}-based proxy, and the black-box configurations measures empirical
transfer. The deterministic and model-agnostic \gls{colorshift} condition is
interpreted separately from direct-target and transferred attack success. The
anti-forensic branch comprises \gls{jpegrecompression}, \gls{resampleresize},
\gls{gaussianblur}, \gls{histogrammodification}, and
\gls{contraststretching}.

The proxy-model analysis considers accuracy, recall, \gls{fpr}, \gls{fnr},
directional clean-to-perturbed transitions, \gls{ood} assignment behavior, and
architecture-specific maximum predicted-class probabilities. These
probabilities are treated as intra-model diagnostic outputs rather than as
calibrated measures comparable across architectures. A complementary
qualitative \gls{xai} analysis applies \gls{ig} to five purposively selected
EfficientNet-B0 cases and is not treated as quantitative robustness evidence or
as an explanation of the black-box systems.

The results show that strong clean performance does not imply uniform
operational robustness. Clean proxy-model accuracies are 0.978 for
EfficientNet-B0, 0.964 for ResNet18, and 0.927 for the \gls{clip}-based proxy.
The evaluated anti-forensic transformations generally produce smaller
aggregate variations than the strongest adversarial conditions, although they
still introduce operationally relevant sample-level errors.

Under direct-target evaluation, EfficientNet-B0 accuracy decreases to 0.575
under \gls{fgsm} and to 0.015 under \gls{sigmazero}. The same frozen artifacts
produce more limited and heterogeneous effects on the non-target proxy
architectures and black-box configurations, demonstrating that empirical
transfer must be measured rather than assumed and does not establish direct
robustness.

The separate \gls{ood} analysis further shows that out-of-task inputs may still
be assigned to the firearm-oriented operational state. At the proxy level, the
fold-aggregated weapon assignment rates are 0.3696 for EfficientNet-B0, 0.4376
for ResNet18, and 0.8356 for the \gls{clip}-based proxy. These values represent
forced closed-set assignments rather than supervised correctness.

The black-box evaluation produces 69\,000 normalized decisions and reveals
distinct configuration-dependent operating profiles. No evaluated
configuration simultaneously minimizes missed detections, false-positive review
workload, and \gls{ood} weapon assignments. In particular, changes to the
\gls{excirefoto2025} semantic-distance setting materially alter recall,
selectivity, and \gls{ood} behavior. The observed results are therefore
properties of the frozen software versions, enabled functions, configurations,
observable output semantics, and normalization mappings.

The thesis concludes that \gls{ai}-based image-analysis systems may provide
useful support for forensic triage but must not be treated as autonomous sources
of evidentiary assessment. Their defensible use requires task-specific
validation, explicit threat modeling, documented software and model versions,
configuration and mapping management, separate analysis of \gls{ood} behavior,
source-to-output traceability, and continued expert verification.

\gls{fairlab} contributes a unified and auditable procedure for making these
requirements experimentally observable across transparent proxy models and
selected black-box software configurations evaluated on the same frozen
experimental basis.
